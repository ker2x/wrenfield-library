## Abstract (as filed)

This work adapts the nine certified readings to persons not yet dead, stating for each whether it may be given to the subject, to the household, or to no one. It prints the refusals and the replies, and the case of a cooper of the Tenterfields who asked and was told. Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is not bound with this volume, as with no volume in this Library.

## 1. What This Discipline Is, and What It Is Not
This is forecasting for the living: the reading of conditions at the end of a life not yet ended. The instrument is the same instrument, under the same warrant, read by the same hand. It does not know whom it is reading; it has no setting for it. What differs is the address. Where the subject is dead, the reading is spoken to a household and the subject is past hearing; where the subject is living, the subject is in the room and the reading is spoken to the face.

People ask for three reasons and I have heard no fourth. Some ask to put their affairs in order: a will, a debt, a roof, a son sent to a trade. Some ask because uncertainty has grown heavier than the fact would be. A few ask to prove that the office cannot do it. It is not a death-date, the instrument returning conditions and not hours; a diviner who supplies a season or a number is guessing, and will be fined. It is not a diagnosis, the physician reporting the body and the diviner the weather of the end. It is not a licence to tell the subject, which is why the Faculty refused this work three times: the reading exists for the subject, and the telling is a separate act.

**Table of terms, for readers outside the Faculty.**

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| Reading | One return of the instrument, held and entered |
| Instrument | The Faculty's forecast instrument, unnamed |
| Warrant | *Warrant to Forecast in Cases of Bereavement*, renewed every third year |
| Address | The person to whom a reading may be spoken, and to whom alone |
| Interval | The period between a forecast given and the weather reported |
| Certified line | The sentence the Faculty permits at each of the nine readings |
| Abstentions | The four things a diviner may never say; read aloud before reading |
| No Reading Obtainable | A reading, not a failure; the instrument returns nothing |

## 2. That the Instrument Reads the Same Person

The Faculty turns on a distinction it does not accept: that the instrument reports conditions without regard to the state of the person standing in them. This is how the tool is tested. At renewal, before the Prefect of Divination, a reading is taken for a living person and one for a dead person of the same name, in the same parish, in the same hour; in two hundred and eleven recorded tests the returns agreed in one hundred and ninety-four. What follows is office, not power: the recently deceased cannot be told, the bereaved can, and a visit to a living subject is entered as *informational*, at no fee.

## 3. The Nine Readings Adapted to the Living
The nine readings are the Faculty's; the adaptation is mine and consists of one column. Where the subject is dead, a reading may be given to any household that asks; where the subject is living, it may be given to the subject, to the household, or to no one, the fee being for the reading and not for the company.

**Table I — The nine readings adapted to a living subject, with lawful address, the withheld address, what the reading may not promise, and the fee.**

| Reading | Lawfully given to a living subject | Withheld from | What it may not promise | Fee |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fair Interval | The subject, on request | No one; nothing is stated but the weather | That it holds, or that it is good | 1 mark 4 bells |
| Long Fair | The subject, with his household present | A subject who asked to be told alone | Duration in any form; *continuing* is struck | 1 mark 4 bells |
| Overcast That Does Not Fall | The subject, on request | The employer, without written leave | That nothing will fall later, or that the sky is clearing | 1 mark 4 bells |
| Gentle Rain | The household, in writing, after the death | The subject, always | That the subject will be told, or that the rain is warm | 1 mark 8 bells |
| Rain, Lifting by Morning | The subject, and any household he names | No one; a stated end may be given | That the end is soon, or the morning his | 1 mark 8 bells |
| Low Cloud on the Hill | The subject, at discretion, never in company | The household, which hears absence as a diagnosis | That what is not visible is absent | 1 mark 8 bells |
| Wind Without Rain | The subject, on a second request | The household, which will call it unrest | That it will settle, or that it is a warning | 2 marks |
| Hard Frost, Bright | No one. The reading is entered and kept | The subject, at any age | That it will deepen; that the cold is his to mend | 1 mark 12 bells |
| No Reading Obtainable | The subject, by silence, in the diviner's presence | The household, unless he sends him | Anything at all | 1 mark 8 bells |

Struck from the permitted wording: *duration*, *certainty*, *soon*, *not yet*, *at least*, and every clause of comfort. A reading given with any of them is not certified and the fee is returnable.

## 4. The Three Refusals, and the Replies

The letters are bound in the order written, which is not the order of their pagination, the binder having bound them as the author instructed and the author being careless.

**Table II — The three refusals, with the ground taken, the author's answer, and the fee returned.**

| Letter | Year and month | Ground of refusal | My answer | Fee returned |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First | Eleventhmonth 1489, three pages | A forecast given to a living person interferes with that life before its completion | The office reports and does not alter; no instrument has been held causally implicated in the weather it reports | 1 mark 4 bells |
| Second | Thirdmonth 1490, eleven pages | The work conflates the diviner's office with the priest's, addressing the living as though the case were closed | The offices remain distinct: the diviner reports what may be known, the priest binds what may be done | 1 mark 8 bells |
| Third | Eighthmonth 1490, a single leaf | The author had a reading taken for himself, and the Faculty declines to certify work done under a breach of the boundary set out | That reading was performed by another diviner, under warrant 1,441, at my request, with Dr. Cottersley present | 1 mark 8 bells |

The fees were returned because the Faculty declined the work, not because the readings were refused; each went to the Diocesan poor-box at St. Werburgh's. In Seventhmonth 1491 the Clerk prepared a fourth letter, recorded in the letter-book and never sent, the endorsement being *not now*; I have not been permitted to see the leaf.

## 5. The Third Refusal, in Full, with My Reply
Provenance: Faculty of Divination & Improbable Prediction, letter-book 1490, folio 88, docket *Rook, E. — forecast, living subjects*.

> *Master Rook. The Faculty has read your paper a second time, and now reads it with the reading taken at your own request by another hand, and declines to certify the work. The Faculty's ground is not the reading you took; it is the reading you had taken. You set a boundary, and stood on both sides of it within the same twelvemonth, and now present the Faculty with a work whose whole argument is that the boundary is lawful. It observes only that a discipline is taught by its example, that your example is a man who would not be refused, and that no Faculty can certify a rule and a dispensation in one volume. It asks you, in the plain terms you affect, whether the abstentions bind the diviner who wrote them. If they do, your third reading was unlawful, and this work is the work of a man who broke his own rule. If they do not, the Faculty will not certify this practice or any other of yours. — For the Faculty, H. Cottersley, clerk, the 9th of Eighthmonth, 1490.*

My reply, entered beneath it:

> *Master Clerk. The abstentions bind the diviner; they do not bind me as a subject. I did not read for myself. I asked for a reading, as any man may, and received the certified line from a warrant I did not hold. If the Faculty means that a man may learn the weather of his own end only by an ignorance the Faculty has chosen for him, then the Faculty has an opinion about my death and no rule about divination. I asked because I am fifty-one years old and a diviner, and did not wish to be the one man in this Faculty who had never heard a line spoken to his face. The reading was Low Cloud on the Hill. — E. Rook, 19.viii.1490.*

A refusal to certify is not a refusal to read; the distinction is the Faculty's, and it has served the Faculty for a century.

## 6. The Cooper of the Tenterfields, Who Asked and Was Told
In the Secondmonth of 1491 a cooper of the Tenterfields, Halsey Warr, sixty-three years of age, of Hoop Lane, master of the trade with his own staves and one apprentice, came to a house on Cooper's Row where I was reading for a widow and waited at the gate.

He put the request in his own words, which the Faculty required me to enter verbatim: *I do not want to know when. Every man in this quarter has wanted to know when and has been told no, and I am no different from my quarter. I want to know what it is like, so that I may be found doing my work and not sitting in a chair with my hands folded. If it is hard weather I will lay in; if it is fair I will finish the Tenterfield vats.*

On the eleventh of Secondmonth 1491, in his own workshop, in the presence of Dr. H. Cottersley of the Faculty, who came at his own cost of one bell, I read the abstentions aloud, entered the reading in ink before speaking it, and gave the certified line. The instrument returned **Fair Interval**. I spoke the line unamended and added nothing. He said it aloud himself twice, called it the best news he had heard in twenty years, and asked whether I would tell his daughter, which I declined, the address being to the subject only. He then asked whether the line could be written down for him to keep. It cannot: the register is the Faculty's, and a written line is a document a household may read after his death and mistake for an amnesty.

The eleven years I have from the parish register of St. Cuthbert, from his day-book, and from his daughter, Avis Warr, who has corrected me twice. He repaired the Tenterfield vats, which belonged to the brewers and to which he had no title. He made forty-one barrels for his daughter's marriage in 1494, and in the spring of 1498 had a coffin made to his own measure and afterwards told the joiner to cut it down for a water butt, a man who buys a coffin having begun to arrange the weather. Each Secondmonth he asked the same question: *Fair still?* Each year I told him that a forecast is given once and may not be repeated. He took it well every year, and did not take it well in 1500, when he said a man who has been given the weather ought to be allowed to look at the sky.

He died on the fourth of Tenthmonth 1501, in his seventy-fourth year, eleven years after the reading, in his own bed, with his daughter in the room, of a congestion of the chest taken in the wet of the harvest. The weather of it was not fair. Avis Warr says the wind came up on the afternoon of the day he took to his bed and stayed until he was buried, and that she minded. Eleven years of interval are not the same thing as a promise, and I have written the case at length because it is the only one in the register in which the subject was told, the interval ran to double figures, and the reading was wrong at the end.

The Faculty's finding, entered at Michaelmas term 1502, letter-book folio 104, is this: *The reading was lawful; the address was to the subject alone; the witness was of the Faculty; the abstentions were read; the fee was refused. The practice is not forbidden by the finding. The finding is also that the diviner shall not attend the same subject in the same month in eleven successive years, there being no reading in it, and that the annual attendance, though lawful, was a pastoral act performed by a man who holds no cure.* I have ceased. I have not stopped thinking about the year 1500.

## 7. That a Forecast Is a Regret Performed in Advance

A forecast given to the living is a regret performed in advance. The instrument reports, the subject hears, and the interval between the hearing and the occurrence is filled with regret, which the bereaved perform afterwards, with the fact in hand; mine is spent earlier, where the fact is not. A man told the weather of his death can no longer hear of anyone's death as an ordinary thing: a funeral becomes news of a neighbour, and the neighbour becomes a version of himself. The Faculty enters this as an injury; I hold that it is grief, performed early and without a grave to perform it at. When the cooper asked in the ninth year whether eleven years had been given him, I could not answer and did not, and his daughter says my silence that morning was the best thing I did in the eleven.

## 8. The Faculty's Objection, and My Reply
The Faculty's objection is that this volume is a licence to frighten the living. It was put to me at the hearing of Thirdmonth 1490 in a form I asked leave to take down.

> *Master Rook. You ask for a licence to tell a living man the weather of his death, and the Faculty will not grant it. Such a licence is a licence to frighten the living. A forecast is not heard as a report; it is heard as a sentence. The man who hears it will order his affairs upon it, and if the reading is Fair he will order them wrongly, and if it is Hard Frost he will have been frightened for eleven years without cause, and for those years no refund can be paid. The instrument is poor light and an hour; it is wrong often, and here it is wrong into a man's own ear, where the Faculty cannot reach it to correct it. The Faculty does not say you intend a harm. It says you intend a practice, and this is what the practice does.*

My reply is four points. First, the fear is real; the procedure at §9 confines it to one hour and one sentence. Second, the alternative is not safety but ignorance, which men wish to leave. Third, the case the Faculty has in mind is not a certified reading: the chandler of Bell Court in 1479 heard a line called *Changeable*, which is not among the nine, from a man at a fair who took two bells for the reading and four for a jar of sirup. Fourth, a diviner who reads for a dead man can be corrected, and one who reads for a living man cannot, so that the objection is not to harm but to answerability.

## 9. The Procedure for a Forecast to the Living
The following procedure is lawful, is not required, and should not be attempted by a reader of this book. Each step is entered in the register before it is taken.

1. **Confirm the subject is living.** Twice in the register a household has asked for a subject three days dead, and a diviner who reads under the living form for the dead takes a fee he cannot keep.
2. **Take the request in the subject's own voice,** aloud, entered verbatim. A household may not request a reading for a living subject, and a physician may not request one, having an interest.
3. **Ask who is present, and shut the door.** **The rule of presence is this: the reading is given to the subject alone; the witness is present for the register and for the diviner and not for the subject, and does not speak.** The room holds the subject, the diviner, and one witness of the Faculty or the Diocese, no one else.
4. **Argue against the reading** for one quarter of an hour, setting out the fear, the objection, the fee, and the fact that the reading will not be repeated. This step has lost me four subjects in nineteen years.
5. **Recite the abstentions aloud:** no duration; no certainty; no licence to tell another; no fee for comfort.
6. **Take the reading** in good light, at the hour of the request, and enter it in ink before speaking it: a reading spoken and not entered is a story.
7. **Speak the certified line** from Table I, unamended, once.
8. **Say nothing for the length of one bell.** Sit down. This is the whole of the practice, and the reason the fee is one mark and four bells and not six clipped: the bell is the interval, in the room, in company.
9. **Take the fee named in the table, in full, and refuse any addition.** A refused addition is the beginning of a promise.
10. **Give the reading once and only once.** No second reading, no annual attendance, and no comparison with a previous reading.
11. **Withhold the household.** If the subject asks that his household be told, decline, and enter the asking. Write nothing for the subject to keep.
12. **Send the register to the Faculty within the month**, on pain of four bells.

If the reading is No Reading Obtainable, the diviner stays for the bell, takes the fee, and says nothing, and this is the one case in which the reading and the silence are the same act.

## 10. Whether the Telling Was Lawful

The reader will want the Faculty's answer to the only question this book is about, and it is at §6. Two things in it deserve a note. The finding decides the case on the address and not on the reading: what made the telling lawful was not that the reading was Fair Interval, not that the man lived eleven years, but that the line was spoken to the subject and to no one else, in the presence of the Faculty, in the words of the table, after the abstentions. A forecast is lawful or not before it is true or false. The single prohibition is against the annual attendance and not against the reading, the Faculty's phrase for it being *a pastoral act performed by a man who holds no cure*, which is why I have printed the finding rather than answered it. The Faculty did not cite the case, and I have put the cooper's name in this volume so that he is in a list of some kind.

## 11. The Telling That Went Wrong, and the Fee Returned
The one failure of this practice in the register is not mine and is worse for being a stranger's. In the Firstmonth of 1493 a visiting diviner of the Bramblegate school, Master Osric Deale, warrant 1,517, was asked by a dyer's widow of Low Town, Annet Skeat, fifty-eight years of age, for a reading of the living kind. The reading returned **Rain, Lifting by Morning**, which is lawful for a living subject and may state an end. He then, in the same breath, told her she had years enough to see her sons settled, that the rain was *at least* lifting before the spring, and that she was not to be afraid, since the household would not be disturbed. Three of those clauses are struck words at Table I, and the third is not a forecast but an indemnity; the case is entered in the Faculty's book of errors at folio 91 under the heading **Surplus of Comfort**.

She told her sons, as any subject will. The elder gave up a place at the rope-walk and the younger sold a share in the dyer's shop to be near her, and the family left Low Town in 1495 for a smaller house on the Aldermarket side. She died in Firstmonth 1497, and the weather of the end was Hard Frost, Bright, a reading no one may be given. The fee was 2 marks 4 bells, the table fee and one mark added by the widow herself for the comfort, and both were returned on the complaint of the elder son out of the Faculty's contingency, Master Deale having spent it.

> 1493. Item. At the case of the widow Skeat of Low Town, a reading given by a visiting warrant with three struck clauses and one indemnity, the subject acting upon them and removing her household. Fee, 2 marks 4 bells, refunded in full from the contingency. Finding: the surplus of comfort is the whole of the error; the reading was lawful and the telling was not. Censure: the diviner suspended two half-year terms, and required to read the abstentions aloud at the quarter's meeting. Remedy: no new form.

Master Deale's mistake was not a wrong reading. It was a kindness, delivered in the third person, to a woman who had asked to be told once.

## Appendix. From the Parish Register of St. Cuthbert in the Tenterfields, 1501

> The fourth day of the tenth month. Halsey Warr, cooper, of Hoop Lane in this parish, aged seventy-four years, master of his trade, communicant, died in his own house about the ninth hour of the evening, of a congestion of the chest. Wind and hard rain from the afternoon of the first day, continuing without abatement through the burial, so that the bearers went in the water to the knee at the gate of the churchyard.
