## Abstract (as filed)

Edmund Tarry, of Bramblegate, borrowed a volume from the Bramblegate Lending Library in 1496 and did not return it; 9 bells accrued. On production of a certificate of his decease the Library waived the fine, as its own borrowing table provides. The author, who died in 1497, disputes the waiver: he did not die in Alderwick, and the certificate is of the wrong jurisdiction. The estate asks only that the sum be computed. Form 7B, the Declaration of Non-Interference with Time, is required for every filing of this class and is not present; the Registry's reply to the estate's request for it is another form.

## 1. The loan

On the 3rd of March 1496, a cooper of Bramblegate named Edmund Tarry took out of the Bramblegate Lending Library the *Principles of Minor Maleficence* of Quillan Ashgrove-Vane, third edition, the good one, the fourth edition of 1441 being held there under a note reading only *not again*. The loan was entered in his own name at the desk, in the hand of Miss Verity Coote, Librarian of Bramblegate. The volume was shelved under **P**, by the first letter of the title, between *Principles of Book-Keeping* and *Principles of Drainage*. He was then sixty-one, a ratepayer of thirty-one years' standing.

He borrowed it because he had a suit with the Drapers' Company of Bramblegate touching the sinking of a well, and had been told that the *Principles* holds the only sustained treatment of injury done by an agent who intended none. He carried the book home and kept it on the shelf above the salt, the driest shelf in the house.

The term was ninety days, extensible once at the desk. The volume left the Library on a Tuesday; the author's draft says Wednesday, and he is wrong, the desk journal for the day being in his own borrowing hand and dated.

## 2. The condition of the volume at loan

The Bramblegate Lending Library grades condition at loan on a table of five marks, **a** (as issued) to **e** (withdrawn from loan). The *Principles* entered at **c**, signed at the desk by Miss Coote, then in her second year there. Nobody disputed the grading then; the estate disputes it now, and the estate would benefit had the volume entered at **e** and the loan been refused. The author, who is dead, sets out what **c** meant that day:

| Mark | Meaning on the Bramblegate table of 1491 | Effect on loan |
|---|---|---|
| **a** | as issued; sewing sound; boards tight | lent, term 90 days |
| **b** | rubbed at head and tail; text unaffected | lent, term 90 days |
| **c** | stained at one leaf, or one plate with a closed tear, or boards rubbed through at the corner | lent, term 90 days; note made at the desk |
| **d** | sewing sprung at one gathering; leaves loose but present | lent at the Librarian's discretion; term 30 days |
| **e** | leaves detached, or a quire wanting, or signs of damp at the spine | not lent; retained at the desk and reported to the Committee |

The volume was marked **c** for a stain at page 41, of oil and not of water; and for the head of the spine rubbed to the board. The stain falls on the sentence the author quotes more than once below.

## 3. The illness, the renewal, and the death

The author's last months are documented here because the estate filed his pocket day-book with the draft, and the day-book anticipates the rebuttal by four years.

On the 9th of August 1497 he was taken with a flux and a low fever and ceased to attend the yard. On the 3rd of September 1497, the term having run on the 1st of June, he sent his apprentice, one Walter Snell, to the desk with the volume under his arm and a note asking for the second term. It is material that the volume was at the desk that day, and that Miss Coote renewed the loan in her own hand, writing *seen and renewed, ninety days, V.C.*, and that the book went home again. The day-book has three words on it: *renewed. so not lost.*

He worsened in October. On the 2nd of November 1497 he died at Bramblegate, in the house above the cooperage, at about the sixth hour, of the flux, with the parish clerk in the room and two neighbours below. He was sixty-two, and was buried on the 4th in ground his wife's family had held since 1461. The book was not in the room; it lay above the salt until the 14th, when his executor took it down and did not know what it was.

The renewal of the 3rd of September deserves its note. The desk journal for that day has, against the entry, the words *carried by hand, wet*, and a second entry in the same hand: *fine not to run during sickness, per table 1491, note 4*. Note 4 is in the margin of the table: *where the borrower is kept from the desk by sickness, the term is not to be counted against him, nor the fine raised, until the borrower is about again or dead.* The estate reads this as a renewal in perpetuity; Miss Coote as a grace for the sick, ending with the man. Neither reading is in the note, which is four lines long.

The executor, Mr. Thomas Tarry, a nephew of the parish, settled the estate the following spring, finding among the papers the draft and the day-book, and among the furniture one volume belonging to the Library across the river. He did the correct thing, which was to report it, and the incorrect thing, which was to keep it pending the report. It is on the shelf above the author's papers in the Registry to this day.

## 4. The procedure on the death of a borrower

The procedure is Bramblegate's, not ours, and is set out here because the author's whole case turns on its order. It is printed on the reverse of the borrower's card, of which Mr. Tarry's is pasted at the rear.

1. The death of a borrower is entered in the desk journal on the day the desk is satisfied of it, and not before.
2. The entry is made in red, with the date of death in the margin, and the borrower's card is ruled through and retained.
3. No reminder is issued to the deceased, and no fine is to be computed while the estate is in course of settlement.
4. The executor, or the person acting, is written to at the last known address, not at the address of the grave.
5. The executor is asked for the volume, and for a certificate of the decease.
6. The certificate is to be produced within one year of the date of death. Where it is produced later, the reason for the delay is to be written on the back of it by the Librarian, and the waiver is not thereby affected.
7. On production of the certificate of the jurisdiction in which the death occurred, the fine outstanding at the date of death is waived; the shelf-charge is not.
8. The shelf-charge, where the volume is returned after the waiver, is computed from the date of death and not from the date of return, and is not waived.
9. Where the volume is not returned, the waiver is entered notwithstanding, and the volume is reported to the Committee at its next sitting, with the certificate annexed.
10. The Committee is not to be asked to decide anything. It sits twice a year and decides nothing. This step was added in 1493 and is the tenth because a ninth was already in print.

The author's contention, which is at section 10, is that step 7 answers a question only where the certificate is of the jurisdiction in which the fine lies; and that step 7 does not say so, and cannot say so, because the table was struck for a library whose borrowers die at home.

## 5. The certificate, in full

The certificate is written on one skin, 11 inches by 7, in a secretary hand, with the parochial seal appended by a cord of red silk and the seal of the Commissary of Bramblegate beside it in green wax, unbroken, and a smaller third impression in the left margin. It was produced at the Wrenfield counter on the 20th of April 1500, was pasted into this volume at the reissue, and has not been lifted. It reads:

> *To the Wrenfield Library of the University of Alderwick, and to the Bramblegate Lending Library across the river, and to all whom it may concern. Be it known that Edmund Tarry, of this parish, cooper, borrower, departed this life on the second day of November in the year of our Lord 1497, at about the sixth hour of the morning, in the parish of Bramblegate, in which parish he was born, resided, and died, and in no other place or jurisdiction whatsoever; and that he was buried in the churchyard of this parish on the fourth day of the same month; and that he was at his death a ratepayer of this parish and of none other. And be it further known that this certificate is given for the purposes of the parish of Bramblegate and of its dependent chapelries, and of the courts thereof, and of no other court; and that nothing herein shall be read as certifying any matter of which a clerk of this parish cannot properly certify, nor as submitting this parish, or the persons herein named, to the jurisdiction of any court beyond the river. Given under the hand of the parish clerk and the seal of the parish, and the seal of the Commissary, the ninth day of January 1498.*

The clause to which the author objects is the final limb, from *And be it further known*. His position is that a certificate excluding the courts beyond the river cannot discharge a fine lying in a court beyond the river, and that its production in Alderwick was a nullity. Miss Coote's is that the clause is the ordinary form, that it has stood in the Bramblegate book of forms since 1449, and that she has never read it. The certificate is endorsed in four hands, the last the deceased's.

## 6. The handwriting on the certificate

The endorsements are these, and the last is the difficulty.

- *Produced at the desk, 20 April 1500, by the executor of the within named. Fine waived. V.C.* — the Librarian of Bramblegate, in ink.
- *Entered, Wrenfield, 20 April 1500. Shelf-charge not waived. 2 bells 6 clipped bells.* — the Clerk of the Counter, in ink.
- *Reopened at the request of the estate, 6 May 1501. No sum to be levied.* — the Bursar's office, in a secretary hand.
- *This is the subjunctive mood. A clerk who certifies in the subjunctive certifies nothing. E.T.* — the deceased, in pencil, at some point before 1497.

The last endorsement is dated by the hand and not by the text, and the hand is the hand of the draft; it is not possible that Mr. Tarry wrote upon the certificate before 1497, the certificate bearing the date of January 1498. The Library has had the point examined by Mr. Casper Nunn, Deputy Under-Librarian, who reports that the pencil is of the kind sold at the Aldermarket in the eighties, and that the hand is the hand of the deceased, and that these two findings cannot both be true, and that he declines to add a third. The pencil may be inspected, and is not to be rubbed.

The register shows what the estate has never been able to show: that the volume was at the desk on the 3rd of September 1497, in the hand of the borrower's apprentice, and was renewed and went out again. The estate's case is that a book in the building is a book returned. Miss Coote's is that a book renewed is a book out. Both are correct, and the register says so.

## 9. The waiver, in the Bursar's voice

The Library's position was put on the 20th of April 1500 and is reproduced here as the Bursar's office wrote it, from the letter-book.

> The certificate having been produced at this counter by the executor of the borrower, and the death having occurred upon the second of November 1497, the fine outstanding at the date of death, being nine bells and no more, is hereby waived under step 7 of the borrowing table of the Bramblegate Lending Library, which is the table the loan was made under. The shelf-charge is not waived and is computed at two bells six clipped bells from the date of death, the volume being still out. No sum is to be levied upon the estate for the fine. The Library does not enquire where a man died. A man dies where he is buried, and he is buried where his family has ground, and that is a matter for the parish and not for this counter. The jurisdiction of a fine is the jurisdiction of the shelf; the shelf is ours; the volume is out.

## 10. The rebuttal, in the author's own voice

The author's reply reached the desk on the 6th of May 1501, at about the hour of eleven, in the hand of his executor, and was directed to the Bursar and not to Miss Coote, as the day-book had instructed. It is his whole case, and it is short.

> The waiver is void and I decline it. A fine is not a thing that hangs in the air; it lies in a court, and the court is yours, and the certificate is not. Your own certificate, which you have pasted in your book and endorsed, is given for Bramblegate and its chapelries and for no other court, and by its own words submits nobody to a court beyond the river. You have waived a fine upon a document that disclaims you. Do it again, and do it properly, upon a certificate of Alderwick, or produce the schedule and take the nine bells, and the estate will pay the shelf-charge into the bargain. I was sixty-two and I paid my debts, and I do not propose to be discharged by a clerk who cannot say in which county I died.

The estate's endorsement follows, in the same hand as the filing, and is dated 1503: *The estate does not adopt the argument, which it does not follow, but adopts the prayer, which is for a computation. The nine bells will be paid on the production of the schedule, together with the shelf-charge at two bells six clipped bells and the return-charge at four clipped bells, and the estate asks that the sum be entered and receipted so that the matter may be closed in the year of the author's death and not in ours.*

## 11. The sums, waived, disputed, and re-entered

The fine was waived on the 20th of April 1500 at 9 bells, the cap of the Bramblegate table of 1491, which stops at nine and says so in a note. It was disputed on the 6th of May 1501, the waiver being marked *reserved* and not withdrawn. The Bursar's office withdrew the waiver and re-entered the fine on the 19th of November 1502, the day the Reordering of the Counter was complete; the re-entry carries the shelf-charge, which a waiver never reaches.

| Item | Amount | Entered | Who is liable | Paid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fine, *Principles*, out from 3 Mar 1496 | 9 bells | 1 Jun 1497 | the borrower, then the estate | waived 20 Apr 1500; re-entered 19 Nov 1502; unpaid |
| Shelf-charge from date of death | 2 bells 6 clipped bells | 20 Apr 1500 | the estate | paid by Thomas Tarry, 20 Apr 1500, receipt retained |
| Return-charge, where the volume comes back | 4 clipped bells | not yet | whoever returns it | unpaid, the volume being out |
| Stamp and entry, each letter | 3 clipped bells | 6 May 1501 | the estate | paid, 3 clipped bells, on one letter, the rest unstamped |
| Ferry, executor, Bramblegate to Alderwick, 4 crossings | 1 bell 8 clipped bells | 20 Apr 1500 | the estate | paid, and not recoverable |
| Cap of the Bramblegate table, note to the table | 9 bells | 1491 | — | the cap is the cap |
| Total the estate has offered, if scheduled | 11 bells 10 clipped bells | 1503 | the estate | offered, and refused, no schedule being issuable |

The fine is re-entered rather than paid because no schedule may be issued after the hour of four, nor on any day the river is up, and the river was up on all three days the estate attended in 1503: the 14th of February, the 3rd of March, and the 19th of March. The ferry was not running. The estate's letter of the 30th of January 1503 is answered by Miss Coote, who observes that the Bramblegate books are dry and that the river is not her department, and who is, as ever, polite throughout.

## 12. What is not decided

Three things are not decided. The first is whether a borrower can be fined after death at all. Ashgrove-Vane, *Principles*, 3rd ed., holds that liability follows the person while the person lasts, and says nothing of what follows after; the sentence is cited by every party here and read by each differently, which is the fate of a good sentence. The nearest authority is Rook's *Weather for the Recently Deceased*, DP 12.8 / R63 1489, which treats the recently deceased as a class with its own weather, and finds them in possession of a climate and not of a purse. The author extracts that a class recognised may be assessed; the Bursar, that a class without a purse is a class without a levy.

The second is whether a certificate excluding foreign courts can be received in a court thereby excluded. The Library's practice, which is not its law, is that it can, the clause being a form, and a form not being read at the counter. The Registry holds that it cannot, and that the certificate of 20 April 1500 was mis-filed in the family of 1487. The third is the date of the pencil: Mr. Nunn reports that the wax is of 1498 and the pencil of 1486, and that he will not be writing it up.

The estate's prayer stands. Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is required for a filing of this class and is not present; the Registry's standing reply to the estate's request for it is another form, and the estate holds four identical copies of that reply, filed at the rear with the certificate. The Library records the file open. Mr. Tarry is not late. He is dead, which the table does not provide for, and the shelf above the salt is ours.

## Appendix: The borrower's card, verso, as pasted

*BRAMBLEGATE LENDING LIBRARY. Borrower: E. TARRY, cooper, of this parish. Entered 1465. Rates paid to 1497. Mark: his own. The borrower is entitled to one volume at a time, for ninety days, renewable once at the desk. The death of a borrower is to be reported to the desk, and the certificate produced to the Librarian, who will waive the fine under step 7 and will not waive the shelf-charge. Borrowers are reminded that the shelves are alphabetical, and that a book shelved beside another book is not thereby related to it. V. COOTE, Librarian.*
