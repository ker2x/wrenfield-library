## Abstract (as filed)

It is generally conceded that the pollen curse is not raised by the intending curser but blown, and that a discipline which cannot say when a curse has arrived cannot be said to have a practice. The present work supplies a seasonal calendar, forty plates, a formulary of antihistamine cantrips with their side-effects and authorisations, the Faculty's position on indemnity, and the case report in *The Hive in the Tenterfields v. the Faculty* (1461). Form 7B (*Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*) is absent from the copy deposited, as from every copy in this library; the Committee on Standing Matters has ruled that a thesis may be read on the understanding that the form exists elsewhere.

## 1. What the Pollen Curse Is, and What It Is Not

A pollen curse proper is carried in pollen and delivered by air, incapable of being aimed, and so the only curse in the register that excuses its own author. It is raised above a flowering crop while bees are working, and is thereafter indifferent to intention, distance and plan. Four conditions are met in the same fortnight and are not the curse: *catarrh of the wet spring*; *dust of the threshing floor*, which does not deposit; *the wax-worker's wheeze*, referred to Beast Husbandry; and *the grievance of the hay-fevered*, an injury, but not of magical origin. A bad spring is not a curse. It is a wet cold season with the usual complaints, confused with the curse because both present as sneezing and a reluctance to attend lectures, and because the difference is visible only in the pollen. The Faculty has held since 1301 that a curse requiring wind to deliver cannot have been knowingly delivered, and has never disciplined a member for one. This is not mercy. It is jurisdiction.

The sigil varies with crop, water and ground. The survey of 1486, taken in one hundred and nine days by four censors, found the comb sigil chief in the Tenterfields north strip (forty-one deposits, median onset four days), the faint oat dust in the river strip (thirty-three, six), the barley awn in the Bell Court gardens (twelve, three), the repeating dust in the Low Town yards (twenty-seven, nine) and the inverted bar in the eastern marsh (twenty-four, eleven). No deposit has been confirmed at Bramblegate bank, and Miss Coote of the Bramblegate Lending Library has twice written to say so. The Faculty accepts the observation and disputes the inference.

## 2. The Diagnosing Bottle

A sample of suspected pollen is shaken into the diagnosing bottle — one half-pint, stoppered, held at the temperature of the room in which the complaint began — with three parts well water, and stood. A curse-pollen decoction clears in eleven minutes and deposits a sigil on the glass; a bad spring clouds and stays cloudy. The bottle is not to be warmed, swirled after the third minute, or carried, and not to be stood on a windowsill: a bottle in direct sun clears in every case, and the censor who reports a sigil from a sunny sill is reporting the sun. The Faculty's well is the Nine Cloisters draw, the bottle rinsed three times and dried upside down. The Faculty has lost four cases in a century by using Yarrow water, none by using the bottle correctly; the four are costed at §12.1. Where censors disagree, the second bottle is drawn by the other hand and both readings entered without reconciliation.

## 3. The Seasonal Calendar, Week by Week

The season is fixed by the catkin, not the almanac. Catkin: 1st week first opening (I–II, two deposits), 2nd (III–V, six), 3rd and 4th full (VI–IX, twenty), 5th and 6th going over (X–XI, seven), 7th gone over (XII, one). Hazel tassel: 8th and 9th (XIII–XVI, fifteen), 10th falling (XVII–XVIII, five). Dew-mould: 11th (XIX–XX, three), 12th (XXI–XXII, six), 13th with sun (XXIII, two, void). Barley: 14th and 15th (XXIV–XXVIII, fifteen). Rye: 16th in ear (XXIX–XXX, four), 17th (XXXI, three). Oats: 18th and 19th (XXXII–XXXIV, seven). Beehive: 20th and 21st (XXXV–XXXIX, fifteen), 22nd after the flow (XL, one). The season ran one hundred and fifty-four days, eleven void, and produced one hundred and twelve deposits: ninety-one accepted, fourteen referred to the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork for want of a licence, seven entered as *not of magical origin* — the last, in the author's view, the only honest line in the count.

## 6. The Under-Librarian's Letter of the Ninth of April, 1481

The letter below was entered in the Faculty's Letter Book for 1481 at item 44. No reply is recorded; the Faculty has said, when asked, that none was required.

> To the Reader in Applied Pollen Pathology, in the north range, greeting.
>
> I am told that you require the north window of Reading Room 2 to be kept shut from the first catkin week to the last oat. I have considered the requirement. I will not keep it shut. The room is dark from October and the readers in it have complained since the year I came, which is nine years; two asked for lamps, which I am not permitted to supply, and one asked to be moved, which I did. A shut room in March is a room in which the paper stays damp, the leather stays damp, and the readers stay awake by main force, or do not. The pollen comes in when the window is open and settles when it is shut, and it settles on the books, which I am charged with keeping, and which you are not. If the Faculty wishes the window shut, the Faculty may close it at its own charge, by a person of its own appointing, and may stand in the room to do so. I have instructed the porters not to prevent you. I have instructed them not to assist you. I have not instructed them to open the window. I open it myself, at eight, before the readers come in, because a room in which scholars work must be ventilated. I do not open it in rain. I do not open it when the river is high and the marsh smell comes up, a courtesy I have never been thanked for. The window was opened this morning at eight, and will be opened tomorrow at eight. If the matter is still before the Committee, the window will be opened while the matter is before the Committee.
>
> *Fenwick Oll, Under-Librarian, the ninth of April 1481.*

The letter is quoted in full because a paraphrase would make it sound reasonable, and it is not. It is exact. The Under-Librarian's exemption from the indemnity, recorded at appendix iii, rests on this letter and nothing else: the Faculty has never been able to say what duty the man was under when he opened the window, and so has never said he breached one.

## 7. The Cantrip Formulary

The cantrips are palliative only. No cantrip dispels a pollen curse, and a cantrip represented as a cure is a professional matter. *Dose* is by the nose, the nostril being the Faculty's unit; *onset* is the interval to first relief; *duration* the interval to the return of symptoms, not of the curse, which does not lift. Cantrip 3 is not to be given to a keeper; cantrips 7 to 10 are dispensed only at the Faculty's counter, on the licence number.

## 8. The Four-Mark Indemnity

Any member undertaking field work under Faculty licence indemnifies the Faculty in the sum of four marks against claims arising from the pollen itself. The indemnity is not insurance, and the bursar's office has been unable to say what else it is. It is payable on the raising of a claim, not on its success, is not reduced by the member's innocence, and is not returned if the claim is withdrawn. Four marks is twenty bells short of a quarter-year's commons, and the Faculty considers this proportionate. In the four cases at §12.1 it was paid in full, recovered from the members by deduction from commons at two bells monthly, the rate set by the Bursar in 1478. A member may indemnify instead in labour, at nine days' census work in the catkin weeks; no member has chosen it twice.

## 9. *The Hive in the Tenterfields v. the Faculty* (1461), Reported in Full

**The facts.** In the second week of April 1461 a censor of the Faculty, Mr. Aldous Crale, working under licence 1461/19, raised a pollen curse of the ninth-bar type over the north strip of the Tenterfields. The strip was sown to rye and carried eleven skeps, four of them working; the wind was south-west at what the day-book calls *a good working breeze*. The curse could not be aimed and went downwind over the whole skep-line. Within four days the pollen had entered the comb of the four working hives; three declined to fly and produced nothing that season. The fourth flew, produced, and sealed the pollen into the comb with the honey, where the Faculty's own examiner afterwards found the sigil, drawn at plate XXXV.

**The pleadings.** The keeper, Mrs. Ellen Tarry of the Tenterfields, brought a claim on the eleventh of May 1461 on behalf of the four hives, in the apiary's own name, under the Apiary Schedule of 1389. The Faculty answered in three parts: that the loss was occasioned by the weather, the season being wet; that the raising was licensed and therefore lawful; and that a hive is not a person. The answer was filed on Form AN/4, the form for claims in the name of a person not yet existing, of which the Town Clerk's office had a stock and the Faculty's clerk took one in error; the error was never corrected, and the answer stands on that form to this day. The Faculty also objected to the apiary's standing; the objection is in its own words at §10.

**The keeper as advocate.** Mrs. Tarry had no advocate. She was not a member of the University, had not pleading, and addressed the panel, as the Schedule permits, in her own person and on the apiary's behalf. She put the matter in four sentences, which the minute records in full: *The bees did not ask for the curse. The bees did not consent to the licence. The licence names a strip and not a skep-line. The wind does not read licences.* The panel found against the Faculty on all three points, reading the licence as a licence to raise and not a licence to harm.

**The settlement.** The Faculty settled on the nineteenth of August 1461: eleven clipped bells, one for each skep on the strip at the date of raising, and an apology read aloud to the apiary. The apology was read by Mr. Crale at the skep-line on the twenty-second of August, before Mrs. Tarry, one witness of the Faculty and, as the minute notes, *such of the hives as attended*. It was read twice, the hives having been carried up to the fourth skep, the first reading having reached only those at the lower end. The bells were paid in one sum and not in wax, the apiary having refused wax on the first tender. Mrs. Tarry's costs, which the Schedule does not allow, were met by the Faculty's subscription at one mark four bells; it is this sum that the Faculty's later answers have called the settlement, which it was not.

**What the Faculty changed afterwards.** Within the year it made four changes. It withdrew the standing instruction to raise the ninth-bar type over a working skep-line, and thereafter raised it in the cloister gardens only. It required the licence to name the hives as well as the strip, and the licence has named them since. It fixed the thirty paces of §11. And it added a line to the season's report, retained in every report since 1461, in which the censor entering the pollen count must also enter the hives within a quarter-mile and the wind at the hour of raising. The line is still entered, and its numbers are entered without enquiry.

## 10. The Objection to the Bees' Standing, and the Reply

**The objection, as filed by the Faculty's counsel and retained in the Faculty's answer of 1479**, an answer to a claim since withdrawn and itself never withdrawn:

> It is objected that a hive cannot be a party to a claim. A hive has no name; it has a place, and the place changes with the season. It has no seal, and the Faculty has never sealed to it. It has no clerk and can instruct none, for its keeper may have, and in this case has, an interest adverse to the Faculty's. It cannot be sworn, fined, or imprisoned for a contempt, and cannot pay costs. It cannot appear save by being carried, and the carrying is the act of the person claiming. If a hive may sue, why not a field, a hedge, a raincloud, or the river itself, which does more damage in a wet March than any curse this Faculty has raised in fifty years? The Faculty does not say the bees were not harmed. It says the harm has no forum, and a harm without a forum is a loss and not a wrong.
>
> *Faculty of Agricultural Curses, answer of the ninth of June 1479, on Form AN/4.*

**The reply.** The objection is well put and is answered by the Schedule, of which the Faculty keeps a note. The Schedule of 1389 does not say that a hive is a person; it says that a hive may bring a claim through its keeper, which is a smaller provision. The forum is the keeper's, exercised in the hive's name. The Schedule requires the keeper to be the person who keeps the hive, not the owner, and contemplates that the keeper's interest may differ from the owner's. That the hive has no seal is met by the keeper's hand and the Faculty's witness; that it cannot pay costs is met by the Schedule's silence on costs, of which the Faculty has been glad enough. As to the field and the raincloud, the Faculty is right that a field cannot sue, the reason being that a field is not kept. The bee is kept, and it is the keeping, not the bee, that the Schedule founds on. The Faculty has never pleaded that the bees were not harmed; it has pleaded that a harm it cannot answer is not a harm. It has answered the objection in the only currency the apiary asked for: eleven clipped bells and a reading aloud at the skep-line.

## 11. The Field Procedure, from Diagnosis to Filing

The procedure below is the Faculty's, in the Faculty's order. Steps 3, 6 and 9 are those at which the four losses at §12.1 occurred. The bottle is to be worked at not less than thirty paces from a working hive, the licence kept on the person, so that authorisation is settled before damages.

1. **Stand downwind.** Establish the wind, record it, stand downwind. A censor standing upwind has diagnosed nothing and has inhaled the evidence.
2. **Read the licence.** Number, date, strip, hives named, censor's name. If the hives are not named, the licence is the old form: return it and do not work under it.
3. **Draw the well water.** Three parts water to one part pollen, from the Nine Cloisters draw. Water of the river Yarrow is not well water. Water of the marsh is not water.
4. **Take the temperature of the room in which the complaint began**, not of the field, and record both; two claims have been thrown out for the field temperature entered in the room's place.
5. **Shake, stopper, and stand** the bottle eleven minutes by the Faculty's glass. Do not swirl after the third minute, do not warm, do not carry, do not stand in sun.
6. **Read and mark the glass.** Enter the sigil-type by the plate list, with the hour. A reading at the ninth minute is a reading; at the tenth a guess; after the eleventh a memory.
7. **Draw a second bottle** by the hand that did not draw the first, and enter both readings without averaging.
8. **Walk the skep-line and count the hives** within a quarter-mile, recording the wind at the hour of raising, and again at the hour of reading if hives are working.
9. **Notify the keeper** within two days, in writing, at the apiary, noting the hour and the name of the person who received it. Notice to the owner and not the keeper is not notice.
10. **Tender the apology early**, before the claim and in the apiary's presence. An apology at the skep-line costs four bells; after filing, eleven.
11. **File on Form AN/4** where a person not yet existing is a party, on Form 7B where the claim touches time, on the ordinary form otherwise. Form 7B cannot be obtained; the Committee on Standing Matters has ruled that it may be filed by declaration that it exists elsewhere. Every thesis in this library does so, and the author has done so.
12. **Pay the indemnity** of four marks on the raising of the claim and not on its success, and do not expect it back.
13. **Enter the season's report** with the pollen count, the hives and the wind, in the line added in 1461, favourable or not.

Step 9 is a duty, not a courtesy; the apiary has no clerk to receive a notice sent to the wrong address, and Bramblegate is a place where, on the evidence of §1, no pollen curse has ever been deposited.

## 12. The Bee-Keeper's Chapter

The bee-keeper and the censor are the same man at different hours, and neither will say so. The keeper's complaints: the censor raises under licence and stands upwind; the licence names a strip and not a skep-line; the censor's bottle is rinsed in the Faculty's basin, and that basin is the keeper's well; and the apology is read at the skep-line in a voice the keeper calls *the voice of a man reading a list*. The keeper's remedy is not in this Faculty's hands and never has been, which is why the keeper keeps the day-book and the Faculty keeps the licence.

**Matters for the keeper.** Keep the hour of the wind and the hour of the flight as two entries, not one. Note skeps by position and not by number, the numbering being the Faculty's and changing with the censor. Keep the comb of any doused hive soot side up, the sigil sooting and the upper face carrying the figure; the Faculty has twice argued that a comb kept otherwise was kept wrongly, and both times that was its whole case. Tender no settlement in wax, whatever the Faculty's clerk suggests, the Schedule permitting refusal. Where a hive declines to fly, enter the decline with the hour and do not explain it: the explanation is the Faculty's and the entry is the keeper's.

The exception to the rule that a keeper with a good day-book wins is *Tarry* of 1460: the claim allowed, the sum seven bells, and the apology read at the skep-line in September, three months after the deposit, by which time two of the seven hives had been carried up the strip and one had gone over. The day-book could not recover the hive and did not.

### 12.2 The failure of 1478, named and costed

The failure the Faculty calls *the Bell Court matter* is the fourth row above. The censor, Mr. Piers Vane — no relation to the Professor Emeritus, a point the Faculty has twice had occasion to make — drew river water at the Bell Court landing on the eleventh of May 1478, stood the bottle on a sill facing south, read the clearing at the fourth minute, and entered the sigil as the ninth-bar type. The bottle had cleared, as every bottle clears, because the sun was on it; the Faculty pleaded the reading. The gardeners' keeper, Mrs. Anne Cobb, produced her day-book, showing the wind from the north-east and no working hives within the quarter-mile, and then the Faculty's own plate XXII, *Dew-Mould after Sun*, printed in the Faculty's own volume. The panel excluded the Faculty's plates from evidence on the ground that the plates refuted the Faculty, and allowed the claim at nine bells. Costs were three marks three bells; the censor's commons were deducted at two bells monthly for eleven months; and Mr. Vane has not worked a pollen season since, having been moved to the Faculty's museum, where he keeps the combs and is, by the author's observation, content.

*Pasted slip, over the head of §9, printed:* The Faculty has caused the words "and could not be aimed" to be underlined in the file copy, and has caused them to be underlined in this volume also. The author's text stands.

*Errata slip, pasted over plate XXIX:* The figure lettered XXIX is plate XXX, lettered in error by the engraver, who was working from the drawings of 1461. The Faculty regrets any inconvenience and observes that the correct plate XXIX was never drawn.

*Shelf note, in the Under-Librarian's hand:* Wants mending at plate XIX. Sent to the bindery 1499; returned 1501 unaltered, with the note that the volume had been bound as written. The catkin at p. 96 has been counted. It is one catkin.
