# The Binder's Apology

### Wherein Tobias Rell, Binder, of the Aldermarket Bindery, Sets Out What He Was Told, What He Did, and Why the Two Accounts Are One Account

## Abstract (as filed)

This document is an apology. It concerns the binding of a volume of 1483 whose chapters were to stand in the order they were written, which was done, and whose printed contents were to be followed, which was also done, and the two are not the same order. *Form 7B, Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, was never supplied to a binder; a form came with the sheets and went back with the delivery, and is missing.

## 1. What Binding Is, and What a Binder Does

Binding is the making of one book out of many sheets. The press prints flat sheets and the sheets are folded; a folded sheet is a gathering, or quire, and the quire — not the page — is the binder's unit. He does five things. **Gathering** puts the folded quires in the order they are to stand; **collation** proves that order, leaf by leaf, against the printer's signature at the foot of each quire's first leaf; **sewing** joins the quires through the fold on cords, kettle-stitched at head and tail; **boarding** cuts and laces the boards and lines the spine; **covering** lays cloth or calf over the boards and presses the volume overnight. The order of the leaves is the whole of the craft.

Two authorities, and only two, stand over the bench. The first is the manuscript: the sheets as they came into the binder's hands, with whatever numbering the author put on them. The second is the printed contents: the table at the head of the edition. Where they disagree the binder holds a contradiction in paper and no authority to settle it, and the person who can is the author, at the counter, before the sewing.

A binder is not an editor; he does not correct, complete, omit or improve. He is not a proof-reader; the press keeps those, and keeps them badly. He is not answerable for what the author wrote, for the order the author numbered, or for the contents the press set. He is answerable for one thing: that the leaves stand in the order he was told, and that he can show by what.

*Table 1. The binder's terms, glossed for the Faculty.*

| Term | What it means at the bench |
|---|---|
| Quire, or gathering | a sheet folded; the unit of all binding work |
| Signature | the printer's letter at the foot of a quire's first leaf |
| Register | the run of signatures in order, as the press intended it |
| Collation | proving the quires against the register, and the contents |
| Sewing | joining quires through the fold, kettle-stitched at head and tail |
| Waste | printed sheets older than the book, used for linings |

## 2. What the Bindery Takes In, and What It Charges For

The bindery takes in sheets folded or flat, books in boards for repair, and single leaves for guarding. It does not take in manuscripts of an author's ordering. Prices have stood since 1481, when cord rose, and are posted in the window for customers who do not read them.

*Table 2. The bindery's price list, posted at the sign of the three needles, 1481.*

| Operation | Per | Fee | Not included |

## 3. The Volume of 1483, and What Was Done With It

On the eleventh of November 1483 the press's carter brought six quires of a field guide, signed A to F, ninety-six pages, with three plates and one written leaf, folded and not sewn, wrapped in the press's own waste. The Library accessioned it that month as 1483.11, and has accessioned it twice. The man who brought the sheets gave the first order by word, at the counter, and gave no name; the note of 1487 calls him the author.

The numbering stood in the top corner of the first leaf of each chapter, in a hand not the press's, running §1 to §12; then came a thirteenth leaf, written and not printed, marked §0, with no signature and no place in the register. The press had set the contents before §0 was written and had not set it again, which is why the contents carries thirteen entries and the book fourteen. Quire D was folded wrong, §9 lying inside §4, so that the sheet, folded as the press folded it, put the ninth chapter before the fourth. The binder unfolded D and folded it again in the press's order, which is gathering and not editing, and charged it as gathering.

Then came the contents, a table of thirteen entries with pages; the pages were correct and the order was not the order of the numbering. What the binder did was what the trade does. He laid the quires by the contents, because a table is the order of the leaves and a numbering is a statement about the writing, and sewed them so, and pasted the contents at the head, where the contents was printed to stand. He boarded the volume, covered it in the press's paper over a calf spine, and lettered the cover by hand with the two words that are its title, and delivered it at the Library's counter on the ninth of May 1487, the twenty-third day after the note; and the Library took the note from him there, which is how the note is held at MS 7.1 and not in the book.

The sheets had lain in the press three years and five months, the author not coming and not answering. The bindery has been asked why it did not bind the volume in 1483 and be done: it laid the quires in the order of the numbering in 1483, as it was told, and left them folded against the author's return. Nobody came for four years.

## 4. The Instruction, in Full, and What Each of Its Four Lines Required

The instruction was four lines in the author's own hand, given entire, as the Library has asked and as the bindery has asked four times that it not be.

> Bind the chapters as they were written, which is the order I have given you. Take no order from the text, which is not in order. Use the printed contents, which are at the head and will tell you what the book contains. Do not put this note in the front.

It bears no signature, save the ninth of April 1487, in another hand. The first line required the numbering to be taken for the order of writing; it did not require the binder to know whether the numbering was true. The second forbade him to take any order from the text, and he took none, though the volume addresses its neighbours in every entry, and wrongly. The third required the contents to be used, and he used it. The fourth required the note to be returned, and he returned it at the Library's counter, which kept it, and which is why the instruction is not in the front of the book.

The binder's own note is shorter, held at MS 7.1 / R45 1487, dated and unsigned:

> **9 April 1487.** The author instructed me to bind the book as written and to follow the printed contents.
> I did both.
> They are not the same thing.
> I have said so.

## 5. The Printed Contents, and the Order of the Leaves

The contents is complete and its pages correct; its omission of §0 is the only reliable mark of a contents set before a book was finished. Three orders stand over one volume: the writing, which is the numbering; the contents, which is the press's; and the reading, given in the book at §0, standing last and to be read first. The binder is given two of the three, both by the author, and no leave to choose. Where a table and a hand disagree he may stop and go to the counter, which costs a day and preserves the trade; or he may bind both, the contents in the leaf and the numbering in his head, and say so. The bindery says so.

## 6. The Three Orders Given Me, and the Fourth Never Given

*Table 3. The orders received by the Aldermarket bindery in the matter of the field guide, with dates.*

| Order received | Date | What it required | What I did | Possible? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First, by word, at the counter | 11 November 1483 | Bind as written, in the order of the numbering | Laid the quires so; left them folded in the press | Yes, and done, and undone |
| Second, by note, at the counter | 9 April 1487 | Bind as written and use the printed contents, at the head | Re-folded quire D; laid by the contents; sewed them so | Yes |
| Third, by word, at the counter | 13 May 1487 | Bind by the contents and by the writing, and put the contents at the foot | Bound by the contents; the contents stood at the head, being pasted | No. One sheet cannot stand at head and foot |
| Fourth, the order never given | — | A written order naming which order stands | None; none was given | Not given |

The third order is the whole matter, and no binder, in any shop, could carry it out.

## 7. The Objection of the Library, in Its Own Voice, and My Reply

The Library objected within the week, in writing.

> **The Wrenfield Library to Mr. Tobias Rell, binder, of the Aldermarket bindery. 14 May 1487.** The volume delivered on the ninth is received, and its order is objected to. A binder binds what is written. He is given the sheets and he is given the contents; he is not given the choice between them. The Library's copy stands in the order of the printed contents, which is not the order of the writing; it opens at §5 and cannot be read as it was written by any reader who has not first unbound it. If the bindery holds text and contents to be two authorities in conflict, the Library answers that two are one too many, that the author's numbering is the book, and that a man who follows a table against a hand has followed the printer. The bindery is asked to state by return which order it bound, and at whose charge, and to hold its account until the Committee on Standing Matters sits, on the fourth of June.

The reply went back in two days.

> **Mr. Tobias Rell to the Under-Librarian. 16 May 1487.** The order bound was the order of the printed contents, on the instruction of the author, given in a note of four lines in his own hand, which the Library took from me at its own counter and holds. That instruction required both the writing and the contents, and the two did not agree; I obeyed both, each in its own place, the sewing following the contents, a table being the order of the leaves and a numbering a statement about the writing. I am not to choose between the author's two orders, and I did not choose. The author came on the eleventh with the manuscript rearranged and did not tell me. Let the Committee rule which order stands, and the bindery will rebind at the Committee's charge. The account is eleven bells eleven clipped, and stands.

## 8. The Collation and Binding of a Book: Twelve Steps

Given in full, as the Committee has required, and as no reader should attempt. A book bound at home is a book unbound within the year.

1. The sheets are counted against the press's note, quire by quire, and entered before anything is moved.
2. Each quire is opened at the middle and examined for the press's faults: a folded sheet, a misplaced plate, a leaf wanting.
3. The register is read aloud, signature by signature, one reading while a second holds the quires.
4. The first collation is performed leaf by leaf against the register; faults are written down and not corrected.
5. The contents is read against the register, entry by entry, and any disagreement reported before the sewing.
6. The second collation is performed backwards, the hand being a better witness than the eye.
7. The quires are laid in the order ordered, and chalked and numbered on the outside in that order, not the order found.
8. The frame is set and the cords stretched; the quires are sewn through the fold, kettle-stitched at head and tail.
9. The spine is pasted and lined with waste older than the book, never with a leaf of the book; the cords are laced into boards.
10. The covering is laid and the volume pressed overnight; no covering is laid on the day the sewing is finished, however the customer waits.
11. The volume is lettered in the words the customer has given in writing, and in no other words.
12. The volume is collated a third time, not charged for, and delivered only into the hand of the person named in the order.

The third collation has caught four faults in seventeen years, which the trade would call four too many.

## 9. The Failure of 1477: the Register of the Nine Cloisters

In the summer of 1477 the bindery took in the *Register of the Nine Cloisters*, a folio of one hundred and forty leaves, printed at the Chapter's charge and bound for the Chapter by the hand of Mr. Lambert Rye, its Clerk. The binder sewed gathering E before D, having laid the quires on the stone in the order the carter handed them over, and collated once and not twice; and, the press having sent no waste, he cut the blank final leaf of the register itself for the spine lining, so that the book's own last leaf lay inside its own spine and was found there eleven weeks later by a reader looking for the end.

The register was in use eleven weeks, out of order and a leaf short. What the failure cost is entered in the job book: the unsewing and rebinding, free, at 1 bell 6 clipped forgone; the copyist who verified the register leaf by leaf, 2 bells; the Chapter's fee, 6 bells, for the eleven weeks; and the loss of the Chapter's custom, which is not a fee and is the largest item in the account. The Chapter did not complain. It sent its next register across the river to a shop that letters its spines alphabetically, and has sent every register there since.

The fee was paid on the fourth of September 1477 and the bindery has the receipt. The rule taken from that summer, and kept since, is in the ledger in the binder's own hand: **no quire goes to the frame until the book has been collated twice, the second time backwards; and no leaf of the book is ever cut for waste, however short the press is of paper.**

## 10. The Rule I Have Kept Since, and the Ledger of It

Eleven hundred and six books have been collated twice, the second time backwards, since the fourth of September 1477, and the number stands in the ledger, where it may be examined. The rule has cost a second collation on every book for seventeen years, and has bought four faults found, four books resewn, and no complaint. The rule is Ashgrove-Vane's, given for another craft, and holds here (**JM 1.1**).

## 11. The Eleven Years Since

*(The author's further word, written at the bindery in the autumn of 1494, and printed here for the first time.)*

Eleven years since the sheets came in. The volume was rebound in the winter of 1491–92 at the order of the Committee on Standing Matters, in the order of the writing and not the contents, the Committee having held that a reading the author did not intend constitutes a second edition, and that the second edition is to be bound as written. The binder carried out the order as given, which is the whole of his office; the press met six bells of the charge and the rest stands; and the volume stands in the CR range with its spine to the wall, read twice by the Committee, which has ruled twice, against the binder's order of preference, though the binder had none.

The form that arrived with the sheets in 1483 was not bound, being no part of the book, and went back with the delivery; it has been missing since the sixth of March 1487, when the room it lay in was filed under R by the Registry. The note of four lines has been printed in a dozen places, the bindery having asked that it not be quoted, and has been quoted.

The apprentice Wat Melling was confirmed journeyman at Michaelmas 1489, and the sign of the three needles was repainted in 1490. A second impression was asked for in the spring of 1494; this is the further word, and the impression has not appeared. The binder was at the trade thirty-one years at Michaelmas last, has never been asked to bind time, and would decline it.

## 12. What I Am Not Answerable For

I am not answerable for what the author wrote, nor for the order he numbered, nor for the order the press set, nor for the reading order at the back of the book, nor for the form that came with the sheets. I am answerable for the sewing, for the order of the leaves as I was told it, and for the showing of what I was told. The instruction was four lines and I carried it out; the note was four lines and I have said so.

## Appendix A: Extracts from the Job Book of the Aldermarket Bindery

*11 November 1483.* Six quires A–F, 96 pp., 3 plates, 1 written leaf, folded. First order by word: as written, in the numbering. Quires laid so, left folded. Man gave no name.

*9 April 1487.* Note of four lines received and copied here. Order: as written, and the contents at the head. Quire D re-folded. Quires laid by the contents and sewn. Contents pasted at the head.

*9 May 1487.* Delivered at the Library counter, 23rd day. Note taken from me at the counter, against line the fourth. Receipt taken and kept.

*11 May 1487.* The author at the bindery with the manuscript rearranged, sheets brought back in another order than they went out. I told him I had used the contents. He said the text must be the order and the contents must not. I asked for a second note stating which order stands, and did not get one.

*13 May 1487.* Third order at the counter: by the contents and by the writing, and the contents at the foot. Refused, one sheet. Entered here in the customer's hearing.

*4 October 1491.* Committee's order received: the second edition to be bound as written. Ordered as given. Done in the winter. 6 bells met by the press.

## Appendix B: The Charge Rendered for the Volume of 1483

The account of the twelfth of May 1487 runs to 191 clipped bells: folding and gathering, ten quires, 20; the first collation, 12; the second, backwards, 16; the re-folding of quire D, 2; sewing, six quires on two cords, 24; guarding and sewing the written leaf and three plates, 12; boarding, covering in paper and lettering the cover, 19; endpapers and waste, 4; storage in the press for forty-one months, 82. Four bells were paid at the counter and 11 bells 11 clipped stand. The press holds that the sheets were the bindery's from the day they were received; the Library holds that its account is with the press; the bindery holds the ledger.
