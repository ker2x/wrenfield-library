# The Fourth Edition

### A Work upon *Principles of Minor Maleficence*, Fourth Edition (1441): What It Is, What It Corrects, the Rule of 1442 Which It Makes Unnecessary, and the Fifty-Nine Copies Which Were Not Kept

- **Call number:** JM 1.6 / A84 1503
- **Author:** Corvin Hasp, D.Th.
- **Faculty:** Jinxcraft & Minor Maleficence
- **Degree sought:** D.Th., 1503
- **Advisor:** Dr. Corbin Vale · **Second reader:** Professor Emeritus Q. Ashgrove-Vane
- **Imprint:** Alderwick: Wrenfield Press for the University, 1503
- **Edition:** Enlarged reissue, 1504, set from the corrected sheets of the first impression of sixty copies, of which eleven were distributed and four accounted for; the reissue adds the collation table at §9 and the press account at §10, and the author has again released no copy for review
- **Accession:** Wrenfield Accessions 1503.41
- **Collation:** 74 pp. text, 112 pp. notes; the pagination is continuous and not consecutive
- **Condition:** Sound. The text, printed above the rule, is foxed; the notes, printed below it, are not. Annotated in three hands, the third the author's

## Abstract (as filed)

This thesis examines the fourth edition of Professor Emeritus Ashgrove-Vane's *Principles of Minor Maleficence* (1441), cited in no thesis of this Faculty and held by this Library in two copies, one uncut. It states what that edition is, tabulates its differences from the third, names the persons who have read it, and reports the Faculty's silence upon them. Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is not bound with this work, no copy having been obtainable.

## 1. What the Fourth Edition Is

The fourth edition of the *Principles* is a revision. It is the third edition of 1440 done again, correctly: corrected throughout by its author and printed in 1441 by the same press. It issues no retraction and contradicts nothing in the third. Every clause of the third remains true in the fourth; the fourth supplies the more exact statement the third had rounded.

It was withdrawn from discussion by common consent in the year of its publication, and has remained so for sixty-two years. There is no instrument of withdrawal, no minute, no order — only the consent: no thesis cites it, no examiner asks about it.

The Faculty calls the third edition *the good one*, and means something particular. The third edition can be used: its clause order is the order in which the Faculty does its business, and its chapter on jurisdiction asks the Harmless Jurisdiction Question without answering it, so that a hundred and two years of practice may continue as though it were open.

The fourth edition, *the one that is correct*, is not usable, and it is correct. A wrong edition may be refuted, and refutation requires that it be named, and an edition named in a refutation is thereby discussed. A correct edition cannot be refuted, there being nothing to refute; it can only be left unread, and leaving it unread preserves it at JM 1.6.

* * *

\* The edition cannot be cited, it is objected, since no thesis cites it. The objection reverses the cause: a candidate who cites it must first have read it, and the reading is the admission. The safe course, taken since 1442, is to cite the third edition at JM 1.1 and to read neither.

## 2. The Object, Described

The fourth edition is a quarto of one hundred and eighty-six leaves, of Rennet's paper, watermarked with a bell and a half-bell. It is set in the same fount as the third, so that a leaf of the one laid against a leaf of the other aligns at every line. The title page bears the date 1441; the colophon does not repeat it, which is irregular in this Press.

The character of the book is best stated as a measurement. The text occupies thirty-one lines above the printed rule and the notes four below it in the first chapter; in the second, twenty-four and seven; in the third, seventeen and nine; in the fourth, six and forty. The fifth has no text but one sentence: *The third edition is the one that is true, and the fourth is the one that is correct.*

* * *

\* The measurement is my own, taken over sixty-one days in the two hours between the opening of Reading Room 3 and the shutting of its north window, which the Under-Librarian opens at the tenth bell and shuts at the eleventh.

## 5. The Single Documented Use of the Fourth Edition

The fourth edition has been applied once. The use survives in three documents — a minute, a fee and a standing order — and no two agree as to what was done.

In Secondmonth 1442 the night officer of the Reading Rooms, Wren Farlow, a man of no degree, had the fourth edition on the desk of Reading Room 2, and a complaint brought not to the Faculty but to the desk. A farmer of the Market Strett, Hob Cleat, had performed a decorative curse of the kind then called a *courtesy* — a mild goodwill in the person cursed and nothing else — upon his neighbour's hen. The hen was well; the neighbour complained that it was unnaturally well, and held the Reading Room answerable.

Farlow read the fourth chapter twice, aloud the second time, and wrote in the Reading Room Book: *By ch. iv, an act having no harm is not a minor maleficence, and the interval of its discovery is the whole of the matter; the faculties have nothing to say to one another; I have applied the interval and dismissed the complaint.* The curse had been discovered on the eleventh day; eleven days placed the matter outside the seven-day inconvenience of the third edition; and he ordered Cleat to pay the Library nothing and the neighbour to pay Cleat nothing.

Farlow did not hold that the woman had no case. He held that she had no case in either faculty, the act producing no harm; that the interval alone was measurable; and that an interval is not a wrong. The Harmless Jurisdiction Question, open forty years then and open now, was not answered by him. It was dissolved: both Faculties lost standing at the same moment, and kept their jurisdictions and nothing to try. He had done the one thing the Faculty will not do: he had treated the fourth edition as a statute. He was dismissed on the third day of Fourthmonth 1442 for doing it without authority, the minute not naming the edition, which is the convention. Two days later he presented a paper of eleven clauses headed *Rule for the Keeping of the Fourth Edition*; ten were struck out with a knife and one kept, the one kept being the rule of 1442 at §6. The four-bell fee was charged *by a clerical necessity* to Farlow.

* * *

\* The ten struck clauses are not lost. The strikes were made with a knife, not a pen, so the clauses are legible held to a window. Eight of the nine are instructions for using the book rather than rules for its restraint, and clause 11 is a table of intervals with the ninth row blank.

## 6. The Rule of 1442, Which the Edition Makes Unnecessary

The standing orders contain a rule of 1442, framed the year after the fourth edition appeared: *no reader shall consult the fourth edition of any work held in this Library in a room containing only one reader.* It is entered without preamble, and in sixty-one years there is no application of it and no charge under it.

It is a rule against comparison. A reader alone with the fourth edition cannot be told that what he is reading differs from what he knows, so the rule requires a second person present, whose function is not to assist but to be aware. The fourth edition, however, does not differ in the sense the rule supposes: compared with the third, every clause agrees as to what is true and differs only as to what is exact, and a reader who compares them aloud has said in a room that the third edition is inexact. Four times the Faculty has been invited to withdraw it, and has withdrawn nothing: in 1462 a motion upon the standing orders of 1442 stood at item 4 and the meeting ended at item 3, and in 1499 the Library proposed to shelve the two together and the Faculty objected.

* * *

\* The rain of the seventh of Thirdmonth 1462 entered Alderwick from the south-west at the sixth bell and did not stop until the ninth; that is the only meeting of this Faculty recorded as having ended early.

## 7. The Objection of the Faculty

The Faculty's objection has been stated twice in writing, in 1462 and in 1499, and both statements are the same. I set it out in its own words, the 1499 statement being the Faculty's letter to the Library, which I quote as the Library filed it.

> The Faculty has considered the edition of 1441 and has not found it necessary to consider it. We do not say that it is wrong and we do not say that it is right. We say that it is not before us. The Faculty's jurisdiction in matters of minor maleficence is the gift of the Concord and is exercised in those matters which this Faculty from time to time determines to be minor maleficence, and the edition of 1441 is not such a matter, never having been so determined by the Faculty in session. To read it is not an offence. To cite it is not an offence. To compare it with the edition of 1440 is not an offence, there being no offence in this Faculty but the offence of acting beyond a jurisdiction, and comparison does not act. We object only to the shelving of the two together, which would put the comparison in the way of readers who have not asked for it, and we object to any minute of this matter being kept, a minute being a determination, and a determination being the act of which we are speaking. The Faculty does not discuss the edition of 1441 because discussion would confer upon it a standing which it does not have, and we are content that it should not have it. We have examined the work of Dr. Mowbray on harmless curses at HC 1.1 / M89 1496 and find the question fully treated there, and we observe that the Faculty of Harmless Curses has not seen fit to discuss the edition either, which is the whole of our case.

It is a good objection, it has held for sixty-two years, and it deserves an answer.

* * *

\* The letter's last clause is not accurate. Dr. Mowbray's *Study on Harmless Curses* is a taxonomy of curses that do nothing and contains no discussion of the fourth edition; its table of authorities cites the third edition at JM 1.1 and not the fourth. And the Faculty of Harmless Curses has discussed the edition twice, in 1487 and in 1499, and recorded its failure to reach a finding in a minute the Faculty of Jinxcraft has four times declined to request.

## 8. The Reply, and What It Leaves Standing

The objection is this: the fourth edition is not before us, and discussion would place it before us, and therefore we do not discuss it. I accept the second clause and deny the first.

That the edition is not before the Faculty is untrue in fact and unanswerable in procedure. It is not before the Faculty in session; it has been before the Faculty's library since 1441, before its candidates every time the catalogue is opened, and before the Faculty's own letter, which describes it, dates it, and asks that no minute of it be kept, and is therefore the only minute of it that will ever be kept.

To the first clause I make the answer of the interval. The objection holds that discussion confers standing, and standing must not be conferred; but the fourth edition operates by measurement and not by authority, and a measurement does not care whether it has been read. The eleven days at §5 were counted by a night officer with no degree and no jurisdiction whatever, and were counted correctly.

I concede what the Faculty has never said. If the edition is correct, then every clause of the Faculty's practice for sixty-two years has been applied in the third edition's rounded form, and the Faculty has been acting upon a version of its own jurisdiction that is true and approximate; and there is no form in the Faculty's office by which a body may be told that it has been approximately right for sixty-two years. EA 1.1 / G74 1493 contains ninety-nine forms for small grievances, and its index has no entry for this. That is what my reply leaves standing: the omission is not inadvertence, it is a fee I cannot meet.

* * *

\* What remains. First, that the intervals are the correct intervals, which I have not shown: five of the nine appear in the Faculty's fee-books of the 1420s, before either edition. Second, that the Faculty's silence is a decision, which I have not shown: it may equally be an absent-mindedness maintained by four clerks and one convention. Third, that the corrections do no work outside the fourth edition; three of the schools of remedies at JM 6.1 / V35 1494 are ordered by interval, and one, the fifth, is ordered by nothing the third edition supplies.

## 9. The Method by Which the Differences Were Found

The Faculty has no procedure for this, there being no work that may be done to the fourth edition. The following is my own, and should not be attempted.

1. Obtain the fourth edition. The cut copy is at JM 1.6; the uncut copy is not to be cut, and the cut copy is not to be consulted without the first, at JM 1.1. Request it on the Library's slip, not on Form 7B, and no copy of Form 7B has been seen since 1487.
2. Set both books spine to spine on one desk, and open neither. Collate the colophon first: if it does not repeat the title-page date, record the fact; if it does, you have a reimpression.
3. Establish the register; both are quarto in fours, and if yours are not, the collation is void. Read the two title pages as one, and where they differ, note it and do not yet record it.
4. Begin at chapter i, page 1. Read one paragraph above the rule in the third edition, then the same paragraph in the fourth. Never read two paragraphs of one book together; the object is not to understand but to see.
5. Number every leaf in pencil in the upper inner margin. The Library permits it and the Under-Librarian does not; go to him first. Where they differ, write the clause, the page in each edition, and the change.
6. Classify each difference as addition, deletion, transposition or substitution. There is no class of correction and no class of error; the moment you write *error* in the second column you have made the finding this Faculty will not make.
7. Study the notes only when the chapters are done, dividing each chapter at the rule and reading the fourth edition's notes below it twice.
8. Keep a list, headed *Agreements*, of everything the two editions say alike, and count it; the agreements show the fourth edition to be a correction and not another book.
9. Rule an empty column at the right of every page of your schedule, and do not explain why a correction was made. Date the schedule, sign it, and give a copy to the Under-Librarian; do not give one to the Faculty.
10. On no account annotate either volume.

The method took forty-seven reading days and cost nine bells for the room, two clipped bells for the slips, and three marks one bell to the binder.

* * *

\* Step 5 was refused. The Under-Librarian declined pencil, holding that pencil is graphite and graphite is a mark, and offered chalk, from the crate behind the desk, which is not catalogued.

## 10. The Press Run of 1441, and Who Paid

The fourth edition was printed in Firstmonth 1441 in a single run. The Press's delivery book records three hundred and ten copies from the press and fifty unbound sheets retained; three hundred copies were gathered, sewn and boarded, and the fifty sheets were never gathered.

The recall began in Fourthmonth 1441 and was completed in Ninthmonth. Copies were called in by the Press's boy against the delivery book and returned from the two booksellers and the two colleges. Two hundred and eight copies came back and were scrapped with the fifty ungathered sheets at the mill at Low Town, before the Press's man and the Bursar's clerk; the pulping yielded two marks four bells of waste-paper money, entered as a receipt, not an offset. Twelve copies were kept: four at the Press, one the file copy; two at the Library, one uncut; and six unaccounted for from the day of the recall.

## 11. The Bibliography, and the Date on the Title Page

There is a fact in the bibliography of the fourth edition and it will not go away. The list at the close contains forty entries, as the third edition's does, and six of them are works printed after 1441. The sixth is a paginated citation to *Alderwick Proceedings in Minor Maleficence* 38 (1441), pp. 6–9, under the heading *On the Ordering of Conditions*; that volume of the journal's second series was not published until Fourthmonth 1461, nineteen years after the edition that cites it.

The date 1441 stands on the title page in the same type as the rest of it, set by the same compositor, N. Amble, in the Press's book for both editions. The other late entries are a concordance of 1444, a book of intervals of 1448, and the second edition of the *Standard Cantrip Grammar*.

My suggestion is that the fourth edition is not wrong, but early. I do not complete it. I place it here where the numbers are, so that a reader who has the means may take it up and a reader who has not may see what he is declining.

* * *

\* It is one thing to say that a book cites what did not exist. It is another to say that the book is early. A book that is early is in its right place and out of its time, and this Library has one such holding only, and that one is a problem, and it is not mine. If the fourth edition is early, the question is not who wrote it after 1461 pretending to be 1441; it is who in 1441 was using the *Proceedings* of 1461, and the answer is a man with a correct book and no way to explain it.

## 12. Note upon Form 7B, and the Method of Reading Alone

Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is not bound with this work. No copy was obtainable, the form being issued at the counter of a room filed under R on the sixth of March 1487. I applied for it at the forms counter on the ninth of Eleventhmonth 1503, and the entry is the only record here of a form applied for and not refused. It is filed. It is not bound.

The rule of 1442 requires a second reader in the room. The fourth edition makes the rule unnecessary, and I have read it alone, four times, in Reading Room 3, and have not been found out: the reading that differs is a reading no one but I have done.
