## Abstract (as filed)

This volume is a field guide to timecrafting: acting upon an interval, regret as an instrument, and the etiquette of meeting oneself. The chapters are bound in the order of writing, which is neither the order of occurrence nor the order of reading; the latter is given at §0, which stands last because it is to be read first. It was deposited with the only completed *Form 7B, Declaration of Non-Interference with Time* the Library has seen, moved in 1486 to the fourth reading room's forms counter and filed with that room on the sixth of March 1487; the form is now missing twice, and the Library has not asked after it.

## §1 On Not Beginning

The first error of the craft is beginning. A timecrafter does not begin; he arrives at a beginning already in progress. The Faculty's first exercise is therefore attempted once, at the counter, under supervision.

*The first exercise: sitting with an interval for two bells.*

1. Present the interval licence (§7) at the counter and take the seat facing the north wall; the window seat is not to be used.
2. Set the licensed watch on the desk, face up, and record the hour. The watch runs backwards, which is correct.
3. Remove three leaves from the notebook before use. These are the leaves the practitioner would otherwise have written.
4. Sit with the interval, which is not a distance, for the first bell. Do nothing. No reading, no notes, no regret.
5. Do not consult the watch; it is issued so that the practitioner may decline to. One who consults it has sat for the first bell and not the second.
6. At the second bell, remain seated until the bell has finished and until the practitioner has stopped listening for it. This last is not timed, and is where the exercise is passed or failed.
7. File the notebook at the counter, the three leaves absent, and take a receipt. The receipt is not evidence. It is filed.

*Marking scheme, approved 1394.* First bell, 10. Second bell, 40. Notebook, three leaves absent, 20. Receipt filed, 15. Return of the watch unconsulted, 15. Pass, 60. The second-bell marks have not been awarded: no candidate has sat through it, and the Faculty does not wish to be discouraging. The column of awards is printed blank.

## §2 The Interval, and Why It Is Not a Distance

A distance is a length to be crossed, and what can be crossed can be hurried. The interval is not a length; it is a state to be endured, and the practitioner who hurries through it does not shorten it but arrives at the far end of himself, which is the condition treated at §3 and paid for at §5.

The survey of 1494 put the mean interval eleven minutes longer than the practitioner believes it to be, and the median belief at four minutes; the difference is entered as the oversight and is not refundable. An interval is measured from the last act of the practitioner to the first act of the event.

Nothing here is of use to a reader who has not read §0, and §0 is at the end, which is the difficulty of the book and not a defect in the binding.

See §12, which you have already read. (False of the binding; true of the craft.)

## §11 What to Do If You Have Already Read §12

Do not read §12 again in order to unread it; that is how the second edition happened. The case is recorded in full.

In November 1491 Mr. Joscelin Rathe, a copyist at the Tenterfields counter and a licensed practitioner of the fourth year, opened the volume where the ribbon lay and read §12 to the end first. §12 forbids the sending back of a book; it was written eleventh and is bound eighth.

To unread it he did four things. He returned the volume and reported himself, the correct first step, taken by nobody. He applied for an interval licence and sat two bells with the volume closed, at 1 mark 4 bells, and was marked 55 against a pass of 60. He read the volume from the last page to the first, a page at a time, over eleven days, and made no note. He burned the three leaves removed from his notebook at the start and filed the receipt for their absence, which the Faculty accepted as evidence that nothing had been written on them. The Faculty's note records that the fourth step is the only step that ever works, and that it does not always work.

He filed four apologies. The first, the 3rd of February 1492, apologised to the volume, and was accepted. The second, the 19th, apologised to the Faculty, which is not the person he was at the time, and was returned unopened. The third, the 2nd of March, was correct as to the person and defective as to the form, having been engrossed on the form for regret (see **EA 1.1**, §7); it was accepted on appeal, at a refiling fee of 4 clipped bells. The fourth, the 30th of March, apologised to the person he had been in November and enclosed a copy of the first; it was accepted. Three of the four were accepted.

The consequence is this copy. The Committee held the unreading to constitute a second edition, the first edition being the copy read wrongly, which is this edition, later. The printed Contents was not altered; the binder, who had followed it exactly, was instructed to bind the second in the order of writing, hence §7 precedes §2 in the hand and not in the Contents. Mr. Rathe's copy is the Library's; the Faculty's own apology, **EA 2.4**, is the only work here that is also an apology.

See §10, which you have already read. (True of the craft; false of the binding.)

## §4 The Two-Bell Rule

Never act within two bells of a foreknown event. Two bells is the least interval in which the event may be said to have chosen the practitioner rather than been chosen by him, and the distinction is the whole of the Faculty's ethics.

*Recorded failure.* On the 7th of September 1496 Miss Edda Woolfrey, an apprentice of the fourth year articled in Low Town, was given notice of a foreknown event at eleven and acted at twelve, having shortened the rule to one bell on the argument that the second bell would have found her asleep. She met the older party in the doorway and shook hands, which is the first prohibition of §3. She was not injured. The older party was. The Faculty fined her 2 marks 4 bells, suspended her licence for one year under Licence CR/W/31, and required the first exercise twice at her own fee. She reported success, and the report was entered, no form existing for the entering of a report believed by nobody.

Practitioners who shorten the rule report success. They are not asked to teach. The Faculty's summary of the shortened rule is printed on the back of the fee card: *one bell is not half of two bells; it is the whole of the other thing.*

See §1, which you have not yet read. (True of the craft; false of the binding.)

## §9 On the Reading of This Book

A reader who has reached this entry has already begun, and §1 was therefore read too late, which is the ordinary condition of the reader and not a fault in him. The volume is to be put down. If it cannot be put down, the reader has reached the condition treated at §10, which is bound after §8 and should have been read second. Readers in this condition are asked not to make notes in the margin; the margin is read by the next edition, and the Faculty's margin reader, appointed in 1499 at 2 bells the volume, has reported twice on this one, both reports brief and neither acted upon.

The Faculty distinguishes the reader who has not noticed the scrambling, which is the happiest and is not taught; the reader who has noticed and reads on, which is the practitioner; and the reader who has noticed and begun to correct the order, which is not the practitioner but the binder (§7).

See §5, which you have already read. (True of the craft; false of the binding.)

## §3 On Meeting Oneself, and the Etiquette Thereof

Do not shake hands. The older party decides who speaks first, who leaves, and which of the two is the older. Age is settled by the watch of the elder — the watch showing the earlier hour — and never by appearance, the apprentice being frequently the elder and never believing it.

Never ask which of the two is the older; the question is settled by the older party's leaving, which is the whole of the ceremony.

Do not compare notes. The notes are the same notes, and comparing them is how §5 comes to be written and how the second edition comes to exist.

Gifts are declined, except food, which is eaten on the spot, the interval not admitting of a parcel. In the register of meetings, opened in 1371, four hundred and six are entered: in three hundred and ninety-one the older party spoke first; in fifteen the younger spoke, which is not to be published.

See §10, which you have already read. (True of the craft; false of the binding.)

## §12 What Not to Send Back

1. The practitioner himself. What returns is not returned; it arrives, and the Faculty has no form for arrival.
2. A message requiring an answer. It will receive one, and the answer will be the message.
3. Money. Money returns, and returns spent. The interval charges no interest, the Faculty having lost jurisdiction over interest in 1404 to the Faculty of Domestic Enchantment.
4. Any book, and particularly this one. The second edition exists because the first was sent back (§11). The Library's copy is the first. This is the second. They are the same printing.
5. The small collar. See **DE 5.5**, whose author had the good sense not to send hers back.
6. A regret, once calibrated. A regret is not sent back; it is left, and found by the next occupant, which is why the Faculty licenses regret by the year (§5).

The list is not a prohibition. The Faculty cannot prohibit a sending; it can enter what returns, and it has entered every item here, item four most often, the readers of a field guide being practitioners.

## §5 Regret as Instrument

Regret is the only tool that works backwards and the only one that cannot be blunted, only calibrated by use. A regret too sharp cuts the interval. A regret too dull returns nothing and is called memory. The use of regret is licensed at 2 marks, waived for the bereaved; the waiver is claimed on the certificate of the Registrar of Deaths, reached by the stairs that are closed.

Calibration is performed against the Faculty's three reference regrets: the Aldermarket regret of 1493, the ninth-bell regret of 1402, and the Tenterfields regret of 1466, the last dull and retained because candidates calibrate high without it. A regret may be calibrated twice; one calibrated a third time is entered as an instrument, the distinction between a regret and an instrument being the ground of the Faculty's dispute with the Faculty of Divination.

Regrets are recorded by the hour of the event regretted. See **DP 12.8**, whose author would say that a forecast is a regret performed in advance; he is right, and it cost him his second impression.

See §10, which you have already read. (True of the craft; false of the binding.)

## §8 Weather in the Interval

Conditions in the interval are not weather, but they resemble it: a pressure, a light from the wrong side of the room, and the smell of a room the practitioner has not yet entered. The Faculty keeps a barometer for these conditions — the Yarrow pattern of 1471, on loan from the Faculty of Divination and never reclaimed — and has never had a reading from it, the needle preferring to hang.

The four conditions are entered in the field notebook against the hour, and are named in the standing orders as pressure, light, smell, and the fourth, which is not to be written down. A practitioner who records the fourth condition is charged at the rate for instruments (§5), that distinction being one the Faculty has declined to draw in print.

Practitioners were the only readers who did not complain of the misprint of 1466, which set Fair as Rain for a year. To them the forecast had become honest, and they said so in writing to **DP 12.8**, which has kept the letters. The printer was not charged, the Faculty holding that a press which misprints the weather is doing the interval's work and should be left to it.

## §6 On the Ordering of These Chapters

The chapters are in the order they were written: written as the author lived them, lived as he read them, read in the order printed in the Contents. The binder followed the Contents exactly; the author rearranged the manuscript afterwards, for the reasons given at §11, and did not tell him. The binder's note is short.

The accession was objected to. The objection is printed here because the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork requires its objections to be read aloud at the counter at which they are filed, and this counter is closed:

> **The Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork, by its Clerk of Appeals, to the depositor.** The volume is received and cannot be accessioned. By the Instrument of the Accessions of 1371 an accession is entered in the order in which it is received, and must be capable of being read in that order by the officer entering it. A volume whose reading order is printed at its end, whose binding follows a contents which is not that order, and whose cross-references are addressed to a reader who has not yet reached the entries referred to, cannot be entered, and the Faculty declines to enter it. The volume is not a thesis and cannot be examined; its deposit is the business of the form for non-interference, which cannot be obtained. The objection is filed, and is not a refusal: the Faculty has issued no refusals since 1491.

The reply was found in the volume and is undated, which the Registry has recorded as a date.

> The rule requires that the officer entering the volume be able to read it in the order in which it is received. The Faculty's officer has read it; the rule is satisfied, and the objection is a description of the volume and not an objection to it. The volume was accessioned twice because the first accession was of the first edition and the second of the second, and both are this volume. If the Faculty will enter the second entry as 1483.11(a), the matter is closed. It is not closed.

The endorsement is in the Clerk's hand: *Entered as 1483.11, twice. The objection is to stand in the file.*

## §10 On Ending, Which Is Done First

An ending is the first act of the craft, not the last. The practitioner settles how a thing ends and then declines to be present for the middle, which is the purpose of a field guide and the reason this one is short. This entry was written fifth, occurred seventh, and is bound twelfth, after §8; it should have been read second.

An ending may not be spoken aloud before the interval has closed. The Faculty fines the speaking at 3 bells and doubles it at a second speaking, which has happened once, at Bell Court in 1494, and produced the Faculty's only recorded apology to a speaker, the speaker having been correct.

Endings are filed at the counter in the order they are settled, not the order in which they occur, which is why the Registry keeps them in a drawer and not in a calendar. The drawer holds three hundred and nine cards and one blank card, which the Registry will not remove, the blank being the ending of a matter still before the Dean.
