## Abstract (as filed)

This treatise sets out the air of the Library's rooms, the draught of each and the officer objecting to it, argues that a room in which scholars work must be ventilated, and lists the five rooms with no windows. Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is missing from every thesis here, and is not bound with this volume, which is not a thesis.

## 1. What Ventilation Is, and What It Is Not

Ventilation is the changing of the air of a room on purpose: an act performed at a window at an hour, by an officer who writes the hour in a book. Readers breathe the air, lamps smoke into it, and the paper on the shelves takes from it and gives back to it.

Its purpose in a library is twofold: the health of readers, who cannot work in air they have already breathed, a close room giving headache by the third hour; and the survival of paper, which is damp, which moulds, and which is eaten at the edges when kept in damp that does not move.

Ventilation is not warmth: the Library has never heated these rooms. It is not a draught, though a draught is its evidence and sometimes its means. Nor is it a favour to the Faculty of Agricultural Curses & Pollen Pathology: it is a duty owed to the books, which cannot petition, and to readers, who mostly do not.

| Term | What it means in this volume |
|---|---|
| Air | The contents of a room, capable of being changed. |
| Draught | Air in motion through an opening, belonging to the building. |
| Ventilation | The deliberate changing of a room's air, recorded. |
| Close | Air that has been used and has not been changed. |
| Mould | What damp becomes when it is left, and the only argument that ends a dispute. |

## 3. The Rooms That Have No Windows, Which Are the True Scandal

Five rooms here have no window at all. They are the scandal of the building and are not discussed, because a room without a window cannot be ventilated and therefore cannot be disagreed about: a room with no opening has one party, and that party is the air.

| Room | Purpose | What is stored in it | What has happened there |
|---|---|---|---|
| The Strong Room, 31 ft × 12 ft | Custody of the plated and chained | Eleven chained volumes, the iron chest, the parcels of 1341 | Occupied eleven minutes at a time, a period the Porter never explained |
| Muniment Room 2, not in the plan of 1223 | Deeds, covenants, roof papers | The tithe rolls 1301–1402; the covenant of the north light | The Bursar's key and no lamp schedule; a hand in the dust on the chest |

| The Porter's store, south range | Brooms, coal, the hand-cart, spare irons | Everything the Library cannot account for | A window in every plan and none in the building; the Porter declines to discuss it |
| The passage behind the catalogue presses | Nothing; it cannot be swept | Nine presses against a wall; behind them, a window of 1462 | The Conservator reports damp quarterly and never the air; mould at the third press |

The last I count as a room, the plan giving it a window; a window behind a press exists for no lawful purpose.

## 4. The Draught in the North Corridor, Which Is Not Mine to Stop

The north corridor has had a draught since the sixth of March 1487, and it is not to be stopped. That is my difficulty: a finding about a room, made to an officer of rooms.

The corridor is the fourth reading room, filed under R in the reordering of the Registry with its counter, its lamp, one completed Form 7B, and the Library's only copy of *Form 7B for Dummies*. Every survey since 1487 has found a corridor of the same dimensions, and the draught in it; the draught was entered in the Registry's day-book on the eighth day as *settled*. The Faculty requires it stopped, and that requirement is addressed to the person who opens windows, which is I in every room here except the one it concerns.

I do not stop it; were it mine I would not stop it either, for it is that room's only air.

## 5. The Faculty's Objection, That a Window Is an Instrument of Delivery, and My Reply Point by Point

The Faculty resolved in session in 1498, eleven votes to four, that the Under-Librarian is *an instrument of delivery*, and has never served the resolution on anyone.

> The pollen curse is not aimed. It is raised above a flowering crop while the bees work, and thereafter carried on the air, indifferent to intention and distance. Where the Library opens a window on the north range in a catkin week, it operates the final fitting in the path of that curse, and a defect in the care of an instrument is the ordinary ground of a claim. The Faculty alleges no malice: only delivery, at six bells, by the officer whose duty it is to know the count.

1. **Of the instrument.** I accept the doctrine of Ashgrove-Vane at JM 1.1; the question is which hand. The Faculty's own instruction at AC 118.7 / T45 1487, §3, closes the Stack windows from Fourthmonth to Sixthmonth and is obeyed to the hour; nothing in it closes Room 2, and no member of that Faculty has entered gallery 3 since 1488.
2. **Of the count.** The Faculty posts and prepares the count at my counter; if it governs the hour, the Faculty governs the hour, and may name an earlier one. It has been asked four times and declined four times.
3. **Of the path.** The pollen crosses the Tenterfields, the river and two walls before reaching my window, and at that distance, in still air, settles in the yard.
4. **Of the claim.** No claim has been brought: four letters, each a copy of the last, and one non-suit before the Dean. A claim would require the Faculty to name an hour at which the window should have been shut and was not; that hour is the hour I have offered.
5. **Of the alternative.** I will close the window at any hour the Faculty names in writing. Its position is that no hour exists, which is a position about rooms, not air.

## 6. The Faculty's Letter of 1488, in Full, with My Reply

The first letter came in Firstmonth 1488 and is printed; its successors of 1489, 1491 and 1494 are this letter with the date and signature altered.

> **To Mr. Fenwick Oll, Under-Librarian of the Reading Rooms, from the Reader in Applied Pollen Pathology.**
>
> Sir — The Faculty has instructed me to write concerning the windows of Reading Room 2 and of the Stack, and to write once, that the matter may be settled by letter rather than by habit.
>
> The pollen curse is carried on air. It is raised above a flowering crop while bees work, and it does not choose where it goes; consequently every opening between the Tenterfields and a reader is an instrument of delivery, and the officer who opens it is, in respect of that reader, the hand that delivered it. The Faculty does not say that you intend harm: it says that you do not count, and that a man who opens a window on the north range in the catkin weeks without counting is a hazard of the order of a man who leaves a gate open on a road used by cattle.
>
> The Faculty therefore requires that the windows of Reading Room 2 and of the Stack be kept shut from the first of Fourthmonth to the last of Sixthmonth, and in the remainder of the year be opened only on the Faculty's written licence, hour by hour. It requires also that the casements be listed by number in the day-book and that no opening be made on a red day without a second signature. The requirement is printed at AC 118.7, and it stands. Should the Library find it inconvenient, the Faculty will receive its reasons in writing and will lay them before its next session. I am, Sir, your obedient servant, **H. Pell-Marrow, Reader in Applied Pollen Pathology.**

My reply is the reply, and is at the counter in fair copy:

*To the Reader in Applied Pollen Pathology: I have received your letter of the date above and laid it with its predecessors. The windows of Reading Room 2 are opened at six bells and closed at the hour your Faculty specified in 1487, both entries made in the day-book at the desk. The gallery 3 window is opened two hours each morning of the closed season by the ruling of 1499, against which the Faculty did not appeal. I do not open your Faculty's windows, having no keys to them. No alteration can be made in the hour, and none has been.*

Four letters answered with one reply is a form requiring nothing further.

## 7. The Mould of 1499, in Full: the Books, the Treatment, the Cost, and the Ruling That Followed

The third gallery of the Restricted Stack is thirty-eight feet by nine, with one grated window and cold stone on three sides, and had been damp in 1488 and again in 1489. It was found on the nineteenth of Fifthmonth by a reader at the window table, whose lamp guttered at the third hour, which she reported as a fault in the oil; it was not the oil, the air having gone close enough to starve the flame. The Porter's report contains the phrase *the shelf is alive at the fourth bay*.

The following were lost or damaged beyond the ordinary.

- *Alderwick Proceedings in Minor Thaumaturgy*, volumes 51 to 70 (1491–1498), the Library's run: volumes 51 to 62 swollen at the spine, the boards lifted and the sewing perished. Four condemned and eight treated, the treated eight remaining in the Stack with the bloom stopped.
- The *Herbal of the Nine Meadows* (1411), whose hand-coloured plate of the marsh gentian lost its lower third.
- *The Vellum Ledger of the Tenterfields* (1389), four leaves gripped at the fore-edge and parted, one losing nine words of a boundary description.
- Twelve quires of a thesis at bay 42, Mr. Aldous Frith, B.A.B., *On the Care of Decorative Curses in Humid Weather*.
- One shelf board, and the linen press-cloth, burnt in the yard by order of the Conservator.

Treatment took five weeks: dry-brushing out of doors under a linen screen, airing between leaves of unsized paper, and a weak spirit wash on the leather boards.

The account, laid before the Committee on the ninth of Sixthmonth 1499: treatment of eleven volumes, 3 marks 6 bells; rebinding of five, 3 marks 10 bells; three volumes bought, 1 mark 4 bells; the joiner, 12 bells; linen, spirit and paper, 8 bells 6 clipped bells. The total was **11 marks 4 bells 6 clipped bells**, of which the contingency met 7 marks, the Faculty of Domestic Enchantment 2, and I the remainder, **2 marks 4 bells 6 clipped bells**, stopped from my stipend in eleven instalments.

The Committee ruled on the eleventh of Sixthmonth 1499 that the gallery be ventilated in the closed season notwithstanding AC 118.7, the window standing open two hours each morning. The Faculty was notified and did not appeal. I have kept the ruling exactly: one hundred and ninety-four openings and closings in four seasons; the bloom has not returned at bay 40.

## 8. The Leak of 1491, and Who Paid for It

The lead flashing at the north light over the third gallery was repaired in Secondmonth 1491 for 1 mark 6 bells, out of the roof account. In Fifthmonth the rain came from the second to the ninth, six days with the wind in the north; the water entered at the foot of the flashing and was not found until the press-cloth at bay 41 was wet to the hand.

The losses: *The Aldermarket Book of Hours* (1462), eleven leaves cockled and the reds run, treated and not restored; a duplicate copy of the *Statutes of the Chapter* of 1274, the Foundation class keeping its own, stained through the fourth and fifth leaves to the loss of four words I cannot supply; and six quires of the thesis of Mr. Aldous Frith, the same candidate, losing its fourth chapter before the mould of 1499 could finish the argument.

The roof account paid the joiner 1 mark 6 bells; the contingency met the treatment of the Hours, 2 marks 8 bells; and Mr. Frith was granted a waiver of all fines for two years, 2 bells in value. The Bursar declined to charge the Dean and Chapter the balance of **1 mark 4 bells**, the Chapter having held the wall to stand entire since 1274, the covenant being silent as to its thickness. I appealed in writing, citing the surveyors' notes at FN 2.1 / — 1223, where the last measurement of the north wall, taken in 1484, gives two feet four inches and adds *of which two feet four inches is wall*.

That appeal is why this volume exists in the form it does. The 1 mark 4 bells was never recovered, and I have entered it as *unrecovered* and not as *lost*, a distinction the Registry declines to accept on a return.

## 9. The Method of Airing a Room in the Pollen Season, Numbered, Being the Method I Use

The following is not recommended to readers, who have no keys and should apply at the desk.

1. **Choose the hour before the count rises.** The Faculty posts its count at the eighth bell; work before it is posted, and copy it afterwards.
2. **Open the leeward opening only** — in the Stack the grate, in Room 2 the east sash — and hang a damp linen screen across it, a hand's breadth clear, to catch the coarse pollen; wring the screen into the yard, not into a basin. One opening makes a passage; four make a wind.
3. **Give the air a way out that is not the way in.** A room with one opening does not change its air; it breathes in and out of the same mouth. In gallery 3 the way out is the stair-head door, which stands open.
4. **Two hours in the closed season, twelve minutes in the depth of winter.** Do not open at a change of wind or in rain; I broke that rule twice, on the fourth of Sixthmonth 1497 and the eleventh of Fifthmonth 1502.
5. **Close at the hour you have named, and write the entry at the desk, in ink.** A closing made late is an opening made late.

A wool thread at the far end of the room, at the height of a reader's book, will lift an eighth of an inch when the air is changing; I report the state of the shelf monthly to the Conservator. The first three steps are the craft and the rest the defence.

The Standing Matters Committee ruled in 1489 that the windows are to be opened for ventilation and closed for the Faculty's convenience *at hours to be agreed*. None were agreed, and both parties have called the ruling a victory, which is the surest sign that the Committee settled nothing. The hours to be agreed are the hours I keep, posted at the counter; the Faculty may name different ones, and the agreement fails not for want of an offer but for want of an hour it can name. I offered again in 1494, in 1497 and in 1502.

## 11. Of the Readers Who Object, and What Their Objections Have Taught Me

Since 1487 I have kept, at the back of the day-book, a list of every reader who has asked me to shut a window: the date, the room, the seat, and what was said. There are forty-one entries. Nine asked in the second hour of the afternoon, at the east sash of Room 2, and eight of the nine were reading at the same table, four feet from the window. Eleven returned the following week and asked for the same seat. One asked on one day for the window shut and the room aired; I opened the window, and he moved two tables back.

## 12. Conclusion: That a Closed Window Is a Decision Made by One Person About Another Person's Air

A window is not a fitting, though the Bursar has ruled that it is one, and not a policy, though the Committee has ruled that too. It is a decision made by one person about another person's air, and no officer should make it twice in one week without writing down why. I have written down why since 1487, and now again in this reissue, which is longer than the first because it has the mould in it.

I am not an enemy of the Faculty of Agricultural Curses & Pollen Pathology, which has a real curse, a real crop and a real argument, and whose count I have posted at my counter for eleven years. I am not a friend of draughts, which have ruined two leaves of my day-book. I am an officer with a window, a book to write the hour in, and no wish to be the man who decided in silence. The north corridor is not mine to stop, and I have not stopped it; the third gallery is mine, and I have opened it one hundred and ninety-four times, and there is no mould at bay 40.
