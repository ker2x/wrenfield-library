## Abstract (as filed)

This history records the dispute of seventeen years between this Faculty and the Under-Librarian, Mr. Fenwick Oll, over the windows of Reading Room 2, which the Faculty requires shut from the first catkin week to the last oat and which he opens at six bells. It gives the record by year, the six rulings, the reply, the procedure, and the case that the window is the last fitting in the path of an air-carried curse. Form 7B is absent.

## 1. What the Window War Is

The window war is a dispute about what a window is.

This Faculty holds that the window of Reading Room 2 is an **instrument**: the last fitting in the path of a curse carried on air. The pollen curse is raised above a flowering crop while the bees work; it is not aimed, and it travels where the air goes. A room with an open window in the north range is the last length of that path, and the officer who lifts the latch at six bells completes the delivery. The Under-Librarian holds the window to be a **fitting**: furniture, to be opened for readers and books.

What is at stake is jurisdiction. If the window is a fitting, the Library opens it on the Library's hours and the standing instruction binds no one. If it is an instrument, the last hands in the path are the Library's, and this Faculty may regulate them. For the Faculty the stake is the harvest; for readers, the air of a wet room; for the books, damp.

Not in dispute: that pollen carries the curse, and that the Library must be aired.

## 2. The Room, the Wall and the Latch

Reading Room 2 is in the north range, forty-one feet by nineteen, with two windows in the north wall and a third in the plan. It has been this Faculty's by usage since 1421, the counters standing in the two bays, and in catkin weeks candidates take the seats at the window head.

The plan's third window is the room's quiet scandal. The plan sheets approved in 1223 show three openings in the north wall; the wall has two. The survey of 1484 found the north wall two feet four inches thick, of which two feet four inches is wall. The casements are iron, on the pattern latch then approved; the sill stands three feet one inch from the floor, the window head seven feet nine. The Faculty counts at the head, and it is at the head that the reader of 1495 sat.

## 5. The Letter of Reply, and the Annotation

Mr. Oll's reply is entered in the incident book under *Substance*, four times, verbatim and in full: 3 Fourthmonth 1497, 19 Fourthmonth 1499, 2 Fifthmonth 1501 and 11 Fourthmonth 1503. The Registry has declined to number the four entries, numbering being for documents that differ. It runs:

> Sirs — The windows of Reading Room 2 are opened at six bells. They are closed at the hour the Faculty has specified. On the third of Fourthmonth the hour specified was the fourth hour after noon; on the nineteenth of Fourthmonth 1499 it was dusk; on the second of Fifthmonth 1501 it was dusk; and yesterday it was dusk, and I closed at dusk, and the closing is entered in the book. The Faculty may specify an earlier hour, and I will close at it, and I have said so four times. I am directed by the Bursar's office to keep the fittings of this Library in repair and by no one to keep them shut. I am further directed by the Committee on Standing Matters to open the room for ventilation, at hours to be agreed, and no hour has ever been agreed with me in writing. The pollen count posted at the counter is prepared by the Faculty; it was posted at the desk from 1487 and at the window head from 1495, and the two do not agree, and I am not consulted upon either. If the Faculty will specify an hour in writing, at dawn if it wishes, I will close then, and the room will be closed from that hour, and the Faculty will have what it has asked for. I have asked four times for the hour in writing. I am, Sirs, your servant, F. Oll, Under-Librarian.

Annotation, adopted by the Faculty: the fourth clause concedes more than the Under-Librarian knows. He offers to close at any hour specified in writing, dawn included; he does not offer to keep the fitting shut. A fitting may be closed at dawn and opened again at six bells, and an instrument cannot be worked twice in a morning to two purposes. The Faculty has therefore never quoted the offer in a letter.

## 6. The Objection, and the Reply

In his own voice, at the counter, on 3 Fourthmonth 1497, reduced without addition:

> *The Faculty asks me to shut the room in the season when the river is up and the wall is wet. The plaster blooms from the last week of Ninthmonth, and the plates fox on the north edge, and the plates are the Faculty's. In eleven weeks of 1492 I entered ninety-three complaints: closeness sixty-one, smell nineteen, lamp smoke thirteen. The joiners found the north bars dry-rotted at the foot in 1496, and the Faculty's own carpenter signed it. No one has been made ill by the air of this room. If the Faculty wants the fitting shut, let it say so to the Bursar who calls it a fitting; I take my orders from the Bursar's office and from no one else.*

The reply is fourfold. First, no malice is alleged; what is alleged is operation. Second, the curse, raised above a flowering crop while the bees work, is carried on air, indifferent to intention, distance and the curser's plans, and the room in which a reader sits is the last length of its path. Third, a path has fittings: the casement, the light, the latch and the hand that lifts it. The doctrine is the University's: Ashgrove-Vane, *Principles*, 3rd ed. (the good one), at **JM 1.1**, holds a defect in the care of an instrument actionable though the instrument's action is innocent, the test being whether the operation lay in the operator's hand. Fourth, the test is met here and nowhere else: the third reading room's south window has never been complained of, the Reserve has none, and the fourth reading room's river window, in the plan of 1223 at **FN 2.1 / — 1223**, has never been applied about.

Two admissions in his own book are relied upon. At **DE 22.3 / O55 1502** he writes that the draught in the north range is not to be stopped, and gives thanks for it; a draught is the curse's carriage, and a man who will not stop it has taken the care of it. And at Appendix v he prints the four replies with the dates identical, as though identity were an argument. The Faculty does not ask that the window be bricked up; it asks to name the hour.

## 7. The Named Failure: the Reader at the Window, 1495

**Mr. Anselm Corby**, twenty-two, of Low Town, a second-year candidate reading for the B.A.B., had never had the pollen sickness. He took the seat at the window head on 2 Fourthmonth, his plates being due; the count that morning was seventy-one grains per cubic foot. The Under-Librarian opened both casements at six bells and again at noon; the Faculty's clerk remonstrated at the latch, the remonstrance is entered in the incident book, and the window stayed open.

Corby had been at the window three hours and forty minutes. At the quarter after noon he was taken: the eyes first, then the throat, then the breathing. He was attended by Dr. H. Pell-Marrow and by Dr. Ottoline Marchetti-Bell, who was in the room for the plate class and has since published a correction to the Faculty's remedy card. He was bled, permitted salted water, and kept out nine days, at a cost to the Faculty of one mark four bells in commons and two in the physician's fee.

The Faculty inspected on the fourth, the Under-Librarian present and objecting. It found the casement open, and the desk bottle reading fourteen grains per cubic foot at the hour the window-head bottle read sixty-seven — fifty-three grains apart, and the ground of the Faculty's refusal since to accept a desk count. The Under-Librarian answered that a slide set in the shade reads the shade; which is why the Faculty stopped taking it so.

The count of the openings followed on the eleventh: sixty-two openings entered for the year, of which the Faculty had been notified of six, and thirty-one closings, of none. On that count the Faculty sought a ruling that the fitting be kept shut from the first catkin week to the last oat, the hour specified by the Faculty, the closing entered by the officer who opened, and a bell for each unentered closing.

The claim was for eight marks: four of indemnity under the Faculty's standing rule, which falls on the Faculty, and four in fees, refused by the Bursar's office on 16 Sixthmonth 1495, an application being a view. The Faculty's concession stands: **the wheeled counter is moved from the window head to the free end of the table in catkin weeks**, by the Library's porters at the Faculty's charge, one bell the season. It has moved thirty-one times, and nobody counted it a concession until this history.

The ruling sought in 1495 was never given: the papers went to the Bursar, whose ruling of 1493 already answered the question the other way.

## 8. The Procedure: How the Faculty Measures at a Window

Set out as it is worked. A reader may attempt it and should not; the bottle is calibrated by this Faculty, and the Library has never accepted the calibration.

1. **Fix the hour.** Count at six bells, the eight-bell hour, noon, the fourth hour after noon, and dusk. The hours are the dial's, not the clock's: the Registry's clock has stood at forty-one minutes past two since 6 March 1487 and is not to be wound.
2. **Set the bottle.** The diagnosing bottle stands on the window head, on the sill, at the inner edge, mouth to the room and not the sky. A bottle facing the sky collects the pollen of the range and not of the room; the Faculty was caught at this in 1493 and glued a mark to the sill.
3. **Expose the slide.** A slide prepared with glycerine jelly, exposed one hour exactly, capped where it stands. Fair copies are to be kept off the sill, a slide having been spoiled in 1497 by a candidate's copy lying across it.
4. **Count the field.** Count in the ruled field of four hundred squares, take the mean of five fields, multiply by the factor on the card, which is 1.16. The factor was set in 1489, and has been asked for annually since 1490 and refused.
5. **Enter the count, the hour and the wind.** Enter grains per cubic foot, the hour by the dial, the wind by the vanes, and the state of the latch at the setting and the capping. A count entered without the state of the latch has been struck four times, once by the Faculty's own clerk.
6. **Do not correct the count.** The Faculty's two known flaws are that the slide under-reads in moving air by an estimated twelve parts in a hundred, and that in catkin weeks the desk count runs a third below the window-head count.

The exchange offered at §6: the Faculty will read its count at the desk as well as the head, at its own charge, from the first catkin week of 1505, provided the closing is entered by the officer who opened. It has not been accepted.

## 9. The Seminar, and the Attendance

The Faculty's seminar on the pollen curse has met in Reading Room 2 since 1498 and is the best-attended course in the University: one hundred and four enrolled against a room licensed for forty, the balance in the doorway and on the sill.

The Faculty records, without comment, that the seminar meets with the windows open; that no member of the Faculty has asked for them to be shut; and that the Under-Librarian attends at the back and takes notes, having attended thirty-eight of forty-one meetings. His notes are in his own hand; a member of the Faculty read them over his shoulder and reports them accurate.

The Faculty's answer, given to first-year candidates and to no one else, is that a lecturer may open a window in a room he is lecturing in, being in it an hour, and a reader may not be opened upon for a season. The distinction is untested. The Under-Librarian has asked in writing, twice, whether a lecture is not the delivery of a curse upon a hundred and four persons; the Faculty has not answered.

## 10. The Two Failures of This Faculty

**The attachment never made.** Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is required of every thesis and is not attached to this one. The Registry was notified at filing and again at this reissue. The Faculty's position is that a history of a dispute about windows does not interfere with time; it has been neither accepted nor refused, the form being unobtainable, the only completed Form 7B in the University having been filed with the fourth reading room in 1487. The Faculty has therefore attached a Declaration of its own devising, asserting that no clause of this history has been served, the author having counted the cost and declined it.

**The resolution never served.** On the ninth of Sixthmonth 1498 the Faculty in session resolved, eleven votes to four, that the Under-Librarian is *an instrument of delivery*. Two of the eleven have since asked to be recorded as voting against, and the minutes have not been corrected, correction being a matter for the mover, who was one of the two. The resolution has never been served on any person, and a finding served on no one is a finding the Faculty keeps; it is cited in the annual letters since 1498 in the form *the Faculty has determined*, which is accurate. This Faculty measures the height of the pollen and not the regularity of its own procedure: on the day the resolution was carried the count at the head was twelve grains per cubic foot.

## 11. The Deed, and the State of the Question

By the Tenterfields deed of gift, filed at **EA 4.3 / W55 1501**, the donor covenanted that no window be opened in the north range, that no bees be kept on the premises, and that the fourth reading room be kept in good order. The Library is in breach of all three, and the Faculty has adopted the deed. The covenant is the Faculty's best instrument and it does not use it, the bees clause being equally unperformed: the hives at the north apiary are this Faculty's own. The apiary has standing under the schedule of 1389, by which a single bee may bring a claim on its own account and has twice; counsel advises that a hearing would put the apiary in the same corridor as the Under-Librarian, and the advice has not been acted on.

The war is in its seventeenth year, no nearer a ruling than in 1489. The Faculty's position is unchanged: the window is an instrument, and the opening at six bells in a catkin week is the last act in the delivery of a curse. Mr. Oll's position is unchanged, and is stated in four identical replies, three quoted nowhere and all four in the incident book. The readers in the north bays go on taking their seats; the counter has moved thirty-one times; the north-corner books have foxed at the north edge.

Two things are recorded and not argued. The first is that the dispute has cost the University — in letters, fees, porters' hours, coats, sashes, slides and one moved counter — an estimated sixty-one marks four bells, of which eleven marks two bells fell to this Faculty and the rest to the Library, which has recorded none. The second is that the counts are rising: the last catkin week stood at fifty-two grains per cubic foot against twenty-two in the first year.
