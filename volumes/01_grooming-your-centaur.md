## Abstract (as filed)

This work describes the grooming of the centaur: what grooming is, the order of operations, the implements of each stage, and the questions to be asked before any stage. The definition, which the withdrawn impression left until last, stands at the front of this one, where the reader may refuse it before beginning. The third edition supersedes the second, a reprint of the first with a corrected title page. Form 7B (Declaration of Non-Interference with Time) was to have been filed with this essay; it is not present, and the Registry has been informed twice.

## I. What Grooming Is

Grooming is the keeping of an animal by a person who has been given leave, for the length of an hour, in an order that does not vary, with implements that are named, in a place where the animal can see what is coming; and it ends when the leave ends, whatever the coat looks like. The definition has four parts, and a groom who has mislaid one of them is not grooming.

1. **The leave**, which is the herd's to give, and to give again at the shoulder and again at the tail; see section II.
2. **The hour**, which is a unit and not a courtesy, and which is owed on to the next centaur in his turn; see section IV.
3. **The order**, which does not vary, and which is set out at section V.
4. **The place**, which must be one where the animal can see what is coming, and out of which he may walk at any point in the hour.

It follows that grooming is not four other things with which it is commonly confused. It is not a **service**, since a service is owed and the hour is given. It is not a **purchase**, notwithstanding the fees at section XIV. It is not an **inspection**, and a groom who counts hooves for the farrier has stopped grooming and begun working. And it is not a **compliment**: a centaur who is told he looks well has been told that the groom was looking, which he knew.

The order, the implements and the place are the Faculty's contribution; the leave is the herd's. A fourth edition would be shorter, would carry one plate of the unpicking and none of the firm hand; the author has written it in her head and will not print it, the third edition being the one her grandfather's plates are in.

## II. The Approach, and the Four Refusals

The first edition, printed in 1447 by my grandfather **Emeric Voss**, assumed a centaur would welcome a groom; he never put the question to one, nor, the records show, spoke to one.

*May I* is not a formality before grooming but its first part, and the only part a groom may not delegate; a centaur who said yes at dawn has not said yes at noon.

| reply | meaning | correct response |
|---|---|---|
| a step back | not today | withdraw, do not ask again today |
| a step back, with a look | not ever | withdraw, and do not raise it again |
| a step forward | the question was unnecessary and mildly insulting | proceed, and be brief |
| a sigh | proceed, but do not enjoy it visibly | proceed |

The four have names in the registers: a step back is **Ferrand's refusal**; a step back with a look is **Bell's**, last given on 3 May 1494 to a visiting Tenterfields under-groom; a step forward is **Osric's**, impatient of ceremony; the sigh is **Tallow's**, who permits the whole operation if nobody remarks on it.

The approach has rules. Come up on the near side. Let the centaur see the implement before it is used, every time. Speak to the centaur, not about him, and never to the owner over his back, since the centaur is standing in that conversation and hears every word; the Nine Meadows herd-book ranks being discussed over as the fourth yard offence, the first three involving injury.

## III. Consent, the Yard, and Who May Be in It

Consent is a permission, not a contract: to a person, for a thing, at a place, at a time. It is not transferable, not inheritable, and does not survive the giver leaving the yard; a groom may not send an assistant in his place, nor his brushes. It is bounded by ground as well as hour: agreement at the trough is not agreement at the fence.

The Nine Meadows list of 1500 admits the trough, the mounting block, the stone shelf on the wall, the oak hoof-stand, one foot four inches, off side only, the crutch rail cut at Michaelmas 1498, the water butt, covered since the fever of 1496, and the gate, which is shut. It does not name the block's predecessor, destroyed on 14 June 1499 by a hoof. Consent given under an open gate is not consent but patience; the Nine Meadows shut its gate in 1492.

Agreement at the trough is not agreement at the fence: **Quill of the Bramblegate water-meadow** has refused to be groomed anywhere but the far bank of the ford for eleven years, and the herd treats the refusal as final.

## IV. On the Hour

The coat is not the point. Of thirty-one centaurs asked what they took from grooming, thirty said **the hour**: an hour in which nothing is required of either party, and a groom who finishes early has taken something. The thirty-first, **Halvard Nine**, twenty-nine, of the north water-meadow, said a clean coat and that a brush is not a philosophy; his answer stands unaltered in the table at section XI.

An hour is a unit, not a courtesy. A centaur who has been groomed owes the hour, stands for the next centaur's grooming in his turn, and the debt passes without a mark changing hands.

## V. The Coat, the Oils, and the Order of Operations

The order is fixed: curry comb, dandy brush, body brush, and, on request only, the finished brush, which is the centaur's to begin. Work from the near side, in daylight, never in a wind that could lift a hair into an eye.

Oils are controversial: the Nine Meadows blend, linseed cut with oil of bay, is acceptable on the quarters and offensive on the face. Offer it on the flat of the hand and let it be declined; warm it in the hand, not over a fire.

| implement | catalogue mark | consent required | common error |
|---|---|---|---|
| curry comb, iron, four-tooth | BH 12.4/A | no | working against the coat |
| dandy brush, stiff | BH 12.4/B | no | using it on the face |
| body brush, soft | BH 12.4/C | no | hurrying |
| hoof pick, boxwood handle | BH 12.4/D | yes, each hoof | heel to toe reversed |
| mane comb, horn, 41 teeth | BH 12.4/E | yes | taking the whole mane at once |
| sponge, sea, quartered | BH 12.4/F | yes | the eyes |
| tail band, plaited leather | BH 12.4/H | yes, and separately | fastening it while the tail is held |
| sweat scraper, brass | BH 12.4/L | no, after work only | scraping a dry coat |
| the soft brush | the centaur's own | yes | assuming it is yours |
| wisp of straw | made in the yard | no | making it too tightly |
| the groom's hands | not catalogued | always | haste |

The sequence itself does not vary, and a groom who has varied it should be able to say why.

1. Present yourself at the near shoulder, inside the animal's sight, and be looked at.
2. Say aloud what you intend to do, and wait for the answer.
3. Curry, with the lie of the coat, near side first, the comb struck out against the wall every third stroke.
4. Offer the near hoof. If it is given, pick from heel to toe.
5. Offer the off hoof. The same order, the same count, and no remark upon the difference.
6. Dandy brush, near side then off, brushing the comb out away from the animal and never towards the face.
7. Offer the oil on the flat of the hand, and let it be declined without comment.
8. Body brush, in daylight, from the withers down and never against the hair.
9. Ask before the mane. Plait loose if asked; if it is refused, unpick your own work, slowly, and do not hand it over.
10. Say what you intend with the tail, wait a count of ten, and groom only the ground the tail has been moved to.
11. The finished brush, if it is asked for. If it is not asked for, step back and stand.
12. Wash your hands where you can be seen, and leave the hour at the hour's end.

## VI. On the Animal's Own Tools, and the Care of the Groom

Three things in the yard belong to the centaur, not the Faculty, and the second edition's inventory omitted all three, an omission raised correctly at the fence on 9 April 1500. The soft brush is the centaur's own and no groom may buy it, a bought soft brush being a claim. The mane cloth, of linen, is kept by the herd and washed at the herd's trough. The tail band, plaited leather, is made by the wearer or a herd-mate, cut to a length particular to the wearer.

The nails are short and filed, the hands washed before the work, the voice low; a frightened groom should say so at the fence, and not in the yard, since fear is legible from the near side at forty feet.

## VII. The Hooves, and the Farrier's Jurisdiction

The hoof is picked from heel to toe, unless the centaur says the reverse, and then the groom says nothing.

The farrier's jurisdiction over the hoof is older than the Faculty's and disputed every Michaelmas; the farrier is a guest and must be introduced as one. The Worshipful Company of Farriers answered through **Master Halvard Crane**, Shoe-Master of the Aldermarket Forge and Reader in Horn since 1471:

> The hoof is horn, and horn is my trade; I was bound seven years to it, and have shod under two kings of the Nine Meadows. Your Faculty gives young persons a paper for naming the parts of a hoof in a warm room; I have never seen a paper hold a nail. When your groom takes my pick to a hoof I have dressed, he lifts the shoe, and that shoe was fitted on that day by a man who wrote the date on a slate. Now the foot is loose, the centaur is lame, your paper is not lame, and I am asked to attend. The hoof is the farrier's business, before your Faculty existed and before the Company's charter of 1402.

The author does not dispute the jurisdiction over horn: the hoof is picked, not shod, no nail set. What is disputed is the foot, attached to an animal standing in a yard and holding views; the question is not who owns the hoof but who may lift it, and the answer is the centaur. A farrier arriving without a word is within his rights under the charter of 1402, and toleration is not consent.

The dispute was heard at the Michaelmas sitting of the Court of the Nine Cloisters on 29 September 1498, which held that a certificate is not a licence.

## VIII. The Tail, and the Incident of 1499

The tail is a boundary: to touch it is to make a claim. The procedure: state the intention aloud; wait a full count of ten; let the tail be moved by its owner, and groom only the ground it has been moved to. If it is not moved, the answer was no.

The second edition's version of the incident omitted the names and was worse than useless.

On the morning of 14 June 1499, at the Nine Meadows yard, **Cael of the Nine Meadows**, a four-year-old of that herd, presented lame on the off hind: a thorn of blackthorn, two inches and a quarter, taken on the water-meadow overnight. The groom was **Osric Tallow**, journeyman, then thirty-one, bound in the Aldermarket, named with his consent and against his preference, who had taken from the first edition a method he called the firm hand, taught at plate 14 as a hold at the shoulder and extended by him to the tail.

Cael set the off hind down twice and shifted his weight. Tallow took the tail. What follows is taken from the depositions laid before the Master of the Yard on 18 June 1499 and from Cael's account, given at the herd's hearing on 21 June before four of the herd and the author. Cael said, and the yard heard him, *not that*. Tallow held. Cael kicked once with the near fore, struck Tallow below the collar-bone and again on the forearm; Tallow went down, his head striking the mounting block. The collar-bone was broken in the middle third, and the left forearm above the wrist. He could not stand for eleven minutes, nor unaided for the rest of the day. Cael stood over him and did not let anyone nearer, telling the yard's boy to set the water at the wall.

Asked at his own hearing why he stayed, Cael's account, read back to him on 23 June, is that a centaur who leaves a man on the ground leaves men on the ground. *I did not stay for him*, he said. *I stayed because I intend to be here.*

The master of the yard entered the matter on 18 June; the herd's hearing fined Cael two bells for the mending of the mounting block, paid on 24 June and complained of yearly since. The yard entered the incident against the first edition instead and withdrew it from the yard shelf, where it had stood since 1447, and has not returned it. Tallow returned to work on 2 August 1499, instructed never to take the tail again.

Cael stands at that fence to this day, and the greeting between him and Tallow took until Michaelmas 1500 to become ordinary.

## IX. The Mane, and the Plait Language

In the Nine Meadows herds the count is not decorative:

- **three plaits** — ordinary dignity; the wearer is available for ordinary conversation;
- **five plaits** — the summer gathering; the wearer will be away, and will say so;
- **seven plaits** — the wearer is not to be approached today, and the seventh plait means the groom should already have known that.

The herd permitted the following on the condition, made at the fence on 9 April 1500, that it not be taught to any person under fourteen years of age.

| plaits | ribbon | knot | reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| three | none | — | ordinary dignity; conversation is welcome |
| three | grey, faded | single | in mourning for a herd-mate; speak gently and briefly |
| four | none | — | the foal is with her; do not use the shoulder brush |
| five | reed-green | double | the summer gathering; the wearer will be absent a fortnight |
| five | reed-green, both ends cut | double | the gathering is to be disputed; the wearer will return early |
| seven | red | single | the wearer is not to be approached today |
| seven | red, with a strand of tail | single, crossed | the seventh plait's meaning, and the wearer's apology for it |
| nine | none | — | the wearer has been asked to stand for the herd at a hearing |

Plaiting is done loose; a tight plait is a claim of ownership. From Martlemas to Candlemas the herd does not plait.

## X. Withdrawal of Consent Mid-Braid, and an Apology for the First Edition

Consent may be withdrawn at any point, including mid-plait. The groom stops, holds the plait, and waits. If the plait is to be undone, the groom unpicks it himself, slowly, and does not hand it over. The unpicking is the apology. On 2 August 1500 the author unpicked a nine-plait that had been in six hours for a postponed hearing; it took forty minutes, and the centaur stood for all of it.

Plates 14 to 17 of my grandfather's first edition illustrate a method he called *the firm hand*, reproduced here unaltered, the subject having annotated them personally. The full captions stand at Appendix A.

| plate | first-edition caption | annotation, in the subject's hand |

| 16 | The Firm Hand at the Flank | *"This is the plate."* |

The author has asked the girl, who states for the record that she is Ariadne Voss, is not a girl, and wrote the third edition.

Plates 14 to 17 were drawn from life by an engraver named **Willem Ashe**, paid nine bells and a supper for the four, refused the shoulder twice, and drew it from the far side of the fence; Ashe died in 1451. The first edition was sold bound with *The Care of the Horse-Beast*, and the two are still shelved together at BH 12.4.

## XII. Other Cases: the Mud Season, the Foals, and the Old

In the mud season, from the first week of November to the turn of the year, the sequence at section V is not followed; the herd permits the legs to be washed at the trough and dried with straw.

Foals are not groomed; a foal may be touched by a seen hand, and by no implement catalogued above, the catalogue's youngest acceptance being four years. The author has touched four foals in three years and been refused twice.

The old are groomed differently: Marrow, thirty-eight, the oldest centaur interviewed, is groomed standing at the trough in the morning. Asked what grooming is for, he said the hands, warm, in winter; asked about 1479, he refused.

## XIII. A Letter from Bartholomew of the Nine Meadows, on the Third Edition

The foreword is Bartholomew's, at the front of the printed volume. The letter below was written to the author on 3 November 1500, after his chapter-by-chapter reading, and is printed with his consent and against his advice.

> Ariadne Voss, at the yard. You have asked me for a letter. The apology is good. I do not accept it. An apology is not a hedge; it is a gate, and you have built it well and it opens, and the field on the other side of it is still burnt. I have given the book marks out of nine. The asking-first has nine. The hour has nine. The coat has seven, because your grandfather's name is in it twice and mine once, and a book of grooming should carry the groom's name and not the herd's. The tail has four, because you have told the story of the boy's arm truly, and put my name on the page as though the herd had agreed to be evidence. We did not agree. I agreed. The plates have two, for the printing, not the drawing. One thing more. You have asked the girl to state that she is not a girl. Do not do that to her again. Let her be the author, and let the herd decide what she is, in our own time and to her face.

The author intends to answer the letter and has not.

## XIV. Fees, and Two Named Failures

The fees are the Faculty's, not the herd's; none has ever been charged to a centaur. The schedule was filed on 30 September 1500.

| matter | fee or fine | payable to | recorded |
|---|---|---|---|
| certificate of attendance, grooming | 6 clipped bells | Faculty | on issue |
| loss of a catalogue implement | 3 bells | Faculty | at issue of the docket |
| oil of the Nine Meadows blend, quarter-cask | 1 mark 4 bells | the herd's fund, via the yard | on delivery |
| remedy of a hoof by the Company | 2 marks | the Company | Michaelmas, in arrears |
| fine for being discussed over | 1 bell | the yard's fund | 1493, the fourth yard offence |
| mend of the mounting block | 2 bells | the yard's fund | entered 18 June 1499, paid 24 June |
| going into the field with a comb | 4 bells 6 clipped bells | the herd | 1496, and again 1500 |
| keeping the hour past the hour | nothing | — | no fee lies, and the herd has twice declined to invent one |

Two failures are named in this edition and both are the author's.

The first is **the Tallow hand**, entered 18 June 1499: Osric Tallow took the tail of Cael of the Nine Meadows, who had said *not that*; the consequence was a broken collar-bone, a broken forearm and eleven minutes on the ground. The cost fell: the mending of the block, two bells; a journeyman's idle weeks, one mark four bells; and four bells to the Company for a farrier's attendance. The first edition was withdrawn from the yard shelf.

The second is **the Michaelmas attendance**, entered 29 September 1500. The author went into the Aldermarket yards with a certificate of the Faculty's own issue, and was asked to hold a horse's fore while a shoeing-smith drew a nail. The fore was Cael's; her fee for the day was four bells six clipped bells, payable to the herd, paid on 2 October. A certificate is not a licence, as the Court held in 1498.
