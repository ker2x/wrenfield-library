# The Diary of Bartholomew of the Nine Meadows
### His reading of the third edition of *How to Groom Your Centaur*, marked out of ten

**Call number:** MS 5.1 / B37 1501
**Hand:** Bartholomew of the Nine Meadows's own; a fair copy from rough notes, which he says are burnt
**Class:** MS — Manuscripts & Personal Papers
**Dates of entries:** 11 February – 12 June 1501, being the weeks of the reading; no gap, though the middle three weeks of April are one line a day
**Provenance:** Brought to the counter by the diarist himself with a letter saying only that the book had been sent to him twice and read twice; the letter is lost
**Collation:** 22 leaves, written on the recto only; watermark a horse-bell
**Condition:** a hoof-print in the dust of the last leaf, which the Conservator measures and declines to discuss

## The Library's Note

This is a diary: the notes he kept through one season while reading the third edition of *How to Groom Your Centaur* a chapter at a time, with a mark out of ten against each chapter and a reason against each mark. He keeps the copy fair, he says, so that a reader may rely on the wording and not the substance. Nothing has been corrected, and the marks stand as he gave them, lower than the Faculty's.

He writes: *I keep this copy fair because a mark without a reason is a slander, and a reason without a mark is a review.*

## The reading

**the 11th of February.** The book came up from the city with a bill for carriage of six clipped bells, which I paid, the herd not having asked for it. It is the third edition. The first was printed in the year I was foaled and written about us without us; this one has my foreword in it and her name on the title page with mine beneath, which is the wrong way round and she knows it.

**the 14th.** Snow on the low meadow and the water butt iced to the second hoop. Chapter the first, on the approach. Seven. A centaur is approached from the left front and not from behind, the reason given being the field of vision, which is correct; the book is correct about eyes. A mark is withheld for the sentence beginning "a centaur likes to be told", which is what the first edition was made of.

**the 21st.** Chapter the second, on the coat and the comb. Eight. The comb before the brush and never against the hair is right, and she says why, which is better than being right. She gives the yard offence to the man who reaches without asking, and the herd's rule is the same rule.

**the 28th.** Cael is nine this year and wants her mane braided for the spring meet, and has asked three times whether nine is old enough. The question is not the age but the asking. She has read the fourth chapter twice and marked nothing, being polite.

**the 3rd of March.** Chapter the third, on the hoof oil. Eight. She is right that the oil is warmed and right that it is not to be scented, and she says the hooves are picked out before the oil and not after, which is right, and she says why, which is better. Osric's colt stood for the warm oil, which at two years is no testimony to the book.

**the 8th.** Chapter the fourth, on the mane and the braid. Five. The braid is a courtesy and not a discipline, and Plate XII figures a braid that would pull at the crest. She asks the reader to ask first, which I allow, and then the plate asks him to hurry. A rule and a plate are two hands, and she has used both.

**the 12th.** Old Bell will not be groomed before the tenth bell, and has not been since her year of the cough; I have entered it as a custom and not an illness. Tallow was groomed in the morning and owed the hour, and stood for Cael's colt in the afternoon, and the debt passed with no mark changing hands.

**the 22nd of March.** Ferrand the second was brought up by a visitor who had read the book and brought his own comb, as the book tells him to. Ferrand went back a step and looked, which is the refusal the book calls Bell's; the visitor asked again, which is one of the four errors, and I put him over the fence myself and not roughly.

**the 29th.** Rain, and the Yarrow up. Chapter the fifth, on asking. Nine. This is the chapter the first edition did not have. Consent is asked for the braid, again if it is to be tight, again if it is to be kept overnight, and a centaur may withdraw it in the middle and the braid comes out. The withdrawal is given in three sentences, which is two more than a girl usually gives it. Nine and not ten, because she calls the withdrawal a right and then writes as if it were a favour done her.

**the 2nd of April.** The first foal in the night, a filly, and up by the fourth bell. The farrier came at eleven and did the four mares, and charged three bells and a clipped bell for the colt's near fore, which had a crack in it that he did not explain. I held the foot, which is the farrier's work and not the groom's; the book has a rule against it, and the book is right.

**the 6th.** Chapter the sixth, on the apology for the first edition. Nine. She apologises for her grandfather, who never spoke to one of us and said we would welcome a groom; she does it in nine lines and does not ask to be thanked for it. I will say here what I have said to her face: I accept the apology and I do not remit the thing apologised for, the two being different acts, and a man may be forgiven and the sentence stand.

**the 9th.** Chapter the seventh, on the plates. Seven. Eight of the twenty-two are her grandfather's re-cut, four plainly so, and she says which, which is honest, and then says a plate is a shorthand and needs no leave. A plate of a hand on a shoulder is a hand on a shoulder. Cael looked at Plate XII a long while and said the mare in it looked pleased; I said the mare in it looked drawn.

**the 14th.** Her letter came, with the second foal's birth noted in it, which I had not sent her. I copy it entire, having returned the original.

> **To Bartholomew of the Nine Meadows, at the Nine Meadows, from A. Voss, at Alderwick, the 9th of April 1501.**
>
> Sir. The third edition goes to the press within the month and I am instructed by the Faculty to send you the sheets of the chapters on the tail and the hour, which are enclosed and which you have not seen. I have kept my grandfather's order of plates and changed his text where you told me to and nowhere else. I would be grateful for your marks on the two chapters, and I would be grateful to have the marks in writing, since the Faculty has asked me twice for the authority on which I say what you think. If the seventh chapter is wrong in the matter of the plates I would rather print it wrong and be told than print it right and owe the correction to a reviewer. I remain, sir, your servant in the matter of the book and not otherwise, A. Voss.

**the 21st of April.** Chapter the eighth, on the tail. Five. The tail is banded with the left hand and asked for with the mouth; she has the asking right and the holding wrong. The book would have the tail held while the band is fastened, and no centaur here over the age of eight will stand for it. Tallow's words, when I read her the page over the gate: *Ask me twice and I will let you do it. Hold me once and you have bought a lesson, and I am not the one who pays for it.* I have no answer, and the book has none either.

**the 2nd of May.** Chapter the ninth, on the hour. Ten. She says the hour is a unit and not a courtesy, that the groom owns nothing of it, and that a groom who finishes early has taken something out of the yard, and gives the count of thirty-one asked and thirty who answered *the hour*. I did not give her that count. Cael gave it her and I do not know when, and I have written to ask.

**the 9th.** The farrier came, and I record the failure and its cost as it was. He had the colt's near fore up and I had the near hind, and the colt shifted and came down on my off fore with the hoof-ball of it on the stone, and split the ball to the quick; my own oil went in cold, I having the can in the same hand as the halter. The book's order is oil after the picking out, and never over a split. I have to my name a horse-doctor's visit at four bells, a farrier's second call at a bell and six clipped bells, eleven days off the near side and the foal-meet missed, and the whole of it written in the yard-book, which is the cost.

**the 14th.** Chapter the tenth, on who owns the brushes. Six. She is right that the soft brush is the centaur's own, and I had not thought of it and have said so. Then she spends two pages on the marks a groom may make on the herd's own implements, which is a matter for the herd and not the Faculty. I have written so in the margin of my copy, and the margin is not printed.

**the 19th.** Cael asked at last for the tail, in the form the book gives, and asked twice, and it was done and stood; she paid the hour back to Osric's colt by standing with him through the comb, which he does not like. The book had a part in it, and I have written to tell her so, and told her the part.

**the 28th.** Chapter the eleventh, on what is not to be done. Ten. The fourth of its four rules is that the beast is not to be spoken of in the third person while he is standing there. It is the best page in the book and it is one line long. I would have it on the yard gate, and her name off the title page, and those two wishes are kept in the same place.

**the 4th of June.** Chapter the twelfth, and last, on the season's end. Four. It is three pages of counsel to the groom to be patient in the winter, and no page of counsel to the Faculty to be patient with the herd; the plate of the empty yard is drawn from the grandfather's block and shows a trough pulled down in the year of the fever. I read the four rules of the eleventh chapter again after it, to end on the better page.

**the 12th of June.** The fair copy is finished and the marks are these, with the sum as I have made it.

### The marks, as given

|---|---|---|---|
| i | the approach | 7 | right about the eyes; wrong about what we like told |
| ii | the coat and the comb | 8 | right, and says why |
| iii | the hoof oil | 8 | warm, unscented, picked out first, in that order |
| iv | the mane and the braid | 5 | a rule in the text and hurry in the plate |
| v | asking | 9 | the withdrawal in three sentences, and called a favour |
| vi | the apology | 9 | nine lines, no thanks asked |
| vii | the plates | 7 | honest about the four, then a plate needs no leave |
| viii | the tail | 5 | asking right, holding wrong, Tallow's answer unprinted |
| ix | the hour | 10 | the hour is a unit; she did not write the count |
| x | the brushes | 6 | the soft brush is ours; the marks on it are not hers |
| xi | what is not to be done | 10 | one line, and it is the whole book |
| xii | the season's end | 4 | patience for the groom, none for the Faculty |

Total, eighty-four of one hundred and twenty. It is better than the first edition, and the first edition was written about me without me, which is a low bar and not the one I am marking against. She has asked whether she may come to the Nine Meadows and read the fourth edition to me as she writes it, and I have said yes, and that the third person stays out.

I wrote the last of this standing at the wall, the fair copy being on the stone shelf and the ink being low, and I rested my off fore on the leaf to hold it flat while the ink took, and the shelf was dusty. That is the print on the leaf and it is mine, and it will not come out with a feather, and I have told them not to.

## Apparatus

*Under-Librarian's stamp, in violet:* The fourth reading room adjoins the corridor and is not suitable for large parties, and the crates are not to be sat upon. Hoof-prints are to be reported, not dusted.

*Conservator's note, 1504:* The hoof-print is a fore-hoof and not from a horse, the shoe being absent and the frog the wrong shape. It is recorded to the eighth of an inch.

*Pencil note, in the diarist's own hand, later:* On re-reading, I think the sixth chapter is worth ten and the seventh seven, an apology being a courtesy and a courtesy a lower thing than an ask. The total would then be eighty-four as before, which is a coincidence and not a correction. Cael has asked once more since, about the tail, and I said yes; she says the empty yard is a good plate, and I have not told her why it is not.

*Errata slip, pasted, in a printer's hand:* In the third edition, chapter the ninth, for "thirty-one centaurs asked" read "thirty-two"; one answer was struck out by the author as not to the purpose, the answer being that a brush is a brush. The count stands at thirty.
