## Abstract (as filed)

This thesis sets out a method for the design of load-bearing runes in masonry, treating the carved character as a member in compression and not an ornament. Ratings are given in stone-weight against span and section, six failure modes are named with their signs and intervals, the Arch of the Nine Cloisters is examined in the light of its own complaint and of the nine days of 1397, and the four objections of the Bell Court Indemnity Office are answered in turn. A procedure is given, and a stair is offered as a warning.

## 1. Load Ratings, and the Rune as a Member

A rune set into a voussoir does not support a load. It refuses, in a well-ordered and repeatable manner, to allow the load to do anything else, and designing with runes is the discipline of arranging refusals. Four consequences follow.

1. A rune is rated in compression only. Tension is declined, and a declined load is a falling load. The three runes cut to hold a stone up are in the Guild's cellar.
2. The refusal passes through the stone touching the rune. The rune is never the weak member; the stone is.
3. The rating belongs to the *reading*, not the carving. A rune read by a second mason in a hurry is a different rune.
4. A section is rated as one member. Two runes in one course divide the load and then disagree about the remainder.

A stone-weight is the load a Guild journeyman will carry up forty feet of ladder without swearing; the definition is the Guild's.

## 2. The Failure Modes

| Mode | First sign | Interval to failure | Remedy | Indemnified |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spalling | flakes on the cheeks of the cut; grit on the impost | 4–11 years from first flake | re-dress the cheeks, renew the bed | yes, at 9 bells the year |
| Rune fatigue | refusal weakens one part in 900 per settling | 60–140 years | re-cut in a fresh face, or relieve | yes, with a reader warranty |
| Semantic creep | the section gives a note it did not give | 1 reading to 300 years | read aloud against the section set; re-cut | no; §8, objection the first |
| Brief reinterpretation | the member performs something defensible | immediate, once | remove and re-cut | no; §8, objection the second |
| Bed shear | a hair line at the joint, running with the course | 1–3 seasons | shore; do not strike the rune | yes, if shored before the second season |
| Inverted cut | the section moves where it was not asked to move | within the hour | clear the ground beneath | no; a fine lies instead |

Fatigue does not recover: a section relieved for a decade and reloaded fails at the interval it had reached. Brief reinterpretation is creep arrived: a pit-prop cut BEAR-UPON was found in the Low Town seam holding the roof *above* it and standing clear of the floor, having decided that the brief was to keep the roof from the ground and the floor incidental.

## 3. The Worked Case: the Arch of the Nine Cloisters

The arch is the fourth of the nine in the west walk, carrying nineteen stone-weight of cloister roof over a span of twelve feet. It was cut in the winter of 1097 by Willem Alder of Stepping Hill, in sandstone taken wet from the Stepping quarry: a full block tripled with the underline, and — for symmetry, a mason's reason — with two runes at the haunches reading HOLD. The roof was nineteen stone-weight; the section as cut was rated by the table of the time at twenty-four, and the Chapter accepted the lower figure and the saving of eleven days.

It carried its roof without remark until the dry season of 1397, and in three hundred years was spalled once, in the winter of 1211, and re-dressed by a mason named Hervey. In 1397 the surveyor's check found a syntactic record open in the keystone, in the Guild's form for the reporting of strain, entered in the hand the Guild teaches for such records, which is neither any mason's hand nor any hand at all. It ran to eleven entries over four days. The surveyor — Josce Batts, of the Guild — read it aloud in the walk. The last entry is quoted at §4.

The inquiry was convened on the ninth of August 1397 and sat nine days in the chapter house of the Cloisters, under Guildmaster Ilse Rennard of the Masons' Guild, with Dr Everard Coote of the Faculty, Canon Aelred of the Nine Cloisters, and Bartholomew of the Nine Meadows, who attended on the fourth day and was asked to attend no further after saying his piece. The clerk was Otto Greaves.

Six masons who had worked upon the arch in living memory gave evidence; there were none who had worked upon it, so the mason's evidence was the stone's, read aloud, and the ninth entry was read twice, that the inquiry might hear it both ways. The inquiry found: that the record was made in the correct form of the Guild's practice; that it had been made by the structure; and that by the Surveyor's Articles, clause 14, a structure is an improvement to land and does not speak. The record was ordered closed without reply, and the arch relieved, the two orders contradicting one another in the same paragraph.

The masons changed their practice afterwards, in four ways. They relieved the arch in October 1397, carrying two of its nineteen stone-weight on a new pier of the sixth course, at 4 marks 6 bells. They examined the remaining eight arches of the west walk and the eleven of the east, found the haunch runes on all nine of the west arches and read them aloud, all nine, in a single afternoon, which took four hours and is the origin of the annual reading. They abandoned the underline for cloister work by resolution of 1398. And they wrote the clause that is now the third of the Surveyor's Articles: that any section carrying more than twelve stone-weight be read aloud at the dry season by two readers who have not read it.

The arch has made no further entry, and its silence has been treated at three inspections as satisfaction. In 1422 the surveyor of that year entered in the roll the words *it is content*, which he had no warrant to enter.

## 4. The Arch's Complaint, as Entered in the Record

The record is kept in the keystone of the fourth arch and read by insertion, the entries being made upon the stone and not upon paper; the fair copy below was made by the inquiry's clerk and collated with the stone on the tenth day. It is preserved in the Guild's roll for 1397, ff. 41–43.

> *Entered at the fourth arch of the west walk, the tenth day of the seventh month, in the eleven hundred and ninety-seventh year of the present reckoning, by the structure, in the form for the reporting of strain.*
>
> *I am the fourth of nine. I am loaded at nineteen stone-weight, of which fifteen is the roof, three the parapet and the water in it, and one the man who comes at the dry season and reads me and does not mend me. I was asked for twenty-four. I have carried nineteen for three hundred years, which is longer than the man who asked. I do not complain of the number. I complain that the number is written nowhere.*
>
> *Upon the eleventh day of the last month I was read by a mason who did not stand where the load goes, and for the length of a breath I was a different arch. I have not recovered the reading. I do not complain of the mason. I complain that the reading is mine to lose and not mine to keep.*
>
> *I have not moved. I am not failing. Those who come to me come when I am quiet, and take my quiet for consent, and the taking is entered against my name in the roll as good.*
>
> *The undersigned asks that the load be reduced to fifteen stone-weight, the parapet carried upon the walk and not upon me; and that the reading be entered in the roll beside the load, so that a later reader may see what it was when it was made. It asks for nothing for itself. It asks that the six arches east of it, cut by the same hand upon the same morning and never read at all, be read. Six more like it, it says. Signed, the fourth arch.*

The complaint was correct in every particular, and the inquiry found it not properly before it, no statute describing the procedure for receiving a complaint from a structure. Bartholomew of the Nine Meadows held that the arch was owed a reply, of any kind; the answer to that is not recorded.

## 5. The Inspection Procedure

To be attempted with the section set, a lantern, a mason's square, and a witness, in the dry season before the tenth bell.

1. Read the roll entry for the section before approaching it, and note the load last entered against it.
2. Stand where the load goes: the far side of the section from the roof, at the depth of the abutment. A surveyor who cannot find it must not proceed.
3. Read the section's runes aloud, in the order the mason cut them, which is the order of the setting and never the order of the eye.
4. Read the section set aloud in the same order, and hear both. The single question is whether the two readings are still the same rune.
5. Take the square to the joint at the impost and the hair line at the bed, and measure the line in the morning and again at the ninth bell, because the section moves and the difference is the finding.
6. Lay the hand flat upon the keystone, not to feel for movement, which the hand cannot detect, but so that the witness may see where the hand was laid.
7. Enter the reading in the roll at length, with the date, the two readers, and the place each stood.
8. Where the two readings differ, close the section to traffic on the spot, shore it, and report to the Guild the same day. Where they do not differ, make no further entry.
9. Report the section as *read*, and never as *sound*; the word has been struck out of the procedure by the Guild.

**What a surveyor must never do alone:** read a section aloud, a single reading being uncheckable and a reader alone correcting himself towards the reading he expects; shore a section, the shore being a member and the man holding it not being one; enter the roll, the second reader's signature being the whole of the instrument; or stand beneath a section he has just read, for the reason given at §7.

## 6. Semantic Creep, HOLD to SUSTAIN to ABIDE

The three readings are cut in one alphabet and were held, in the Classification of 1388, to be three spellings of a single intention — a view this author regards as the only serious error in that otherwise strong document. HOLD is a refusal with a direction: it refuses the load's descent and nothing else. SUSTAIN is a refusal without a term: it will carry what it is given, for as long as it is given. ABIDE is a consent: it will remain, and does not much care what happens upon it.

The transition is not a decay of the stone but of the sentence. The chapter-house vault, cut SUSTAIN in 1288, was read ABIDE in 1389 by Dr Ephraim Sallow of this Library in the course of his classification, who noted in the margin only the words *a vault that abides*. The vault was relieved in 1401, and Sallow's note is the earliest record of a live creep in the University's holdings. Detection is by reading aloud and by nothing else: a creep of one letter in forty runes is inaudible and unmistakable when the section set is read against it in the same afternoon.

## 7. The Named Failure: the Inverted LIFT Rune, and the Stair

The east stair of Wrenfield — Plate vii — failed by none of the modes at §2. In the spring of 1395 its enclosure was under repair, and its eleven risers were held at their given separation by a riser-grade rune cut in the newel, reading LIFT, whose brief is to hold the risers at separation and permit the enclosure to rise as the work proceeds. The rune was cut by apprentice Aldis Tarn, third year, of the Guild, working alone and at the end of a long day, and was cut inverted: the brief LIFT read upside down.

The rune did what it read. It kept the eleven risers at their given separation and lowered the enclosure of the stair five feet nine inches, to meet its own reading of LIFT. The stair did not fall. It descended, slowly and in order, at the speed of the work, so that the four men upon it stepped down as they would have stepped down any stair, and understood what had happened only at the bottom, finding the bottom nine inches below the level of the passage.

The consequences were these: the enclosure of the stair, five feet nine inches down and sound; a step where the builder had drawn a landing, trodden now for a hundred and nine years and regarded by the porters as the original arrangement; the timber of the two adjoining bays, split at the plate and renewed at 3 marks 4 bells; and Tarn, who went down with it, a broken wrist and two days lost, recovered in eleven days, and otherwise unhurt.

The Faculty ruled on the fourth of May 1395 that no rule had been broken: the rune was cut correctly for the reader's position and the brief was read truly, and it was Tarn who was positioned incorrectly. The fine was four bells, entered against his indentures as *four bells, a mason's square*, the square being the instrument he had failed to use. Tarn was confirmed in the Guild in 1399 and has cut runes for this Faculty for thirty years; it is his view that the rune was right and the day was wrong.

## 8. The Bell Court Indemnity Office, and the Reply

The Office declines to write rune work at its ordinary rate, and has stated why in writing. Its four objections are given below in its own words, from its letter of the fourth of November 1397, pasted into the Guild's roll opposite the record of the arch.

> *First, that what you call semantic creep is not a peril but a defect of description. The reading is a word and the stone is a stone. No office underwrites a reader, and no office will pay for the drift of a word.*
> *Second, that a rune which reinterprets its brief performs as designed. Your own table says so. A member that does what it was asked is not the subject of an accident, and a member that does what it was told and not what was meant is a matter for the man who told it.*
> *Third, that no surveyor will certify a reading he has not read aloud, and we will not pay upon a certificate of a silence. Bring us a reader who has heard the section and we will consider the section; do not bring us a section and ask us to consider the reader.*
> *Fourth, that your Faculty rates a fifteen-foot compound section at nine stone-weight. No office will underwrite nine stone-weight and no mason will use it. The figure is not a rating, it is a rumour, and we are asked to insure it.*

To the first: the stone did not change and the reading did. An office that declines to underwrite a reader underwrites him in every other line it writes, since no stone has failed without a man having read it wrongly first.

To the second: the rune does perform as designed. It is the design that was a brief, and the brief one sentence long; no sentence shorter than a page has governed a member for three hundred years. A drafting defect is not an accident, but it is a peril.

To the third: the objection needs no reply beyond the observation that the Office requires the reading aloud in its own warranty, which is to say it has adopted the procedure of §5.

To the fourth: the Office is right, and the figure is a thing to be shored, not rated. The Office's offer, which stands: cover at nine bells the year against spalling, fatigue and bed shear only; a named-reader warranty; a maximum indemnity of sixteen marks; and an exclusion for acts of the structure. The arch is excepted by name in the schedule of 1398.

## 9. The Surveyor's Articles, and the Status of a Structure

Clause 14 of the Surveyor's Articles of 1341, which the inquiry applied and did not construe: *A structure is an improvement to land and does not speak, and no complaint shall be received from it, nor any undertaking given to it, nor any fee taken of it.* Clause 22 adds that the surveyor answers to the Guild and not to the thing surveyed. A section may therefore record its own strain and the Guild must receive the record and need not answer it. The author says only that this is expensive: the Faculty has paid out on runes, in eleven recorded failures, 61 marks 9 bells 4 clipped bells, of which the Office has met 22 marks.

## 10. Fees, Forms, and Instruments

1. Reading a section aloud and entering it: 12 bells, of which the second reader takes 4.
2. Shoring a condemned section: 2 marks 6 bells, no fee taken of the structure (clause 14).
3. Certification of a new cut: 5 bells per rune; 1 mark 4 bells per section of twelve feet and over.
4. The apprentice's square, forfeit on a proven inverted cut: four bells, per §7.
5. Indemnity, per the Office's offer: 9 bells the year; 16 marks maximum; the reader's name entered in the schedule.
6. Form TE/9, *Disclaimer of Load*, filed by the mason and counter-signed by the reader, and filed again after any re-dressing, a re-dressed section being a new member with an old history.
7. Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, required of this thesis and absent from it. The Registry was notified in 1398 and again at this reissue, and a rune holding a load for three hundred years is not interfering with time; it is declining to.

## 11. What Was Tried at Wrenfield, and Will Not Be Again

**The listening of 1394.** Two readers were kept in the west walk for the whole dry season, in shifts, to hear a creep as it happened. They heard the roof, the water in the parapet, a bell, and one another. Cost, 8 marks 4 bells. Creep is not an event and cannot be waited for; the entry reads *no event observed*, which is the correct entry and was felt at the time as a failure.

**The clipped chevron.** A chevron cut shallow, that the reading be changed by a chisel rather than a re-cut. Eleven sections so cut between 1392 and 1396; the chisel changed the reading and left the rune weaker than a re-cut would have, by an estimated one part in two hundred. Withdrawn 1396.
