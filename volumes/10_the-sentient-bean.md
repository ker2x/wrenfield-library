## Abstract (as filed)

This thesis examines the dignity of the cultivated legume, proposes a procedure for its insult, and reports germination under praise, indifference, and mockery, under the Faculty's licence of 1497. Dean Ambrose Quill's ethics review has been pending since the first trial; the author's position is consent by growth. *Form 7B, Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is missing from this copy and both deposits.

## 1. On the dignity of the legume

The Faculty's definition of dignity in the plant kingdom was adopted in 1466 and has not been revised. A plant possesses dignity where it (i) maintains a distinction from its substrate, (ii) responds to address in a manner not accounted for by light or gravity, and (iii) is capable of being wronged. The legume satisfies all three. The third is where the Faculty has split, and where the greenhouse keeps its own minutes. Dignity is not sensibility: nothing requires that a bean feel anything, only that it be *addressable*. It is not a pet, and not a parishioner: it is a party.

The doctrine was first applied to a legume in 1471, at a bed of broad beans in Low Town whose cultivator cursed them and complained of the crop. The court found for the beans, the cultivator having addressed them as a party, and ordered an apology read at the bed. The apology was read; the beans did not improve.

Two distinctions govern. A curse is an operation upon a plant's fortune; an insult is an operation upon its standing, and may be complete without effect and effective without harm. Refusal and condition are otherwise: a seed in dry storage cannot be compelled, while *Tenterfield Quiet* near laughter is in condition, and cannot be compelled either, for the opposite reason.

## 2. The insult protocol, in procedure

The protocol was drafted with the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork, which returned it with eleven queries and no approval. A licensed administrator is required for movement four; the licence costs six bells and is renewable at Martinmas.

1. **Standing.** The administrator stands at the frame's marked end, three feet from the soil line, and states name and faculty.
2. **Notification.** The insult is read aloud once, in the licensed form, so that nothing is said to the bean not first said in its hearing.
3. **Interval.** Nine minutes of silence; delivery inside the interval is void and the fee is not returned.
4. **Delivery.** The insult is spoken at a measured voice, not shouted, once, and not repeated.
5. **Withdrawal.** The administrator leaves the frame within one minute of delivery.
6. **Entry.** The insult, the hour, and the administrator's state of mind are entered in the day-book by a second person.
7. **Rest.** Forty hours of silence at the frame, during which the bed is watered but not spoken to.
8. **Count.** The bed is counted at the eighth day by a person who did not administer.

**The rule about who may not be in the house.** No person who has laughed at any part of a trial, licensed or unlicensed, may be in the house during movements one to five, and the exclusion runs for fourteen days. It applies to the whole house, not the frame, and whether or not the laughter was that person's own. It is the oldest rule in the protocol and the only one that cannot be waived by the Dean, the licence, or the author. It has been invoked twice; one is the case at §4.

The catalogue of forms runs to thirty-one entries, four struck out as insulting the cultivator and not the plant. The administrator's state of mind is recorded in five terms: *even*, *hurried*, *unwell*, *amused*, *sorry*; the house has never entered *amused*.

The cultivar *Tenterfield Quiet*, entered as a control, will not germinate within earshot of laughter. The effect is not diminished by distance, by a brick partition, or by a written joke read silently; it germinates normally in the dark, and in the presence of weeping. Sixty-one sowings were made before the result was accepted, and the frame first sown has yielded nothing in three seasons and stands as the record.

## 4. The case in full: Row F and the keeper, the twentieth of June

Hesper Rill, keeper of the north houses, appointed 1494, entered on the second of May 1500 the sowing of Row F: one hundred and four seeds of *Tenterfield Quiet* in a frame marked as the visitors' row, set aside for visitors unconnected with the trial. The protocol had been read to the house the Monday before. On the twelfth of March the day-book records Row F laughed at by seventeen persons for an hour, the keeper's initials in the margin.

On the twentieth of June, Row F stood at nothing. The day-book for that date, in the keeper's own hand, reads: *Row F, nothing, and the row has been laughed at every day since the second of May ~~and the keeper is of opinion that the keeper must be the one to begin the laughing~~.* The words after *May* were struck out that week by Mr. Casper Nunn, Deputy Under-Librarian, unsigned. The leaf is bruised; the line is legible.

What is not in doubt is the laughter. It began among the visitors; the keeper, who stood at the marked end of the frame to read the schedule, laughed at the third line of it, in the house, before the delivery, in the hearing of the bed. The laughter was not licensed. The insult that followed was licensed and correctly delivered, and by the rule at §2 it was void, the administrator having been in the house within fourteen days of laughing in it.

The consequence came in three parts. First, the row was resown on the eleventh of July and again on the second of August, one hundred and four seeds each time, and nothing came up. Second, Mr. Rill was summoned by the Faculty of Ethics on the nineteenth of July, entered no defence the Faculty could use, and was relieved of the north houses on the twenty-third of July, the day-book passing to the second gardener. He was not dismissed. The Faculty found that he had laughed and that the rule excluded him; it declined to find that he had begun it, the entry asserting the beginning having been struck unsigned. Third, at the close on the twenty-ninth of September Row F stood at nothing, and the frame remains in the north house, charged to the Faculty, and is not to be moved.

Mr. Rill's account, entered after the close, is in the Appendix. He denies the line, and says that if he wrote it he was not himself at the time.

## 7. The ethics application to the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork

The application was filed on Form EA/3 in the ninth month of 1497, one hundred and forty-one words short of the length required, and is reproduced in full, as the Faculty has not.

> To the Dean and Chapter of the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork, from Rosalind Wren, D.Th. candidate, of the Faculty of Herbology & Sentient Botany. I apply for a licence to insult a legume. The legume is *Vicia* cv. *Tenterfield Quiet*, a cultivar of the Tenterfield fields; the trials are to run in the south house of the HS range, in three frames per species, under matched soil and light; the forms are those at HS/2, HS/4, and HS/9; the number of seeds is four hundred and nineteen in the first trial; the injury proposed is none, the harm proposed is none, and the wrong proposed is that the bean be addressed as a party and found to answer. Consent will be established by growth, since a seed that germinates has entered and a seed that does not has declined; where the count shows declension tracking the content of the trial, the author will read the declension as an answer given. No animal is concerned, no student is exposed, and no member of the public will be admitted. I am aware that the Faculty has not approved an application of this class since 1402, and I have read the reason for that. I undertake to close the trial on the Faculty's word and to publish the refusal if it comes. Rosalind Wren, ninth month, 1497.

The application has been pending since the first trial and through four seasons, two of which are accounted in the tables at §3. The author has answered the eleven queries twice, in 1497 and 1499, unopened. Dean Ambrose Quill does not write; the file is with him, and no step in this study has been taken under any approval.

## 8. Consent, the review, and the doctrine of entrance

The beans consented by growing. This is the author's defence, offered without embarrassment. A seed in dry storage is in refusal. Four hundred and nineteen were sown in the first trial; those that germinated entered by an act of their own, and those that did not are counted as refusals in Appendix C, the entrants being twenty-five. The mockery frames germinated below the indifferent, declining in a manner tracking the trial's content — most where the insult concerned the stock and least where it concerned the angle. A subject that can decline in proportion has been asked.

The Faculty of Applied Necromancy's ethics annex on household poultry, AN 2.3 / C76 1499, is the closest precedent in the library and is cited on consent. It is not comfortable: the annex holds that a reanimated hen cannot consent because it has no state to consent from, and follows the argument to the bean by an analogy the author rejects, the bean having such a state, and having been in it when it declined. The Faculty of Ethics' own handbook of forms, EA 1.1 / G74 1493, provides at its eleventh chapter a form for a grievance held by a party that cannot write, and none for a party that can answer only by growing.

## 9. The objection of the greenhouse staff, and the author's reply

The greenhouse staff do not accept the study, and have entered their objection in the day-book eleven times. Three are reproduced here, the author's practice being to reproduce an objection before answering it.

> **the 12th of March.** The protocol was read to the house on Monday, as it must be, and Row F was laughed at by seventeen persons for an hour. The house is a place of work and not a theatre, and the ferns in the north two have not been right since.

> **the 19th.** Row C now four. The insult administered at the eighth bell by the licensed party, who read from the schedule and did not depart from it. We ask that the schedule be read to us also, since we are in the house and hear it whether we would or no, and the fourth form is a lie about the Lentil, which has done nothing to deserve it.

> **the 17th of April.** Row C, one. It has stood a month. The insult has been administered daily and the schedule read aloud each time, and the seedling has not germinated and has not died. We do not say the work is cruel. We say it is cruelty with a form attached, and that the form is the cruelty, and that we are the ones who water it.

The author's reply is threefold. First, the staff's third objection is one of the Faculty's eleven queries, answered twice, unopened. Second, the schedule will be read to the house before the season opens, and the fourth form has been struck on the staff's objection, not the Faculty's. Third — a concession, entered as one — the daily administration to Row C was unnecessary after the fourth week, continued because a daily trial is easier to count than a weekly one. She has discontinued it. She does not concede the cruelty; she concedes the convenience.

## 10. The move to the north greenhouse: a named failure

The study was moved to the north greenhouse on the twenty-sixth of April 1498: a failure with a fee attached. The laughter travelled the south house through the open vents, and the vents are the duty of the Faculty of Agricultural Curses, whose windows the Under-Librarian requires shut. A frame laughing there was heard at eleven o'clock on the nineteenth of April in the potting shed, ninety-four feet away, by a person who laughed; the row nearest the vents was exposed to laughter the licence did not name, and the south-house trial was void from that hour.

The move required a licence of the Faculty of Ethics, which charged six bells for the removal of a licensed trial from the house named in the licence, and four bells 4 clipped for the re-registration of three frames and the south-house row: ten bells 4 clipped, which the author paid. The south-house trial had run four weeks, carrying three species at matched light; the move broke the matching and the planted rows of the season were abandoned, at three hundred and twelve sowings and no result. The move cost eleven days, three frames carried by hand through the north corridor, whose floor is unsound at the eleventh flag; one frame was dropped at the ninth, and one hundred and four seeds were lost. The north house is colder by eleven degrees, noisier by the kitchens, further from the water. It has not laughed. A house which has not laughed is not a house which cannot.

## 11. Whether a bean can be defamed, and the findings

The move made the question practical. A defamation requires a body of the reputed, and the body named by *Tenterfield Quiet* was the bed. The beds of the north house are two hundred and eight beans of the same cultivar, and none has germinated, so none can give evidence. A party whose whole acquaintance is in the same condition cannot be defamed in their hearing, or out of it either, there being no one to hear. The Faculty has heard the argument and has not ruled; the author cites Ashgrove-Vane, *Principles*, 3rd ed., at JM 1.1, on the silence of parties who cannot speak.

The findings are three. The legume possesses dignity under the definition of 1466, on all three criteria, and is a party. *Tenterfield Quiet* will not germinate within earshot of laughter, a condition and not a refusal; germination under mockery falls below indifference in all ten species tested, in one to nothing; consent is established by entrance, and declined by declension. Row F was laughed at in the hearing of the bed on the second of May 1500 and stood at nothing; the keeper's line was struck on the twentieth of June and restored by the author.
