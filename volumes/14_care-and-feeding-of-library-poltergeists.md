## Abstract (as filed)

This compilation sets out what is known, and lawfully payable, about the resident poltergeist of the Restricted Stack: its incident register, the rules of feeding and naming, the complaint as to windows, and the crate behind the desk. It is filed by the officer most often complained of in it. Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is not bound with the volume.

## 1. The Resident, and Why It Is Not a Department

The Restricted Stack has one resident, there since at least 1487, and not on the roll. It is not a ghost: the Library asked, and the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork confirmed the question properly put. It is not a familiar: the Faculty of Domestic Enchantment asked, and declined to ask further. It is a poltergeist, and keeps librarian's hours — quiet until it is not, and never at a reader reading. It works the third gallery and, four times, the second, and has never thrown at the bindery, lamp or window.

## 3. Feeding, and What Must Never Be Fed

The resident is fed on the last day of each month at the desk, on bread unbuttered and milk in the armorial saucer, the only one the Stack has not thrown. Milk is offered, never left; a saucer left overnight is a complaint.

| What may be given | How often | What must never be given | What happened once |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bread, unbuttered, one hand | Last day of the month | Butter, of any kind | 1496: the bread returned by way of the ceiling |
| Milk, in the armorial saucer | Last day of the month | Milk left overnight | 1501: saucer inverted on the shelf-list |
| Water, in the same saucer, rinsed | When the gallery is damp | Water from the entrance-hall vases | 1500: three stems found in bay 12 |
| Salt, none — see next column | Never | Salt, in any quantity | 1498: bay 40 emptied, volume by volume |
| The Firstmonth gate-cake, broken | Once, in Firstmonth | The Fifthmonth gate-cakes | 1503: returned, at speed, via the ceiling |
| Blank paper, the Stack's own stock | When the resident asks | Paper with writing on it | 1502: an examiner's hat, adjacent |
| Nothing at all, deliberately | Ninthmonth, one month in nine | Anything still warm | 1499: the desk lamp extinguished |
| Its own name, spoken once, as a warning | Never | Anything named | 1489: every volume in bay 40 taken down |

The Ninthmonth fast is not in the Standing Orders; it is in my hand in the desk-book, kept because the register is quiet that month every year, and because a resident fed on every last day learns the calendar. Asked my authority, I answered in writing that the desk is mine and the bread is mine. The Faculty has twice proposed a schedule, and twice been told the Stack is not a cat.

## 4. The Rule of No Names, and Its Age

The rule that it must not be given a name is the oldest entry in the standing orders, older than the sub-shelf, older than the desk. It is in the hand of Mr. Ephraim Sallow, Under-Librarian 1381–1391, who was unwell and who wrote the Classification of 1388; it is the last line of that fair copy, and three words long. The copy in **FN 6.1** carries it.

The rule is not squeamishness, though the Faculty has called it that and superstition too. A resident with a name is a resident with an identity; an identity may be entered on the roll; an entry on the roll is a post; a post is filled by appointment; and appointment lies with the Committee on Standing Matters, which has not met since 1492 and has no quorum. I have no wish to see the resident's appointment debated by a body without a quorum, and have said so at four meetings. Nor is the rule the resident's: nothing in the register for a hundred years suggests it wants a name, and it has never asked. It was offered one once, and the eleven days follow.

## 5. The Naming of 1489, in Full

On 4 Fifthmonth 1489 a reader in the third gallery, Mr. Hollis Wren, an undergraduate of the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork and no relation to the Margrave, addressed the resident as **Wrenfield**, reasoning that the Library is named for the Margrave, who bequeathed four hundred books and one unresolved argument, and that the resident had *settled it*. He said the name three times, before two witnesses whose names stand in the visitors' book, then offered bread and butter.

The resident accepted the name, and took the bread and did not return it. In the eleven days following, as the register has it: it took down every volume in bay 40, laid them open on the floor, spines up; on the second day replaced them in the correct order, which no officer of the Stack could have done in a day; on the third took down bay 41 and began again. Thereafter the lamp went out whenever the name was not said; the visitors' book was found closed at the page for the third; on the sixth and seventh days, nothing; the saucer was inverted; the second gallery was worked; the catalogue press opened at W. On the eleventh day the resident answered to a reader who had not used the name; I entered the matter as *answered*.

The enquiry sat before the Library Committee on 2 Sixthmonth 1489, the Faculty of Domestic Enchantment represented and the Faculty of Agricultural Curses not. Mr. Wren attended, was heard, and was not contrite: the name had been accepted, acceptance was consent, and consent was the whole of the law under *Principles of Minor Maleficence*, 3rd ed., the good one, at the chapter on consenting domestic intelligences. The Committee found that a name had been given to a resident not on the roll; that such a resident has no name to give; that the giving was a nullity; and that the register must nevertheless be corrected. The standard fine for misuse of a reader's privileges, **1 mark 3 bells**, was set on him.

I hold that the fine was the Library's to pay — not Mr. Wren's — and have held so in writing for fifteen years. The Standing Orders make the desk responsible for the resident and nobody else; a reader is not the desk. If a name should not be spoken, the officer who keeps the rule must say so while a reader is at the shelf; no notice to that effect was posted in 1489, and none is posted now. The Library should have remitted the 1 mark 3 bells and posted the notice, and did neither; it corrected the register instead, striking the name at the page for the fourth. The Library paid nothing. The fee it paid was 4 bells to the binder for re-sewing the bay-40 volumes, and 6 clipped bells for a lamp wick, charged to the sub-shelf.

## 6. Percival Nott, in Transit since 1461

Mr. Percival Nott entered the third-floor mirror on the sixth of May 1461, during a demonstration, and remains in transit. He is not dead: the Library satisfied itself of that in 1463. His card is still active, the Standing Orders providing that a card is cancelled on the reader's death, and there being no death to record; I check the roll twice yearly. The balance is **4 marks 6 bells 2 clipped bells** — sixty-three years of overdue items at the rate set in 1462, most of it a quarto of threshold geometry, **LT 3.7**, borrowed the day before the demonstration. The fines are waived annually during the search, at the September meeting of the Library Committee: **forty-three consecutive years**. The Standing Orders allow a fine to be waived and not deferred, so each waiver is final, and a final balance cannot be final forty-three times. In 1503 I asked whether a waiver during a search is final or a deferral; no ruling has come. The Assistant Librarian has computed the balance to 1580 without instruction, at 11 marks 4 bells, and posted it in the office.

## 7. The Crate behind the Desk, and Why It Is Not Mine to Open

The crate behind the desk has awaited cataloguing since 1494. It is pine, iron-bound, one and a half feet by one foot by nine inches, and not shelf-listed. It is not accessioned by me, and not mine to open; the reason is not obstinacy but the Instrument of the Assessing Under-Librarian, engrossed in 1391. The accessioning of an unaccessioned thing is that officer's duty, its custody the duty of the officer at whose desk it stands. There has been no Assessing Under-Librarian since 1391, and the stipend of the post, 2 marks 8 bells, has stood in the contingency unremoved.

I have counted the crate four times, before the Deputy Under-Librarian; at the third count it had moved four inches left of its mark, wedging the projection drawer. The manifest, in a hand of 1494, is one volume, octavo, self-help. The Committee has had the matter before it four years running, and each time passed on.

## 9. The Closed Windows

By standing instruction **AC 118.7 / T45 1487**, the Faculty of Agricultural Curses & Pollen Pathology requires the Stack windows to be kept shut from the first day of Fourthmonth to the last day of Sixthmonth, the Stack drawing pollen from the Tenterfields and the Faculty's own windows not being openable in compensation. The instruction hangs on the second gallery wall, framed by me.

I object, and record it. Paper wants air. The third gallery has been damp in Fifthmonth since 1488 and bay 40 has gone mouldy twice; the Conservator reports the damp in the flat voice of one who does not intend to discuss it. No member of that Faculty has entered the third gallery in eleven years, established from the visitors' book kept at the desk by me — a fact the Faculty does not dispute, though it disputes its meaning. I open the windows at six bells each morning from Fourthmonth, and close them at eight, high pollen or no, because the alternative is a rule I should keep in my head. This is the offence most often laid against me, and the argument of *On Ventilation*, **DE 22.3 / O55 1502**: a room with no air is not a room with books but a room with a condition.

## 10. The Fourth Reading Room, and the Bookings of 1503 and 1504

The fourth reading room is in the plans of 1489 and not in the building. The plan shows four desks, a catalogue press, a river window on the north range and a counter; the building has a corridor of the same dimensions, no window, and a draught not to be stopped. The room is in the room-book and may be booked. Twice I have booked it, for inductions, in 1503 and 1504; no reader attended either; both complained of a draught, the only thing reportable of a corridor. The room-book records both bookings as **kept**, the Library having no form for *not in the building*. There is no form for Form 7B either, and Form 7B, the *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is missing from every thesis here. I have written to the Bursar three times without reply; the third letter is in the Appendix.

The plan of 1489 is kept in the plan chest in the Lower Gallery, and shows the chest standing inside the fourth reading room. The Conservator has measured both, and will not reconcile them. The room was filed under R in the reordering of the Registry in 1487, and is not mine to unfile; it went with its counter, its lamp and its papers, and the Registry's clock has stood at forty-one minutes past two since. The manual that stood at the counter, ***Form 7B for Dummies***, an Aldermarket piracy, octavo, bought in 1483 for 4 bells, is catalogued and not held. Readers ask weekly; I send them to the room, which is filed, and thence to the treatise on the filing, **EA 2.4 / P54 1504**, the only work here that is also an apology.

## 11. The Restricted Stack Procedure

The procedure is posted at the desk in my hand; intended to be attempted, not recommended.

1. **Before opening.** Take the key from the board; count key and crate. If the crate has moved, do not open the projection drawer; record the movement and continue.
2. **Unlock and enter.** Push the door, do not pull it. It is a pull door and pushing has never worked, but the resident has twice rewarded it.
3. **Announce yourself.** Not by name. Say *the desk*, which is the office and not the officer; wait four breaths.
4. **Feed, if it is the last day of the month.** Bread unbuttered, one hand; milk in the armorial saucer; offer, never leave. In the Ninthmonth fast, do not feed.
5. **Work the shelf-list only.** Do not open the catalogue press, and not at the letter W, for any reason, including research.
6. **If it is already in the room.** Do not retreat, read as flight; do not advance, read as an offer. Stand at the desk, both hands on it, and read the shelf-list aloud.
7. **Closing.** Close the windows at eight bells, no earlier. Count the crate, the key and the armorial saucer.
8. **Report.** Enter the hour, the count and any throw on the yellow slip and file it. A reader under a throw is indemnified from the sub-shelf's fund, 4 marks a year.

## 12. Objection and Reply

The Committee's objection, at item 9 of 1503 and repeated at item 2 of 1504, is that **the Restricted Stack is not a department and its resident not a post.** Its reasoning, in its own words as taken by the Clerk, is that the Stack is a room and a room is not a department; that the resident is not on the roll, and a thing not on the roll is not an establishment; that the Standing Orders provide for officers and readers only; that a feeding schedule kept in an officer's hand is not a Standing Order and cannot bind the Library; that the indemnity fund stands in the estimates under *Reading Rooms, Sundries*, with no line of its own; and that the Committee will not create a department for a schedule it did not make.

My reply, filed 1504: the objection is well made; I accept every part of it except the conclusion. A room is not a department; but the Stack is a room with a key, the key is counted, the counting is a duty, and a duty is not a room. A resident not on the roll is not an establishment; but the roll is a list of persons, and the resident has never claimed to be one, has never been entered, and I have entered it nowhere. The schedule is not a Standing Order; it is a schedule, kept in a hand, at a desk, and if the Committee will not make it a Standing Order it must accept that the feeding lies at the discretion of the officer at the desk — that the discretion is mine, exercised ten years, and that no reader has been harmed. An indemnity is the only thing here that has ever made a reader whole. The Committee will not create a department; I have not asked it to. I have asked it to read the instrument of 1391, which it cannot read; to fill a post, which it will not; and to open a crate, which is not mine. I have kept both without a loss entered against me, and the register shows one, in 1489, and it was the Library's.

## Appendix: The Under-Librarian's Third Letter to the Bursar

*Provenance:* fair copy in the letter-book of the Reading-Room Under-Librarian, 1504, f. 212, sent 9 Thirdmonth 1504.

> Sir — I write for the third time concerning the fourth reading room, and shall be brief, the matter being small and the room smaller. The room is in the plans of 1489 and not in the building, and is nevertheless in the room-book; this year I booked it for an induction, the booking entered as kept, no reader attended, and the reader complained of a draught — that of the north corridor, which is not to be stopped. The Library has no form for *not in the building*; I have looked in the forms chest, and there is none, and none for the plan chest either, which the plan shows inside the room. I am therefore obliged to record a kept booking for a room that is not there, and to send readers to it; I have sent two. If the Bursar will have the room expunged from the room-book, I will do it; if built, I will keep the key and count it; if neither, I will go on as I am, and the record will show that I asked. Your obedient servant, Fenwick Oll, Under-Librarian, Reading Rooms.
