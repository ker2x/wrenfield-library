## Abstract (as filed)

This manual sets out draconic dental practice as it is properly conducted: examination, consent, anaesthesia, measurement, restoration, and fees. It reports enamel thickness for four draconic forms, a fee schedule in marks, bells and clipped bells, the examination sequence in full, and the litigation record of the Faculty to the year of imprint. Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is not filed with this volume, nor with any volume here.

## 1. Upon the Word Dentistology

The discipline has been practised in Alderwick since the Low Town farriers took up the teeth of draught drakes in 1362, and for thirty years it had no name. It had a ledger: that of Peter Pellow, farrier, of Cockle Row, 1362 to 1389, consisting almost entirely of the phrase *drake, front, filed, paid*. In the margin of the entry for the eleventh of May 1374 the same hand wrote *dentistologie*. The Faculty of Beast Husbandry bought the ledger in 1394 for one mark and eight bells and, transcribing it, corrected the word to *dentistry*, "as being the received term and the honest one." The correction is in Beast Husbandry's own hand.

**Dentistology** names the study of dentition in its own right, in its own mouth, on its own terms. *Dentistry* was borrowed from the human practise of the tooth-drawer and drags with it three assumptions: that the patient sits, that the patient has hands, that the patient's jaw may be held. None of the three holds.

The author's degree, the Doctorate of Dental Thaumaturgy, was created in 1502 and conferred in the same week upon the author, by the author. The Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork has queried the arrangement four times without resolving it; the Registrar has entered the degree in the roll; and a dragon does not ask to see a certificate, but will ask to see the fee.

## 2. The Dentitions

Four dentitions and six forms are treated in Alderwick, and they are not variations upon one mouth: the Fen Drake and the Netherfen Grey share a jaw, the Marrow-Green stands alone, the Ridgeback and the Saltmarsh Black differ only in the hinge, and the Wyrm is the old line. The measurements below are means of the author's casebooks, 1481 to 1501: three readings to the tooth, the lowest recorded and the mean of the two highest reported.

| Dentition | Jaw | Enamel, in lines of the probe | Probe | Fault band | Anaesthesia required |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Common Fen Drake | Isognathous; upper overhangs by 2 lines | 9.2 | Buccal, direct, hand steady | 0.4 | Compliment, 11–20 min |
| Marrow-Green | Anisognathous; lower projects 9 lines at rest | 14.7 | Buccal, direct; lingual abandoned | 0.9 | Compliment, 25–40 min |
| Tenterfield Ridgeback | Isognathous; fused symphysis after the fourth year | 21.4 | Buccal, direct, chisel adjunct | 1.1 | Compliment, 40 min; second practitioner for the count |
| Old-line Wyrm | Anisognathous; replacement continuous | 6.8 | Taken at forty feet, through a mirror, by consent | 0.3 | Compliment, sustained; the compliment is the whole of the visit |
| Netherfen Grey | Isognathous; the canines not opposed | 11.3 | Buccal, direct; the fourth tooth refused | 0.6 | Compliment, 15–22 min; a second compliment refused |
| Saltmarsh Black | Anisognathous; the jaw hinged at the seventh | 18.9 | Buccal, direct, with two practitioners to hold the light | 1.4 | Compliment, 30 min; worked only on the ebb |

The *fault band* is the depth, in lines, at which the enamel fails and the dentine shows a discoloured seam. It is a measurement of the tooth and not of the dragon. Practitioners new to the work read the band as damage and propose restoration; the band is usually hereditary, and a proposal of restoration made on a hereditary band has cost the Faculty more in actions than every extraction of the last thirty years.

## 3. The Approach, and the Compliment

The approach is the first procedure and the most often failed. A dragon does not consider itself ill; it considers itself approached. The practitioner has no standing until one is given, and standing is given by the compliment or not at all.

The compliment must be true in substance. A dragon hears the difference. An untrue compliment produces a rise of four to nine degrees at the nostril, followed by a relocation of the practitioner and occasionally of the instruments. A true compliment settles a middle-sized dragon for eleven to forty minutes: enough for a measurement, not enough for an extraction.

The sequence is as follows, and a practitioner who alters it will be asked why.

1. **Stand downwind and on foot**, at the patient's ground, instruments in a canvas roll carried open and low.
2. **Give the compliment**, per the formulary below, and note the interval obtained.
3. **Read the consent form aloud in full**, in triplicate, before any instrument is uncovered.
4. **Take the readings** at the buccal face, three to the tooth, the lowest recorded, and state each finding aloud as it is made.
5. **Stop at the patient's word**; a stop is not a complaint and is not to be argued with.
6. **State the fee**, in marks, bells and clipped bells, before the work begins.
7. **Withdraw the roll, close it in the patient's sight**, and enter the withdrawal in the ledger.

The examination sequence is as follows, and a practitioner who alters it will be asked why.

1. **Stand downwind and on foot**, at the patient's ground, instruments carried open and low; a closed case is read as an intention.
2. **Give the compliment**, in the formulary below, and note the interval obtained.
3. **Read the consent form aloud in full**, in triplicate, before any instrument is uncovered.
4. **Take the readings** at the buccal face, three to the tooth, the lowest recorded, and state each finding aloud as it is made.
5. **Stop at the patient's word.** A stop is not a complaint and is not to be argued with.
6. **State the fee**, in marks, bells and clipped bells, before the work begins.
7. **Withdraw the roll, close it in the patient's sight**, and enter the withdrawal in the ledger in the patient's own words.

1. **Name the line.** The four dentitions are not interchangeable, and a drake addressed as a Wyrm will correct the address before it permits anything else.
2. **Praise one feature of the dentition in view**, and only what is in view.
3. **Never mention size.** Length, span and weight are contested; the Ridgeback counts by hands, the Marrow-Green by paces, and no agreed conversion exists.
4. **Offer no opinion on the wings.** The wings are a separate jurisdiction, presently before the Guild.
5. **Continue speaking while working.** Silence is read as calculation.

A compliment that satisfies the formulary is entered in the ledger as an administered dose, with the time of delivery and the interval obtained, and is charged at five bells the quarter hour.

## 4. Consent, in Triplicate

No examination proceeds without a signed consent form. Dragons sue. In the twenty years to 1501 the Faculty has been respondent in sixty-four actions and has lost fifty-eight, the six successes being actions brought against the wrong Faculty. The form is therefore the principal instrument of the practice, and it is completed in triplicate: one copy for the practitioner, one for the dragon, and one for the dragon's advocate, who attends, will have read it first, and is paid by the Faculty at a rate the Faculty has twice sought to revise.

It is printed below in the register of the old line, and the Faculty may not modernise it. A paraphrase is not the form: of the nineteen actions lost on consent not read aloud, eleven turned upon a practitioner having read the sense of a clause instead of the clause, and the dragon's advocate has only to ask for the words. A shorter form is held for the Fen Drake, who will not sit through the Long; it is a translation, it is filed as one, and it is not to be used above the Fen Drake.

> **FORM DD/19 (ORDINARY, LONG). CONSENT TO DRACONIC DENTAL WORK. IN TRIPLICATE.**
>
> I, the dragon whose line and name are written below, being at this hour in possession of my faculties, mine opinions and mine own advocate, do consent to be approached, examined, probed, measured, filed, dressed or drawn, as the case may require, by the practitioner named below, upon the terms following:
>
> **First**, that thou shalt declare aloud, ere thou uncover any instrument, the whole of what thou purposest to do, and shalt declare it again at every stage; and that I may stay thy hand at any stage, for reason or for none, and that my staying of it shall not be construed as a breach of this form, nor as a refusal of thy treatment, nor as a complaint against thee.
>
> **Second**, that thou shalt administer no anaesthesia other than the compliment; that the compliment be true in substance; and that I may require it to be spoken again once, and but once, in any quarter of an hour.
>
> **Third**, that thou shalt bring with thee no more than two persons; and that any escort of mine own line shall be fed at thy Faculty's charge, in the manner and quantity customary, ere the work begin, and not after it.
>
> **Fourth**, that no measurement taken from my person shall be published, tabulated or read aloud in my hearing until the page be shown me and I have had a night to consider it.
>
> **Fifth**, that I may withdraw this consent at any hour, and that my withdrawal be entered in thy ledger by thine own hand, in mine own words as near as thy hand can come to them.
>
> **Sixth**, that thou shalt state thy fee in full ere the work begin, in marks, bells and clipped bells; and that thou shalt make no further charge for the drying of my papers.
>
> In witness whereof I set my hand in triplicate — the dragon, the practitioner, the advocate — and the escort where an escort is present.
>
> *Printed beneath the dragon's hand, in the Faculty's ordinary hand:* This form does not substitute for Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, obtainable elsewhere, and never in the author's knowledge completed.

## 5. Anaesthesia by Compliment

Dragons cannot be rendered insensible by ordinary means, and the means which do work are prohibited by the Faculty, the Guild and the dragons. The method in use is the compliment, delivered in a low, unhurried register while the instruments are arranged. It lasts eleven minutes in the Fen Drake, twenty-five in the Marrow-Green, forty in the Ridgeback, and indefinitely in the Wyrm, whose visit has no other object; a second compliment in the same quarter hour buys no further interval and is charged as though it had.

The author has twice worked past the interval. At Widdershins Bridge in 1489 the patient tolerated the probe and objected at the file; the objection was recorded, priced at two marks six bells, and lost on appeal. At the Tenterfields in 1497 the patient tolerated the file and objected at its withdrawal, and the matter is treated at §9. The interval is a tabulated allowance, not a licence, and a practitioner who runs past it works unanaesthetised upon a speaking patient.

## 6. Enamel, and the Fault Bands

Enamel is measured in lines of the Alderwick probe, one line being the forty-eighth part of an inch as kept at the Guild. Three readings are taken at the buccal face, and their disposition is recorded as well as their value: a band widening toward the crown is hereditary and is left; one widening toward the root is acquired and is dressed; one the same at all three points is neither, and is drawn, the camera not having been repaired since 1488.

The discolouration is not diagnostic and has been treated as though it were. Marrow-Green bands run from pale straw to a green nearly black, and the colour follows the river: drakes of the upper Yarrow pale, of the tide-reach dark, and a drake moved between the two changes in a season.

Fault bands are common and mostly innocent. Of four hundred and seventeen Marrow-Green teeth measured between 1481 and 1501, three hundred and thirty-one presented a band at a mean depth of 0.9 lines, and of those two hundred and ninety-nine required no work; the eighteen that did required it for reasons the band did not disclose, chiefly a cavity at the lingual face which the band had concealed.

The Ridgeback's enamel is the thickest and the least rewarding: it resists the probe, dulls the chisel and conceals its cavities at depth. The author's practice is not to search for them in a patient who is comfortable, a policy the Faculty calls indolent.

## 7. The Fee Schedule

Fees are charged by the work, not the hour, and are payable in advance where the patient has eaten a practitioner. One mark is twenty bells; one bell is twelve clipped bells.

|---|---|---|---|
| Consultation at the patient's ground | 3 bells | Attendance note DD/4 | Any practitioner of the Faculty |
| Consent, read aloud and witnessed, in triplicate | 1 mark 4 bells | Form DD/19 in triplicate | Any practitioner; advocate's fee is the Faculty's |
| Compliment administered, per quarter hour | 5 bells | Ledger entry, with interval | Any practitioner; a false compliment is a charge |
| Enamel measurement, per tooth, three readings | 2 clipped bells | Measurement sheet DD/22 | Any practitioner; the Wyrm only by mirror |
| Filing, per tooth, simple | 9 bells | Operation declared, Form DD/11 | Second-year farrier or above |

| Extraction contested, advocate present | 4 marks 6 bells | Indemnity, advocate's attestation, escort fed | Certified practitioner, plus one to count |

Two lines govern most of the Faculty's difficulty: the ninth, where the twelve marks are credited on withdrawal and the credit is the Faculty's charge against itself; and the consent line, charged whether or not the work proceeds. Indemnity is not in the schedule, not being charged to the patient: the Faculty pays four marks the practitioner the year, and nothing to one who has worked without the advocate present.

## 8. The Paperwork, Accounted For

Of the working year, 61 per cent is paperwork; the remaining 39 per cent is spent with the head in the mouth, and it is the paperwork that the Faculty calls the discipline. The figure was put before the Faculty in 1495 and quoted against it at every revision since.

The sixty-one per cent divides as follows, on the author's returns for the year to Michaelmas 1501: consent, drafted, read and filed in triplicate, 14 per cent; indemnity renewal and the practitioner's annual declaration, 9 per cent; the instrument return and probe certificate, 8 per cent; the species declaration, 7 per cent; the temperature log, 8 per cent; the fee ledger and recovery of arrears, 11 per cent; and the filing of the whole, 4 per cent. There is no entry for the missing 1 per cent, and the author's clerk has looked for it since 1499.

The temperature log is the author's own addition. The interval obtained from a compliment lengthens by one part in twenty for every five degrees above the seasonal mean, which is why the Wyrm is examined in the afternoon and the Ridgeback in the morning; the draft revision of 1496 sought to remove the log, and the author has refused to sign it.

The species declaration must be made in the patient's own words, and is the form most often omitted. In the year to Michaelmas 1501, of two hundred and six attendances, it was made in the patient's words in one hundred and forty-one, by the advocate in fifty-two, and omitted in thirteen; nine of the thirteen were Fen Drakes.

### 9.1 A named failure: *In the matter of Cinderhale*

Cinderhale is a Marrow-Green drake of the Tenterfields, of the upper bank, whose ground runs from the Widdershins Bridge to the osier beds; he is the drake of §11, in the author's care since 1494.

The examination was made on the fourth of June, 1501, at his own ground, the only ground at which a Marrow-Green is seen at ease. The approach was made downwind and on foot, from the osier beds, the instruments in a canvas roll carried open and low. The compliment named the line correctly, praised the third upper buccal tooth, which was whole and well set, and said nothing of the wings, which are large.

The consent form was read aloud in full, at the patient's insistence and against the author's habit, which was to read the first four clauses and ask for the pen. Cinderhale required the whole of it, including the sentence concerning Form 7B. He asked whether the drying of papers had ever been charged, and was told the truth, which is that it has twice. He then struck the words *as the case may require* from the first clause with the author's pen and wrote above them, in a very good hand, *as I shall agree*. Both copies were initialled; the advocate's copy retains the strike.

The examination found a band at the sixth lower left, widening toward the crown, hereditary by the rule at §6 and to be left; and a cavity at the lingual face of the same tooth, of about two lines, which was reported. The finding was stated aloud, which is required, and the patient objected. The band, he said, was not a fault in his tooth but a record of his line: the eastern reaches had kept the like for four hundred years, and a practitioner who called a hereditary mark an injury had first to establish that the line was injured. The author replied that the cavity was not hereditary. The patient replied that the cavity was the author's to prove and not his to deny, and that he would consider the matter over a night, as the fourth clause entitles him to do.

He paid the four marks and six bells on the sixth of June, in clipped bells, counted twice, and did not return. The attendance fee was credited on withdrawal, as the ninth line of the schedule requires; the consent fee of one mark four bells stands; and the Faculty assessed against the author two marks three bells for the aborted attendance.

The loss was not the tooth. In the Hilary term of 1502 the action was filed, and the complaint was not the cavity but the drainage ditch at the osier end of his ground, which the Regents had undertaken in 1498 to dig and had not dug. The Faculty read the action as concerning the examination, which was an error, since the action was heard on the ditch. It lost, was ordered to dig, and was required to pay the advocate's costs, the escort's keep for two days and the whole of the fee of four marks six bells, a fee taken for work not done being held on the dragon's behalf. The ditch was dug in 1503, to forty-one paces.

## 10. The Objection of the Faculty of Beast Husbandry, and the Reply

The Faculty of Beast Husbandry & Veterinary Thaumaturgy has objected to this Faculty's existence since 1494, and in writing, which is a courtesy and is here answered. Its objection is reproduced from its letter of the eleventh of March 1503, filed with the Registrar as item 4.

> A dragon is a beast. It is a beast of uncommon size, of speech, of memory and of counsel, and this Faculty has never denied any of these; but the Schedule of 1388 classes it with the beasts, the Guild's tables of draught list it with the beasts, and this Faculty has treated its ailments since 1389 under a warrant not revoked. The mouth of a beast is this Faculty's work, and has been since the farriers of Cockle Row, whom the author of DD 7.2 claims as his own; those farriers were warranted by this Faculty and their ledgers are held in this Faculty's chest. This Faculty objects to the licensing of a second body to open a draconic mouth, to the charging of a separate fee for a service it already provides, and to the use of an invented vocabulary for a trade it has taught these hundred and fourteen years. It does not object to the author personally, whose measurements it has found accurate and whose fee schedule it has found too high.

The reply rests upon the centaur doctrine, which is not the author's. It was stated by Bartholomew of the Nine Meadows in his diary for the fourth of October 1501, a fair copy of which is held by this library at MS 5.1, in one sentence: *a creature that can refuse has not been handled by me but has been consulted by me, and I shall not describe the second as the first.* It was made for centaurs, in the matter of the clipping of the tail, and Beast Husbandry has accepted it since the third edition of *How to Groom Your Centaur* (BH 12.4 / V67 1501).

The doctrine applies with greater force to a dragon, which can refuse in writing, in triplicate, through an advocate, and has fifty-eight times had its refusal upheld. A beast that cannot refuse may be handled; a patient that can refuse is a party; and a party is not an ailment. Beast Husbandry does not in fact treat dragons: its returns for the year to Michaelmas 1501 record four draconic attendances, three being the registering of a death and one an extraction abandoned at the approach, the note reading *patient declined, no fee taken*.

The author concedes the farriers, concedes the Schedule of 1388, concedes that a dragon is a beast for the purposes of the Guild's tables of draught, and declines to concede the mouth. Beast Husbandry is welcome to examine any dragon that will have it. Two have.

## 11. Upon a Dragon with Opinions about Flossing

A Marrow-Green drake of the Tenterfields, in the author's care since 1494 and the same Cinderhale named above, holds and will state at length that flossing is an affectation from human practise, that it weakens the gum, and that the matter was settled in the eastern reaches before the University was thought of.

The correct approach is not to correct him. Let the argument be heard in full, standing, with the instruments lowered and the roll open. Agree with the elements that are true, of which there are two: that human floss is cut to a human length and so is short for him, and that no one has shown him a gum injured by a human practice. Then propose a trial, with the practitioner's own floss and no charge for it, and let it be described as the dragon's own. He now flosses daily, and has an opinion about the thread also, which is that it should be waxed; he is right.

The rule is that a dragon's objection is not an obstacle to treatment but a term of it, and that a practitioner who wins the argument has lost the patient. Cinderhale's objection to the hereditary band, at §9.1, was of the same kind; the author conceded at the wrong moment and the Faculty paid two marks three bells for the lesson.

## 12. Dean Quill's Non-Approval

The manual is issued without the approval of Dean Ambrose Quill, in whose gift the imprimatur lies. His approval was sought in the first month of 1502, in the third, and at the Dean's door on the ninth of June. It has not been given, nor refused: it remains, in the Registry's phrase, in the course of consideration.

The Dean approves nothing. The author records this not as a grievance but as a fact of the Faculty's constitution, having satisfied himself that the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork has issued no imprimatur since the Dean's appointment, that the register of imprimaturs ends in 1481, and that its last entry is an approval given to a work never printed. The author's enquiries were made courteously, in writing, four times, and have not been answered, which is the Dean's manner; the Clerk of Appeals calls it the safest form of consent.

The volume is issued without Form 7B, the *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, required of every work in this library. The author's enquiries indicate that the form is issued from a room on the plan that cannot be entered, and never completed. The absence is of no consequence here, since a manual of teeth does not travel in time; the author has said so twice in writing to the Registry, which has filed the letters under the same number as the volume, the nearest thing to approval the Faculty has received.

## Appendix: The Four Plates

The plates are drawn by the author's own hand and engraved by Mistress Halloway of the Aldermarket, who has never seen a dragon and has worked from the drawings, which is the reason for the third plate's difficulty.

**Plate I. The mouths of the six, in profile, at one scale.** Six profiles, head on, mouth open to the working angle, all at the same scale; the folding sheet has been done correctly by the binder. The Old-line Wyrm is not drawn to scale on the sheet, at his own request, and the Fen Drake's upper overhang of two lines is shown against the rule, and the drake is named in the caption, by his own wish, as Bosun.

**Plate II. The four jaws, from above, showing the approach of the probe.** Four views, each with the probe's line of approach in a broken line and the abandoned approaches in a dotted one. The Marrow-Green's lingual approach is shown twice, as attempted in 1493 and as abandoned; the second drawing is the more useful. The fold crosses the Ridgeback's fourth molar and the author regrets it.

**Plate III. Fault bands, drawn at four depths, with the hereditary seam distinguished.** Sixteen bands in four rows of four, each with its depth in lines and the year of its taking. The hereditary seam is shown at the top of each group and the acquired band beneath it, and the difference is plain once seen and invisible before. The engraver has drawn the discolouration in three tones where the author gave her two; the third is the colour of the upper river, which is not a fault, and is retained.

**Plate IV. The consent form, DD/19, reduced.** The whole of the Ordinary Form (Long) reduced to a single folding plate at the size at which it is filed, with the sixth clause legible and the rest not. It has been used once, at Widdershins Bridge, where the patient read the sixth clause with a lens and demanded the original.
