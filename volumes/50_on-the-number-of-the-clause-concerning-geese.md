## Abstract (as filed)

This enquiry establishes that the clause of the Capitular Statutes of 1274 under which the Faculty of Applied Necromancy holds its warrant, and under which it has refused every goose since 1458, is cited by this Faculty as clause 14 and stands in the Chapter's own engrossment at clause 11; that the drift is not uniform, other clauses being out by two, by three, and in one instance by nothing at all; and that the number cannot be settled by anything this Faculty holds. The work is not about geese, and the geese are not in any difficulty. It is about a numeral, and the numeral is in this Faculty's licence forms, which are printed. A completed Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is absent from this submission, and would not have helped, being a declaration against time and not against a number.

## 1. What This Enquiry Is, and What It Is Not

This work is about a numeral. It is not about geese, though geese are in it. It is not about the validity of a single reanimation this Faculty has performed, and it reaches no finding on any of them. It is not a criticism of the Capitular Statutes of 1274, which are in no difficulty and have been in none since the Michaelmas Chapter of 1274, and which do not require this Faculty's help.

The difficulty is this. This Faculty has one warrant. That warrant is a clause of those statutes. The clause is cited in this Faculty's licences, its refusals, its fee tables and its printed forms as **clause 14**. In the Chapter's own engrossment, held at **FN 1.1**, the same words stand at **clause 11**. Both numbers are in use in this University at this moment. Neither is a copyist's blunder, and the words under both are the words the Chapter enacted.

What follows is three things: the fact, set out in full at §3; the cause, which is plain and is at §4; and the consequence, which is not plain and is at §9. Between them a case, at §5, in which the difference of a single figure cost a widow nine marks, an operator six bells and a bird three years of standing, and produced no finding of any kind.

**What this enquiry is not** is a proposal to renumber anything. The proposal is obvious, has been made in this author's head four times, and is examined at §10, where it is shown to be unavailable on four independent grounds, any one of which would serve. The reader who comes to this volume expecting a fix will find a description. The description is the useful part.

A word on terms, since the whole work turns on three of them and a reader who takes any one loosely will lose the thread by §6:

| term | what it means here | what it is not |
|---|---|---|
| the **warrant** | the clause of 1274 that gives this Faculty leave to act where a bird is left, lost or unclaimed | the Faculty's charter of 1458, which is a licence to teach and says nothing about birds |
| the **drift** | the difference between a clause's number in the Chapter's book and the number it is cited at elsewhere | an error; the drift is not an error and has no author |
| the **offset** | the size of that difference, stated as a figure | a constant; §3 shows that it is not one |

The offset is not a constant. That is the finding on which everything else in this volume rests, and it is the reason the matter cannot be repaired with a pencil.

## 2. The Warrant, and the Words Under It

The Chapter's clause, as engrossed at **FN 1.1 / — 1274**, reads:

> **11. Of geese.** No goose shall be raised from the dead within the precincts. And this is not for any cause touching the art, which the Chapter leaves to the schools, but upon the law: for a goose is a tenant, holding of the Chapter at a rent of one egg, and the Chapter does not disturb its tenants.

The clause immediately following is the same statute's whole answer to the question the first clause raises:

> **12.** That the same is not to be said of hens, which hold nothing.

These two clauses, and no others, are the Faculty's ground. Everything this Faculty has refused since 1458 it has refused under clause 11, and everything it has licensed it has licensed in the space clause 12 leaves. The exemption is legal and not magical, a point established at **AN 2.3** §3 and confirmed at **AN 3.1** §9, and no rite, fee, indemnity or premium will obtain it.

The second clause the Faculty lives on is the clause of lapse, by which a thing left with the Library and not claimed falls to the finder in the eleventh year. In the Chapter's book that is clause 17, with the counting rule at 18 and the seal at 19. The Faculty cites all three as clause 19, and has done so long enough that the figure appears on the licence itself:

> LICENCE AN/4, No. 1, issued under clause 19 of the Capitular Statutes of 1274, upon the application of the keeper …

That is the sentence a licensed keeper has held in her hand since the fourth of November 1497. It cites a clause which, in the Chapter's numbering, forbids an officer to break a seal to discover what has lapsed. The proposition it is cited for — that an unclaimed deposit falls to a finder — is clause 17. Both propositions are true. They are not the same clause, and the licence does not say which is meant, and the licence is the document the keeper has.

## 3. The Drift, Set Out

The table below sets every proposition this Faculty, the Catalogue, or the Registry has cited to a clause of 1274 against the number that proposition actually carries in the Chapter's engrossment at **FN 1.1**. It was compiled over two terms from the licence register, the Faculty's printed forms, the Catalogue, and the Dean's memorandum at **EA 6.1**. Every entry is sourced. There are no gaps in it, because there is nothing else to source.

| The proposition | In the Chapter's book | Cited at, and by whom | Offset |
|---|---|---|---|
| No goose shall be raised from the dead within the precincts | **11** | **14** — Crowe, *AN 2.3*, at §3, at §6 step 1, at §11, and in the fee table | +3 |
| A deposit unclaimed eleven years lapses to the finder | **17** | **19** — the Catalogue, *Outstanding Matters*; the Dean, *EA 6.1* §7 and §36 | +2 |
| The eleventh year is counted from the Epiphany following the deposit | **18** | not cited out of doors; the Registry computes it without naming it | — |
| No officer shall break a seal to discover what has lapsed | **19** | **22** — the Dean's table, *EA 6.1* §2, entry 3 | +3 |
| What has lapsed is opened by the finder, and privately | **19** | **19** — the Dean, *EA 6.1* §57 | 0 |
| The finder is the officer in custody at the moment of lapse | **not in the book at any number** | **20** — the Dean, *EA 6.1* §2, entry 2, and §47 | — |

Four things are to be taken from this table, and the fourth is the one that ends the matter.

**First, the drift is old.** The goose clause was cited as 14 in 1499, in a printed volume, by the Faculty's senior author, in five separate places, in a work whose whole argument depends upon the citation being traceable. It was cited as 14 in the licence form before that, since the form was drafted with the volume.

**Second, the drift is not confined to this Faculty.** The Catalogue carries the lapse at 19. The Dean carries it at 19 twice. The Registry computes the Epiphany rule without ever naming its number, which the author takes to be prudent and not accidental.

**Third, the offsets differ.** The goose is out by three. The lapse is out by two. The finder's duty to open is out by nothing. A reader who assumed a uniform shift — who took a pencil, added three, and corrected his own citations — would fix the goose and break the lapse. This is the trap at §8 step 4, and the author fell into it in the first issue, in Michaelmas, and is the reason this edition exists.

**Fourth, one proposition cited to a clause is not in the statute at all.** That the finder is the officer in custody at the moment of lapse is a good proposition. It is the working rule of the Library and of the Registry, it produces the right answer in every case tried, and it is not a clause of the Capitular Statutes of 1274. The Chapter's clause 20 says something else entirely: that the lapse shall not run against the Chapter, nor against a person in the Chapter's service, nor against a person whose death the Chapter has no notice of. The proposition at 20 is, on the evidence of this table, an inference from practice that has acquired a number by standing at a desk long enough. It is not the first time that has happened in this University, and it will not be the last.

## 4. How the Numbering Came to Be So

The cause is not a mystery and is stated in the Chapter's own book, in the Library's description of it at **FN 1.1**: the fair engrossment is in the hand of a Chapter scribe signing *W. de Aldre*, and **the numbering is in a second hand throughout**. The engrosser wrote the words. Someone else wrote the figures.

That second hand also **interrupts** the numbering. The eighth and ninth leaves are in a later hand, contradict the sequence they fall into, and are the two clauses the Registry has relied upon most heavily. Clauses 9 and 10 — the reservation of the room next the river, and its accounting as a room of the Library — are marked in the text as being in that later hand, and the Library's own note against them records that they are not in the roll of 1401 and are not confirmed.

Three insertions into a sequence numbered by a second hand will shift everything below them by three. Two will shift it by two. One will shift it by one. The Chapter's book shows insertions at more than one point, at more than one date, and the leaves are of different vellum with later sewing, as the Conservator found in 1501 and recorded. **The offset is therefore not a constant because the insertions were not a single event.** A clause written before 1274 and a clause added after it can carry the same figure in two traditions, and both can be right, and neither can be proved wrong without the Chapter producing the roll of confirmations, which stops at 1401 and which the Chapter has never explained and this Library has never asked after.

There is a further complication which the author raises only to set aside, since it cannot be worked. The clauses from 13 to 18 are in Norman French and the rest in Latin. Whether the numbering runs across both, or restarts, or was applied to a Latin original before the French clauses were engrossed, cannot be determined from the leaf, because the figures are in the second hand and the second hand is consistent throughout and therefore tells nothing.

The author put one question to the Chapter, in writing, on the eleventh of Fifthmonth 1503, and its answer is at §7.

## 5. The Case of the Two Clause Elevens: Nethercot, 1493–1496

On the ninth of Wintermonth 1493 **Alice Wren** of Nethercot, a widow holding a garth of a rood and a half on the south side of the precinct, applied to this Faculty to have a bird reanimated. Her application was entered on the form then in use, which asks, in its fourth box, whether the bird is exempt, and directs the applicant to the statute.

Alice Wren had consulted the Chapter's book. She was entitled to: the statutes were read at the rent-day, and she had been present at two rent-days. She entered in the fourth box: **exempt, clause 11**.

She was correct. Clause 11 is the goose clause, and her bird was a goose.

The Faculty's clerk, working from the Faculty's copy, in which the goose clause stands at 14, read the entry as a reference to the Faculty's clause 11. The Faculty's clause 11 is not about raising the dead. It is the Chapter's clause 8, and it reads: *That no beast of the field, the yard, or the water shall be distrained upon for the debts of a scholar, if the beast be the whole of the scholar's substance.* It concerns distress, and it concerns a scholar, and Alice Wren was neither. The clerk read a clause about beasts and debt, found in it no exemption of any bird from any rite, concluded that no exemption had been claimed, and issued the licence on the thirtieth of Wintermonth 1493, the fee of 6 bells having been paid. The author notes that the clause came within one word of being the right one, the bird being a beast of the yard, and that this is what makes the cause useful.

The operator who performed the rite was **John Merivel**, a licensed operator of the second class, of the Holloway, who had performed eleven rites and passed his inspection twice. The bird stood at the seventh minute, took the mash at the ninth, and was entered in the register as *stock of the yard, ordinary*, the entry being made by the Inspector of Standing Stock, who inspects the byre and not the bird. She was called **Nell**.

She lay two hundred and six eggs in three seasons. The widow kept her, and no one objected.

The rent-warden walked the tenancies in the late spring of 1496, between Easter and Whitsun, with the rent-roll and no other man's opinion, as clause 16 requires. He found Nell. He read the licence. The licence cited clause 19 for the Faculty's authority and recorded no exemption, and the Chapter's clause 11 was on its own book and unbroken, and the warden found that a tenant of the Chapter, holding at a rent of one egg, had been maintained in a state of death at the suit of its keeper for two years and eleven months.

The Chapter sat at Candlemas 1497. The penalty under clause 11 is nine marks. The Faculty's additional charge for the unlicensed years could not be levied, because the Faculty's charge was computed under clause 14, and the Chapter, reading its own clause 14, found that it concerned rooms.

> **The Chapter's minute, Candlemas 1497, entered by the clerk within eight days as clause 5 requires:**
>
> *Upon the bird of Alice Wren of Nethercot: that the bird is a goose; that a goose holds of the Chapter at one egg; that the bird has been held these three years in a state against the peace of the Chapter; that the widow pays nine marks. And upon the Faculty's second charge, that the clause it has pleaded is a clause about rooms, and that the Chapter will not hear a charge laid under a clause concerning rooms in a cause concerning a goose. And upon the licence, that it is void as to its recital and good as to its bird, the bird being dead either way and the Chapter not being the party who made her so. And that the warden is thanked for the walk.*

The widow paid the nine marks in coin and two pecks of barley besides. Clause 24 provides that no fine shall be paid in kind save the fine of a goose, which is an egg. The Chapter held that an egg could not be had from a dead goose, that two pecks of barley was an egg at the Michaelmas price, and that it had not departed from the clause but had found the clause to be difficult in the winter. The barley was taken. The author records that this is the only ruling in the cause that any later court has followed. Merivel was fined four bells for the entry and two for the appeal, which he withdrew before finding, the fee for a withdrawal being the same as the fee for a finding and smaller than the fee for neither.

The bird's standing afterwards is the part of the cause this Faculty cites and does not explain. She was unlicensed under the Chapter and exempt under the Chapter, and the two findings were entered in the same minute and are both good. She was not taken. She was not licensed. She continued to lay. In the spring of 1500 she was found dead in the garth, and the Chapter's minute of that sitting reads, in full: *the goose of Nethercot is dead; no cause; the rent of one egg is paid by the tenant and not by the bird, and was paid.*

The whole of the loss in that cause — nine marks, six bells, two years of a licensed bird, and three years of a Faculty's standing — is attributable to a single figure in a fourth box on a form, and to the fact that two clauses in the same statute are called eleven.

## 6. Three Clauses Cited As One

The lapse cluster deserves separate treatment, because there the drift is not between two bodies but inside one document.

The Dean's memorandum at **EA 6.1** cites clause 19 four times, and cites it for three propositions.

At §7 he writes that clause 19 is the operative clause and is not in dispute, the only proposition on which the Registry and the Under-Librarian agree, and the proposition there is that an unclaimed deposit of eleven years falls to the finder. That is the Chapter's **clause 17**.

At §36 he writes that the crate will be the Under-Librarian's in 1505, when clause 19 makes him the finder. That is again the Chapter's **clause 17**.

At §57 he writes that the officer in whose custody the deposit lies will then be obliged by clause 19 to open it. That is the Chapter's **clause 19**, which says that what is lapsed shall be opened only by the finder, and privately.

Three citations, one figure, two clauses, one document, and no contradiction anywhere in it, because both propositions are true and the figure carries both. The Dean's table at §2 then lists a third entry, clause 22, for the proposition that the lapse is certified by the officer to whom the deposit does not belong, and calls that clause a solecism and treats it at §6 to §8. The Chapter's clause 22 is that a scholar who strikes a bell out of order pays twopence, and a scholar who strikes it twice pays nothing, the second being the offence.

The author's finding on this cluster is narrow and is not a criticism of the Dean, whose memorandum is careful and whose conclusion that the matter is not before him is the only conclusion available to anybody. The finding is this: **where one figure carries two clauses, a reader cannot tell which clause an instrument operates under, and an instrument whose operating clause cannot be identified cannot be pleaded.** The Dean did not need to plead his. It is a memorandum, and it decides nothing, and that is its virtue. A licence has to be pleaded. A licence decides something, and the licence at §5 was pleaded, and it was pleaded wrong.

## 7. The Objection of the Chapter, and the Reply

The author put one question to the Chapter on the eleventh of Fifthmonth 1503, in writing, addressed to the rent-warden, in these terms: *whether the clause of geese is clause 11 or clause 14, and by what roll the number is to be taken.*

The answer came on the second of Harvestmonth, in the hand of the clerk and not of the warden, and reads in full:

> The Chapter has considered your question and answers that the numbers are not the statute. The words are the statute. You have the words. The Chapter does not amend a number, the number being nothing, and a Chapter that amended a number would be understood to have amended the clause.

The first sentence is right and the second is the whole difficulty.

The Chapter's own clause 3 provides that no clause shall be pleaded in a court of the University until read at two sittings. A clause is read at a sitting by its number. The clerk reads a number. Where two clauses in the same book carry the same number, the clerk's reading is ambiguous, and a clause read ambiguously at two sittings has been read twice and pleaded once, which is not the same thing. The Chapter's answer that the words are the statute is a answer to a different question: it tells the author what governs, and does not tell him how to get it into a court.

The author's reply, which he does not expect the Chapter to read, is that the words are indeed the statute and that the words are not in dispute anywhere in this volume. Nobody in this University thinks a goose may be raised. Nobody thinks a hen may not. The words have never been argued about in two hundred and thirty years. What is argued, and what cost Alice Wren nine marks, is which line of the page a clerk is to write a figure against.

The Chapter's clause 36 answers this better than the author can: *that the Chapter shall answer an appeal in writing, an answer not in writing being no answer, and that the Chapter's practice is accordingly to answer nothing.* The Chapter has answered this author's question in writing, and the answer is that numbers are nothing, and the answer is not in writing in the sense the clause requires, and the practice stands.

## 8. Procedure: How the Number of a Clause Is to Be Determined

The following is the procedure this author has worked out for establishing the number of a clause of the Capitular Statutes of 1274, in the order in which it must be attempted. It is set out in full because it is the only complete account of the matter in print, and because a reader who attempts it will arrive at §8 step 6 in about a day and a half and will then understand this volume better than any review of it could give him. **He should not attempt it.**

1. **Establish which proposition is in issue.** Not which clause — which proposition. Write it out in the reader's own words. A citation error is invisible when the citation is read as a label and unavoidable when it is read as a claim.
2. **Find the proposition in the Chapter's book at FN 1.1.** Read the clause. Note its number. Note the language it is in, since the French clauses run 13 to 18 and the numbering across that boundary cannot be verified.
3. **Find every citation of that proposition outside the book.** The Faculty's forms, its licence register, the Catalogue, the Dean's memoranda, the Registry's computations, and any volume of this shelf that has leaned on the clause. Do not stop at the first agreement. The author's first issue stopped at the first agreement and was corrected by a reader who did not.
4. **Test for a uniform offset.** Subtract. If every difference is the same figure, a single correction will serve, and the reader is invited to make it and to bring the result to the author. **The test fails.** The goose is out by three, the lapse by two, the finder's duty by nothing. A uniform correction fixes one clause and breaks two.
5. **Ask the Chapter.** The question goes to the rent-warden, in writing, and comes back from the clerk. The answer is at §7 and is that numbers are nothing. The author does not advise the question, having asked it, the fee being one bell and the wait a hundred and thirteen days.
6. **Ask the Registry to determine the number by ruling.** The Registry will not determine a number in the Chapter's book, the Chapter's book being the Chapter's and the Registry's authority running to its own series. The Registry's position, given orally at the counter in Harvestmonth 1503 and not entered, is that the Registry does not know what number the clause is and has never needed to.
7. **Cite both numbers, always both, in the form** *clause 11 (14)*, **and state in the first line which is the Chapter's.** This is the only step in the procedure that works, and it works by not resolving the difficulty. It is adopted at §11.

The author notes that a reader who has reached step 7 will observe that the procedure ends where it began, with the proposition, and that the number was never needed. That is true, and it is the Chapter's answer at §7 restated from the other side. It is not much use against a printed licence form.

## 9. What Becomes of the Faculty

The Faculty of Applied Necromancy has been Provisional since 1458. The word stands in its charter, its calendar, its signage and its stationery, and it describes neither the Faculty's competence, which is examined annually, nor its curriculum, but the fact that the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork has never brought the charter to a vote.

Confirmation requires the Faculty to plead its warrant. To plead a warrant is to name a clause and read it at a sitting. The Faculty's warrant is a clause with two numbers, and the Faculty cannot say which it is pleading without saying that the other is not, and the other is in the Chapter's book and is about rooms.

The Faculty's position, put by its Reader in Minor Rites and recorded here as the Faculty's and not the author's, is that the drift is immaterial: that the words govern, that the Chapter has said so, and that no cause in this Faculty's history has turned on a number.

That position is contradicted by §5 of this volume, and the Faculty has asked that the contradiction be entered, and it is entered here, in the Faculty's own words, which are: *the cause at Nethercot turned on a number and the Faculty lost a bird it should have refused, and the position is still the Faculty's, the Faculty having lost on the number and won on the bird.* The author records that this is not a position and declines to call it one.

Two consequences follow and neither is remediable by this volume.

**The first is that the Faculty will remain Provisional.** Not because of the drift, and this is not the place to say why it will remain Provisional. The drift is one of several things, and it is not the largest, and the author does not wish the table at §3 to be read as a case against the Faculty. It is a case against the numbering, and the numbering is not the Faculty's either.

**The second is that the licence forms are printed.** Form AN/4 carries the figure 19 in its recital. The form was drafted in 1461, printed in a run of two hundred, of which one hundred and ninety-nine burned in the press fire of 1469, and the form is the document a keeper holds. A corrected form cannot be printed because the Faculty cannot obtain a form, and a corrected recital on an unobtainable form is a correction to a document that does not exist. Every licence that issues from this University will therefore recite clause 19 for the next however long, and the recital will be wrong in one of its two senses in every case, and the licences will be good.

## 10. The Proposal, and Why It Is Not Made

The obvious remedy is to renumber: to take the Chapter's book as authoritative, to add the offsets, and to correct the Faculty's forms, the Catalogue and the shelf. It is a Saturday's work. It has been considered four times, in 1496, 1499, 1501 and 1503, and it is not available, on four independent grounds, any one of which would serve.

1. **Clause 32.** These statutes may be amended only at Michaelmas, only by seven of the nine, and only after the clause proposed has stood a year on the roll unamended. A renumbering is an amendment, since the Chapter's own answer at §7 holds that a Chapter which amended a number would be understood to have amended the clause.
2. **Clause 33.** A clause once proposed and withdrawn may not return within the year, and a clause twice withdrawn not until the ninth year. The renumbering was proposed in 1496 and withdrawn in 1496, on the ground at 3. It was proposed again in 1499 and withdrawn again, on the same ground. It is therefore out until 1508.
3. **The Chapter will not amend a number, the number being nothing.** This is the Chapter's own answer and it is conclusive on the point. A body that holds a thing to be nothing will not move it, and is right not to, and cannot be argued out of a position it has stated in nine words.
4. **The Classification of 1388 allows no alteration, and this Faculty has had the Classification described to it.** The Classification of 1388 is retained without alteration out of respect for its author's memory. A Faculty that has petitioned yearly since 1496 for a form it cannot obtain is not in a position to ask the shelf to move a number, and has not asked.

The proposal is therefore not made. It is recorded here as considered, on four grounds, and as not made, which is the second most useful thing that can be done with a proposal. The first is to make it, and that is unavailable.

## 11. What the Reader Is to Do

Cite both numbers, always both, and put the Chapter's first: **clause 11 (14)**. State in the first line of the citation which is the Chapter's and which is the Faculty's, and do not let the reader work it out. This is the form adopted throughout this volume and it is the only form the author can recommend.

Three instructions follow from it, and they are small, and they are the whole of this volume's practical output:

- **Never cite a clause of 1274 by number alone.** If the number is all a reader has, the reader has nothing. Cite the proposition and the number, in that order.
- **Where a form carries a number the reader cannot change, cite the form and not the clause.** A licence is a licence. The author's phrase for it, taken from the Chapter's minute at §5, is that the recital is void and the bird is good, and it will serve in every case this Faculty has.
- **Do not correct a citation in a printed volume by hand.** The first issue of this work was corrected by hand after the press, in four copies, each differently, and the difference between those four copies is a drift of the author's own making and is the reason this edition was set again. A pencil correction to a printed number creates a fifth tradition. Use the form above and let the number stand.

The author expects this volume to be cited for its table and read for its case, and expects neither to change anything, and has written it so that the table will still be accurate in a hundred years, which is the most the author claims for it.

*Errata slip, pasted:* The table at §3 was set correctly in the first issue and corrected wrongly by the author's own hand in four copies, the author having assumed a uniform offset of three. The lapse is out by two. The finder's duty is out by nothing. The first issue is withdrawn and the author apologises to the four readers who hold it, and asks that they not correct it.

*Examiner's note:* The candidate has established a fact of no use to anybody and established it thoroughly. The case at §5 is the best thing in the volume and is not the candidate's case, being the Chapter's. The remarks at §9 on the Faculty's status are to be removed. They are not removed. Passed.

*Note of the Faculty of Applied Necromancy (Provisional), in the Reader's hand:* The Faculty does not adopt the finding at §3, holds the position at §9 as its own, and has asked that the contradiction be entered as it is entered. The Faculty has also asked that the goose-feather at p. 34 be removed, and the Library has declined.
