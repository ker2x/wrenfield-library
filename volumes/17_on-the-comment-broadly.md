# On the Comment "Broadly."
### A philological study of forty years of second-reader annotation in the Faculty of Jinxcraft & Minor Maleficence, with a census of the seven variants, the two interrogative cases, and a finding

## Abstract (as filed)

This thesis presents a census of the second-reader comments of Professor Emeritus Q. Ashgrove-Vane for 1425–1464: 214 comments, recovered from the examination counterfoils of this University and tabulated by variant, decade, faculty and legibility. It identifies the two candidates annotated "Broadly?" and establishes that one of them failed. Why is not established; the finding that nobody knows is defended at §7 against four explanations. The absence of a completed Form 7B from both files is recorded at §5 and is not pursued.

## 1. The Corpus and Its Recovery

The examination files are kept in the Registry, which files by series; series R was reordered on 6 March 1487. The comments survive because they were written in the counterfoils, which were bound separately, in quires of twelve, and were not moved. Of the 228 reports returned between the Michaelmas term of 1425 and the Candlemas term of 1464, 214 carry a mark in the second reader's hand; the other fourteen were returned unannotated and are excluded, the census being a census of comments.

Marks that are not the second reader's are recorded as contamination: a fingermark, a pressed leaf, a wax stain, a clerk's tick. The pressed leaf, at file 1431/19, is a catkin, and the Faculty of Agricultural Curses declines to say whose.

## 2. The Seven Tests by Which a Comment Is Counted, Read and Graded

The census was made under seven tests, applied in order. The order is not arbitrary: a reader who begins at the third test has certified a forgery, which this Faculty has done twice, in 1466 and in 1499, paying the candidates' costs, eleven bells and nine bells.

1. **Presence.** The annotation space is the verso of the report cover, between the examiner's note and the fold; a mark must be present there. A mark in the candidate's own text is not a comment, however much it resembles one.
2. **Hand.** The mark must agree with the second reader's specimen, twenty-two signed sheets deposited in the Registry in 1441 and never consulted by the Registry. Disagreement excludes the mark.
3. **Position.** The mark must follow the examiner's note and not interrupt it. Two comments sit above the note; they are counted, and noted as irregular.
4. **Ink.** Oak-gall, of the recipe printed at §0 of the *Principles* (3rd ed., the good one; JM 1.1). A comment in lamp-black is a copy, counted only where the Registry's duplicate stamp is present.
5. **Word-status.** The mark must be capable of being read as a word. The test is form, not sense: a word must be a word before its sense can be disputed, and this Faculty has always disputed the sense first.
6. **Legibility of the leaf.** Where the word cannot be recovered under test 5 the comment is graded [illegible]; where the annotation leaf is absent it is graded "the page torn out", and measured at the gutter.
7. **Identity of the file.** The counterfoil must carry its file number. Two comments are counted twice in the Registry's index and once here.

## 5. The Two Reports Annotated "Broadly?"

Two reports carry the interrogative form, and no third. A. Alderney read in 1439 in Locomotion on threshold gait, and passed, with the note *sufficient, narrowly*. E. Bray read in 1448 in Harmless Curses on deferred curses, and failed, with the note *not reconciled to the literature*.

The two marks differ in one measurable respect. In Alderney's file the question mark has a point beneath it, pricked through from the reverse of the leaf, as though the point had been made twice; in Bray's file the question mark stands alone. There is no other difference in ink, pen or position.

An examiner's note of one word is a decision; with a question mark it is a decision left in the room, and the candidate who fails inherits the room. Form 7B, required for every thesis and missing from every thesis in this library, is missing from both files; its absence tells us nothing, which is a finding, and it is stated here to prevent another thesis on the subject.

## 6. The Case of E. Bray, 1448

Emmeline Bray, of the Tenterfields, matriculated at Michaelmas 1442, took the B.A.B. in 1445 and the M.M.M. in 1446, and was admitted to the doctorate in the Faculty of Harmless & Decorative Curses the same year, on the Tenterfield Exhibition, which she held for four years and repaid in the fifth as a condition of the award. She submitted her thesis, *On Deferred Curses, Being Those Which Take Effect After the Death of the Curse-Maker*, on 30 November 1447: eighty-eight pages, six plates, in the hand of a copyist, which the Faculty permitted and afterwards regretted.

Her viva was held on 14 February 1448 in the small examination room off Bell Court, from the second bell, running three hours. The examiners were Dr Alderick Trant, of her own faculty, and Dr Josceline Marle, of Chronology, the external appointment being customary when a thesis treats of anything taking effect after a death; the second reader was the Professor Emeritus. She was examined on the nine items of her bibliography, two of which were in the press at the viva and appeared in 1449. She asked leave to reconcile the literature in writing afterwards and was told that reconciliation is done before, not after. Her mark schedule totalled 61 of 100 against a pass line of 60; the board minuted a pass at its first session and reported a failure at its second, the report carrying the interrogative above the note *not reconciled to the literature*.

The degree was withheld on 20 February 1448, and resubmission was permitted within two years on payment of a fee of 2 marks. She paid in four instalments, the third short by one clipped bell, made good in 1451; she did not resubmit, and the degree was not taken.

*Registry's note, in the margin of the fee book, in the clerk's hand:* "Fee 2 marks, taken in bells, being 40; the fee is for the re-convening of the board and not for the reading of the thesis, which is not read twice; not returnable where the candidate does not return."

She kept a school. It stood in the Tenterfields, teaching letters, ciphering and the safe handling of small jinxes to the daughters of the quarter, under a rule that no curse be set on a Wednesday. It was licensed in 1453 and inspected twice, both inspections finding it orderly and not to be enquired into further. She wrote to the Faculty twice: on 4 June 1452, asking whether the fee was returnable if the board did not sit, and on 11 October 1459, asking that her report be read aloud to her. She was answered once, on 22 June 1452, in four lines the Registry declines to read as a refusal; the second letter is endorsed *no further action* by a clerk whose initials are not in the register of clerks.

Her manuscript notes on deferred curses are cited by Mowbray at HC 1.1 / M89 1496, which describes them as not held by the Library; Mowbray's taxonomy arrives at the arrangement Bray proposed at her viva, which is a finding and not a reconciliation.

## 7. Why Nobody Knows: Four Explanations Taken and Discarded

The thesis exists to establish why one of the two interrogative candidates failed, and establishes instead that the question cannot be answered.

The first explanation is the schedule: Bray's marks stood one above the pass line, and a board that minutes a pass and reports a failure may be read as having corrected itself. It is unsupported, the minute and the schedule being in the same hand and ink, and the hand being the clerk's, who minuted every pass of 1448 in the same words.

The second is the Harmless Jurisdiction Question: a deferred curse may cause no harm, and a jinx causing no harm is not, on Jinxcraft's reading, a curse. It is unsupported, the question not being raised in the minute, and it would apply equally to Alderney, who read in Locomotion, where a threshold gait is a gait and not a curse, and who passed.

The third is attendance: Bray attended all three sessions and signed the roll at each, the roll being signed in the same hand at all three, and that hand being the clerk's, who signed for the absent, as clerks do.

The fourth is the weather: on 14 February 1448 the Yarrow was in spate and the external examiner was two hours late, and a board that sits late sits briefly. It is unsupported, Alderney's viva of 1439 having been held in the fog that closed the Low Town for three days, and Alderney having passed with the note *sufficient, narrowly*, which is not brief at all.

Three examiners were thought to survive. The author interviewed Dr Josceline Marle, then ninety-one, who answered every question about the weather and none about the candidate, and the Registry's clerk for 1448, retired, who produced the fee book and asked to be quoted only on the fee. The third died before the interview. The finding is that nobody knows.

## 8. The Letter of 1471

The Registry holds one letter from the Professor Emeritus concerning his own comments. It was found folded twice inside the counterfoils of series J, at file 1461/7, sealed with his seal, a bell above a closed book, addressed to the Clerk of the Registry, dated 3 October 1471, endorsed *read, no action*, and initialled. It is printed here for the first time. The Registry granted permission on Form EA/11 at a fee of 4 bells and asked that the letter not be printed in full; the permission and the request are filed together, and the author has complied with one of them.

> Sir — I am asked what I meant by a word I have written some four hundred times, and I am asked it, as I understand, by a candidate, or by the Faculty on a candidate's behalf, or by the Registry on behalf of the paper, which is the same request in a larger hand. I do not propose to say. The word is adequate to my meaning, and my meaning is the only meaning I am competent to certify. If I had meant a longer word I would have written the longer word, having the use of the alphabet. A comment is not a syllabus, and a reader is not a lecturer. I was appointed to read, and I have read. If the Faculty wants a rubric, let it compose a rubric, let it print the rubric upon the report, in the space, and I will read the rubric and write upon the report what I have always written upon the report, and the Faculty will have what it wants, which is not what it is asking for. I do not undertake to explain the interrogative. The interrogative explains itself; the candidate did not. The forms are a matter for the Registry. I have never seen a completed Form 7B, and I do not intend to be shown one. — Q. Ashgrove-Vane.

## 9. The Objection of the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork, and the Reply

The objection was filed in 1503 on Form EA/9 with a fee of 6 bells, returned unentered because the form had been amended that year, and refiled with a fee of 8 bells. It is set out in the Faculty's own words.

> The Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork has had before it the question whether a philology of one word is a subject, and finds that it is not. A subject requires a corpus, a method and a consequence; the work offered has a corpus of 214 counterfoils and a method of seven tests, and no consequence whatever. The Faculty observes that the work proposes to establish why a candidate failed and concludes that nobody knows, which is an admission and not a finding; and it advises that the work be recatalogued under Bibliography, that the author's degree not be delayed on this account, and that no further thesis on the subject be admitted, the subject being, in the Faculty's view, exhausted by its own failure.

The Dean was consulted and did not sign, which is his practice; the objection is filed under the hand of the Clerk of Appeals, who signs for him, as the Dean knows.

The author's reply was filed eleven days late, on the eighth of the ninety-nine forms for small grievances, that being the form for a complaint against a faculty's finding in a thesis (EA 1.1 / G74 1493), with a fee of 4 bells, paid. It is made in four parts. First: a word attested 214 times is not one word but a class, and a census of a class is a subject. Second: the consequence is on the books, at fol. 61 of the fee book of 1448, where 2 marks stand open, carried for fifty-six years by a Faculty that cannot say what the candidate failed to reconcile; a word that has cost a candidate two marks and a degree is a subject, and the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork is best placed to agree. Third: the request that no further theses be admitted concedes the argument, a subject exhaustible by one work being a subject with one work in it. Fourth: the advice to recatalogue is accepted in part, the volume standing at JM 1.4.

## 10. On the Ink, the Hand, and the Forty-One Millimetres

Three comments lack the dot of the *i*, the mark of a hand writing quickly at the end of a long examination; in each case the word is legible and counted. The minuscule variant is not a dash but a shortened hyphen drawn with a worn nib, matched in fourteen instances to specimen 14 in the Registry's specimen book, a nib the Professor Emeritus used from 1446 until the book was closed. Eleven comments are made with a nib that does not match, and are entered as a separate question.

The forty-one millimetres is measured from the gutter to the first fibre of the tear in each of the 47 torn leaves, and does not vary by more than a millimetre at either extreme. The instrument was the Conservator's rule, borrowed at the counter on a signature.

## 11. The Candidates' Copies of the *Principles*

Seven annotated copies of the *Principles* (3rd ed., the good one; JM 1.1) belonging to candidates in the corpus survive, of which three are in this Library. Alderney's copy carries the word *broadly* in the margin at §0 and no other annotation in forty years of ownership. Bray's copy was given to her school, where it was used until 1471 and then lost to damp, a loss the Conservator declines to quantify. A third copy was used to press the catkin at file 1431/19, a use the Library records without comment and has recorded without comment since 1472.

A fourth copy corrects the third edition toward the fourth throughout, the subject of Hasp at JM 1.6 / A84 1503, a work in which the footnotes grow longer and the text shorter. Vale, at JM 6.1 / V35 1494, uses "broadly" as a technical term for a jinx whose effect is distributed among all persons present, which the author regards as a capture of the word by a Faculty and a small catastrophe; she has written to Vale and has not been answered.

## 12. Conclusion: On the Value of an Ambiguous Corpus

The comment "Broadly." is not vague. It is a classification with the class boundaries left in the classifier's head, which is where this Faculty prefers all its boundaries to be, and which is why the assessment committee declined in 1503 to adopt the tabulation of §3 as a rubric. The corpus should be bound in one volume, read once and not consulted; the author has asked the binder for a spine and the Library for a chain, and expects the chain first, the chain being a form.

The census is closed at 214. The fee book stands open at fol. 61. The interrogative stands in two files, and only one has the point pricked through from behind. Form 7B is missing from every file in this library, and its absence has now been recorded in three theses, which the author expects to be the whole of its usefulness. The Professor Emeritus was asked once, in 1471, and answered; he is not to be asked again, and this thesis is not an exception to that rule, it is the reason for it.
