# On the Non-Existence of the Second Volume

### A Divinatory Proof, by Scrying and by Argument from Silence, that the Second Volume of the *Cantor Minor* was Never Written

- **Call number:** DP 44.9 / N66 1301
- **Author:** attributed to N. Nott; the title page is anonymous and the attribution is disputed
- **Faculty:** Divination & Improbable Prediction
- **Degree sought:** M.M.M., 1301
- **Advisor:** Master Hildebrand Ost · **Second reader:** Professor Emeritus Q. Ashgrove-Vane
- **Imprint:** Alderwick: Wrenfield Press for the University, 1301
- **Edition:** Enlarged reissue of the first impression; the second impression, here proposed and not produced, is treated at §4
- **Accession:** Wrenfield Accessions 1301.4
- **Collation:** 47 leaves, 3 plates
- **Condition:** repaired at the spine with linen thread of a later date; lower margin damp-stained from folio 25; annotated in a hand that is not the author's, and once in a hand that may be no one's
- **Form 7B:** *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*. Filed without it, as is every thesis in this Library.

## Abstract (as filed)

This thesis establishes, by the certified scrying of absences and by argument from silence, that the work universally cited as the second volume of the *Cantor Minor* was never written, notwithstanding the catalogue that lists it. It is filed without a completed Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, missing from every thesis in this Library.

## 1. The Object of Inquiry, and the Report of Its Absence

The *Cantor Minor* is in three parts. The first survives here and is consulted; the third was accessioned on the second of February 1298, received damp, dried at the fire, and has since been lost; the second has been cited in Alderwick for two hundred years. That is the whole of what this Library can say of the work, and it is not a small thing to be able to say it.

> *Library's annotation, in a later hand, written in the margin against this section:* The definition is required at the front of every work, and this one is not given, and is not to be supplied. The work is entered in the Accessions register without a subject, and the Library is content to class it as it is classed — there is nothing here for a later hand to be plain about. The exception is entered rather than taken, as at `HC 1.1`: this volume is about the absence of a part of a work, and a work described is a work present. The author has been dead two hundred years, and the reader who wants the subject may go and ask him, which is what the Faculty has done for two centuries and with what success the reader may see.
>
> *A second note, same hand, dated 1504:* The Library does not enter a second exception lightly, and enters this one because the attribution will not lie down. The register of 1301 names the depositor, at §5, as **N. Nott, of this city**; and the N. Nott of this University, Percival Nott, was nineteen years old in 1461 and has been in transit since, having been born in 1442. The Library contends for no cause, resolves nothing, and records only that this volume is filed without the declaration the volume is about, **Form 7B having been required of every thesis since the founding of the University and never once completed by this author in either of his hands**.

It is not cited because it is rare, but because there is no second volume, and this whole work is the proving that a book wanting and a book never written are not the same.

The volume was entered in the Accessions register on the eleventh of March 1301 and reported absent on the nineteenth, and the entry was not struck out. The Library keeps the entry, the only part of a lost book that can be stamped.

## 2. The Method, Which Is the Scrying of an Interval

Scrying for an absence proceeds by the interval and not the object. The diviner sets a bowl of still water over a wax impression of the shelf-mark and reads the interval: the width of the space between the volumes on either side. A book written and now lost leaves in the interval a texture torn at one end, as though the impression had been pulled; a book never written leaves a flatness recorded as a single even stroke.

The Library's second-best bowl was used throughout, the first being with the Chapter. Twelve readings were taken on Shelf 44.9 between Michaelmas 1300 and Candlemas 1301: nine flat, three torn. The torn readings were taken while the author was feverish, or between two shelf-marks rather than two volumes, or with a reader present.

## 3. The Procedure, Numbered, with the Things That Must Not Be Done
1. Lay the shelf-mark on the wax in a single impression, at the left-hand edge, the numeral uppermost. Do not trace it twice.
2. Fill the second-best bowl to the inner ring and no higher. The inner ring is scored; the outer is decoration, and readings to it are void.
3. Set the bowl on the interval, not on either volume. A reading across a volume is a reading of that volume, and returns its colophon, which is another discipline.
4. Sit with the left hand on the shelf and the right empty. An occupied right hand takes the shape of what it holds.
5. Wait for the surface to be still. Stillness is not silence; the draught disturbs a still surface and does not disturb a reading.
6. Read from the far end inward, and record the texture as flat, torn, or the crossing recorded at §9.
7. Record the hour, the bowl, the reader, and the day. A reading without an hour is a rumour.
8. On the ninth day read the same interval again, from the same mark, at the same hour. If the two readings differ, the earlier is withdrawn as withdrawn.

The following must not be done. The interval must not be measured with a rule while the bowl is set, the rule being a rival instrument. The reading must not be taken by a reader who has asked for the volume that week, since such a reader reads the interval he has been refused. The bowl must not be brought in a covered basket. No reading is to be taken on a Thursday, the shelf being checked that day by a person whose habits the author has been unable to establish.

## 4. The Accession of the Eleventh of March 1301, Told in Full

On the eleventh of March 1301, N. Nott, of this city, brought to the counter of the Wrenfield Library a volume described in the register as octavo, self-help, in a vellum wrapper, and deposited it under the reading-room rules, which permitted the deposit of a book for consultation against a refundable fee. The fee was two bells, paid in clipped bells, and the receipt is in the register in the smaller hand that begins at the fourth entry of the fragment. The author did not read the volume. He could not. There was no volume.

On the fourteenth the volume was catalogued at Shelf 44.9 and given a collation, an extent and a plate count from the wrapper, the only part of the volume ever produced. The clerks had not opened the book. On the nineteenth the volume was reported absent, in the same hand as the entry, and not as a correction: the entry stands.

At his advisor's direction the author surveyed the shelf on the twenty-first and twenty-second of March, finding seventeen volumes listed and sixteen present. He held for one day that it was a mis-shelving, and was corrected by the measurement at §11.

Between these findings sits the complaint of 1299, reproduced at §5, from a reader who asked for the volume two years before it was accessioned and was kept waiting. It is the hinge of the case: that the Library may accession a book it does not hold, and catalogue a book it has not opened, is ordinary; that a reader may be kept waiting for a book never written, twice, by two different staff, is what was done.

## 5. A Complaint of 1299, Reproduced in Full

The complaint was filed at the counter on the fourth of November 1299, in the hand of the Deputy Clerk of Complaints, the complainant adopting it before two witnesses.

> I am Brother Godfrey of the Cell of Saint Anselm in Low Town, a priest of that house forty-one years, and I make this complaint without heat, having been at the counter on the ninth, the eleventh and the sixteenth of October, and having sent my novice on the twentieth and the twenty-fourth. I was told the book was in hand. I was told the book was at the binder's. I was told on the fifth that the book was in the catalogue. I asked then to see the entry, since a catalogue entry is a promise, and the clerk said that was a matter for the Registry, and the Registry said that was a matter for the Faculty, and the Faculty said the matter was one of access and would be considered in the spring. I sit in the north aisle of our church, where the light is good until ten, and I had prepared six sheets on the sedilia chapter, and I require the second part of the *Cantor Minor* for the seventh and the ninth of those sheets, and there is no copy in the city at the Chapter or at the priory. I do not ask for the book to be fetched today. I ask that the Library write on my complaint whether the book is coming, so that I may learn another subject, or whether it is not coming, so that I may learn it with a settled mind. Either answer is acceptable to me. Being kept in the interval between them is not.

The Library's note records that *Cantor Minor*, II.7 was cited to the complainant by a clerk from memory, in the third person, and that this is the sole reported reading of the volume in the Library's history. A book read once, by a clerk, to a man who asked for it, and never written, has produced all it was required to produce. The complainant died in 1304, and the complaint was never withdrawn.

## 6. A Matter of Two Bells, and the Bursar's Reasoning

The deposit of two bells has not been returned. The author requested it at the counter on the twenty-fourth of March 1301 and was refused the same morning, and again in April and in 1302, on the ground that a fee once entered is not a fee in the hands of the Registry but a fee in the accounts, and the accounts for the year were closed at Candlemas. The matter was properly one for the Bursar, who was in the north range, at the windows, which are closed.

In the fourth week of March 1301 the author had a hearing from the Bursar, Master Osric Fen, before the catalogue clerk whose hand had made the entry. Master Fen allowed that the volume did not exist, and set out the Registry's account, which the author transcribes.

*Of the two bells, being twenty clipped bells at the counter rate, nine were consumed in the year in which they were taken: the leaf, the transcription of the entry, and the wax of the shelf-mark, the last remaining the Library's. Four were paid over as the fee of the catalogue clerk, a piece-work officer whose work on the fourteenth of March was correctly performed upon the wrapper. Seven remain in hand and cannot be set against a loss which the Library has not entered, the volume having no extent. A refund is a correction of a credit. There has been no credit. The Registry observes likewise that the volume, if refunded, would be refunded to the person who deposited it, and that no deposit has been claimed by any person of that name in respect of this volume.*

The author asked whether, had the volume existed, the two bells would have been returned on its withdrawal, and was told that they would, the withdrawal of a volume being a proper case for a refund, and such a case requiring the volume. Two bells is his largest expense in this work, exceeding the cost of the second-best bowl's water. In 1504 the Library ruled on a like case in **EA 1.1**, consistently with Master Fen; and Master Fen is dead.

## 7. The Seven Counter-Arguments, and Their Refutations

1. **That universal citation proves existence.** It proves only that the citation is universal; a work may be cited precisely by those who have never seen it, provided everyone supposes that someone else has.
2. **That the volume was written and lost.** A lost book leaves a tear and a trace: a charge in the lending register, a sewing shadow, a reader's complaint. Shelf 44.9 carries none. The complaint of 1299 is a trace of the counter, not of the book.
3. **That it was suppressed and destroyed.** Suppression leaves a decision, and decisions leave minutes. The minute-books for those years are complete, legible, and occupied with the cost of candles: eleven entries in the winter of 1301, nine being tallow.
4. **That the author never finished it, so that part exists.** A draft is a written thing, however bad, and the interval would return a texture for the worst draft imaginable. A draft is paper, and paper casts.
5. **That the author never began it, but intended to.** Intention is not a book, and the Faculty does not catalogue intentions, except in the case of Dr. Marchetti-Bell, whose *Correction* notices are signalled in a footnote.
6. **That argument from silence is not argument.** It is argument, of a modest kind, when the silence is total and the instrument sound. The author has set out at §3 the rules under which it fails.
7. **That the author's readings are unreliable.** They are, and the objection is allowed. The alternative requires a librarian, and no librarian has mis-shelved anything without noting it twice.

## 8. What Would Settle It, and Why the Library Cannot Do It

The question would be settled by one of four things: the sewing shadow of a volume removed from Shelf 44.9; a second citation with a locus no copy of Part I can supply; a minute of suppression; or the wrapper's leaf.

The wrapper was the vellum of the quire in which the entry was made: the author has compared the two with the Deputy Under-Librarian, and the grain is one grain. The leaf had no sewing shadow of its own, no paste, no turn-in, no board; it had been ruled, and the ruling does not fit the entry. All four things are accordingly unavailable, and three of them in the same way, which is that the volume did not obstruct anything.

The Library's advantage is the register. The register is not a witness but the thing witnessed. It is kept because a work may be catalogued, counted, cited and absent, and the entry is the only part of a lost book that can be stamped. That rule is stated on the wrapper of the press, and it is the reason the thesis was allowed to be written and the reason it cannot be answered.

## 10. The Objection of the Faculty of Divination, and the Reply

The Faculty of Divination & Improbable Prediction objected early and in writing, and its objection is preserved in the Faculty's roll for 1301, the Faculty being then three in number.

> The Faculty objects at the threshold and not at the conclusion. The candidate proposes to divine an absence, and an absence is not an object of divination. Where there is no thing there is no reflection, and no reflection, no reading. What the candidate has done is to measure a gap between two books and to call the measurement a divination. The Faculty does not dispute the measurement, and will send a man with a rule at its own expense. The Faculty further objects that the conclusion is not falsifiable. The candidate asserts that the volume was never written, and the Faculty can set against this no scrying of the volume, there being no volume to scry, and a proposition which cannot be put to the bowl is a proposition of the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork and should be filed there.

The author replies in three parts. First, the bowl is directed at the interval, which is a thing with a number. Second, the Faculty's remedy is the author's own: a man with a rule will find eleven and one quarter inches where seven and three eighths were required, and he has been asking for that man since March. Third, the reading which would falsify the proposition is the reading returned at iv and viii, and both are dismissed for reasons the author has entered against himself.

The Faculty's answer is not on the roll. The author has inquired three times, and has been told the Faculty is now two.

## 11. On the Shelf, Which Is Shorter Than It Should Be

Shelf 44.9 is shorter than it should be, and the difficulty is its company and not its construction.

- Shelf-list extent, 1301: seventeen volumes. Physically present: sixteen.
- Shelf as built: four feet seven and one quarter inches; occupied: four feet three and seven eighths.
- Interval between the sixteenth volume and the shelf end: eleven and one quarter inches.
- Thickness of the volumes either side: one and three eighths, and one and one half inches.
- Interval required by a volume of the reported collation: seven and three eighths inches.
- Surplus: three and seven eighths inches, unexplained in the catalogue and not explained by the wrapper, one leaf.
- Requisitions for a shorter shelf: three — 1301, 1302, 1304 — refused on the grounds that the shelf is the correct length and the volumes are short.

The shelf is the correct length. The volumes are short. The two rules did not agree by nine sixteenths of an inch. A shelf of the correct length and a shelf-list one volume longer than the shelf are not two findings. They are one finding, and the finding is the thesis.

## 12. Conclusion, and the Readership of the Second Volume

The volume was accessioned on the eleventh of March 1301 and reported absent on the nineteenth, and not struck out. Its deposit fee of two bells was not refunded, and the Registry's account of the fee, at §6, is on its own terms correct. There is a reader, Brother Godfrey, a priest of forty-one years in Low Town, who was kept waiting two years for the volume and then died, and whose complaint was never withdrawn.

The author accordingly reports that the second volume of the *Cantor Minor* was never written. He does not call it a proof, the word proof being reserved in this Faculty for propositions put to a bowl before two Fellows, one of whom must be a physician. He calls it a survey, and calls the survey complete.

The second volume has been read by more people than the first. That sentence is not the author's; he has found it in four works, in four hands, none of them early, and it is not in this volume. It is entered here so that whoever wrote it first may find it again, and so that the Library may record where it was found.
