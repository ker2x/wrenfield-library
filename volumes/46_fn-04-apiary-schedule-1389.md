# The Apiary Schedule of 1389

### A schedule of process and fees for causes brought by and against bees, engrossed and sealed, with the roll of returns 1390–1402, the table of standing, and the schedule of penalties

**Call number:** FN 4.1 / — 1389
**Class:** FN — The Foundation Collection
**Text:** English, engrossed in a Chancery hand, with the Latin forms of pleading in the margin; one hand throughout
**Date:** Sealed at Candlemas 1389, and engrossed before the seal, which is set upside down
**Custody:** Kept with the Chapter chests in the Upper Crypt; not to be produced out of doors between April and September, when the schedule is read to the apiaries
**Collation:** 4 leaves, one roll of later returns 1390–1402, and one loose leaf of forms in the clerk's hand
**Condition:** Wax spots on the second and third leaves, honey-coloured, old; the seal is cracked across and has been re-attached with a linen thread, which is irregular

## The Library's Note

The text is the schedule of the court of the apiary: the clauses sealed at Candlemas 1389, which settle what standing a hive has and what standing a single bee has, what forms a cause requires, what fees are payable and in what wax, how near a hive a writ may be served, how a settlement is published, and what is done with a bee that does not attend. It is not a treatise on bees and says nothing of their nature; it is a book of process, made because the causes were being brought and there was no schedule to bring them by. The schedule is authentic and sealed, and its roll of returns runs to 1402 complete, which is more than can be said of the returns of any other court in Alderwick. The Library can verify the seal, the hand, and the wax; it cannot verify the keeper of the apiary who attests the second clause, whose name appears nowhere else in the record, and it declines to investigate, there being no way to put a question to an apiary that would not itself be a cause here. The schedule contemplates the death of a party in terms that make the death no interruption to the cause. No Form 7B is required here, the form governing theses.

## The Schedule

### Of standing

1. That an apiary shall have standing in the courts of the University: an hive may bring a claim in its own name, suing by its keeper, and the keeper shall not be answerable for the hive's costs beyond the wax of the hive.
2. That a single bee may bring a claim on its own account, and has twice done so, and may plead by its own motion and not by a keeper; and the two causes are entered in the roll as *Apis contra* and *Apis contra*, the second being the shorter.
3. That a bee suing on its own account shall be brought to the court in a box, and the box opened by the clerk, and the bee not shaken out.
4. That a dead bee has no standing, save where the cause began in its life, and then the cause survives it, the hive standing in its place.
5. That a swarm is not a hive while it hangs, and is a hive when it settles, and the day of settlement is the day of its standing.
6. That a hive settling upon another's stand shall be its tenant at one comb in the year.
7. That all bees of one hive are one party, and a plea by one bee is a plea by the hive.

### Of forms and fees

8. That the fees are these, and are payable in wax by weight of the plaintiff except where clause 9 applies.
9. That a bee whose life is of the shortest is not to be delayed; and a cause brought by a single bee shall be entered, heard, and determined within three days of the complaint.
10. That every cause shall be entered in the form set out in the schedule of forms; a cause entered otherwise is entered at the clerk's risk, and the complainant shall swear the complaint before the keeper.
11. That a hive shall swear by its keeper, and a single bee by the clerk upon the keeper's information.
12. That the fee of entry shall be paid in wax, and the wax weighed before the clerk and not after the hearing.

### Of the thirty paces

13. That no distress shall be taken, and no writ served, within thirty paces of a hive, and that the thirty paces are measured from the stand and not from the fence; and that a writ served within the thirty paces is void, and the server fined.
14. That the paces are the server's and not the hive's, and where the two differ the Chapter's surveyor shall walk them, and his walking is final.
15. That a keeper who removes a hive to defeat the paces shall answer for it as for a contempt, and shall bring the hive back at his own cost.
16. That the paces run from the stand in every direction, and that a stand upon a wall runs from the foot of the wall and not from the coping.
17. That the court shall sit in the open air where the cause touches a hive.

### Of settlement and publication

18. That a settlement, the parties being agreed, shall be read aloud to the whole apiary, at the hives, and read twice if any hive is in swarm.
19. That the reading shall be on a fair day; a reading in rain is void and shall be read again.
20. That the reading shall be attended by the keeper of every hive named, and by the Chapter's clerk, who shall enter the names of those present and of those absent.
21. That a settlement read and not entered is not a settlement but a conversation, and that no settlement shall be written until it has been read, the writing bearing the day of the reading.

### Of a bee that does not attend

22. That a bee which does not attend upon the day, having been summoned, may be fined, and the hive shall pay the fine, and shall not be heard to say that the bee was about its business.
23. That a second default shall double the fine, and a third shall be entered as a contempt of the hive, and the hive shall be out of the court's favour for a year and a day.
24. That where a hive is in swarm upon the day, its attendance shall stand over to the next court day.
25. That the court shall rise at the first frost, and causes pending shall stand over to the spring.
26. That the keeper of the apiary shall be sworn before the court at Michaelmas, and shall hold his office five years, and shall not keep bees of his own while he holds it.
27. That no cause shall lie against an hive of more than seven years, the memory of bees being of that length.

## The matter of *Apis contra*, the second cause

The two causes brought by single bees are both in the roll of 1390–1402, the first of 1394 and the second of 1401, and the second is the shorter, as the schedule says. The second cause was brought by a single bee of the hive at the Cowgate against the keeper of the apiary, and it was entered on the 3rd of June 1401 and determined on the 5th, the bee having the shortest life and no time to spend on forms. The bee's complaint was that the keeper had moved its hive three paces westward to make room for a cart, and that the moving had put the hive within twenty-seven paces of the highway, where the writs are served. The keeper answered that the hive was his and that the paces were his to arrange. The court found that the hive is the party and the keeper the hand, and that a keeper may not by moving the hand shorten the paces of the party; it fined the keeper one mark, ordered the hive set back, and ordered the cart to go by the long way. The keeper paid the mark and set back the hive, and the bee died on the 6th, of the season and not of the cause. The entry reads, in full: *Apis contra Custodem; breve servatum; noxae non; solvit custos marcam; alvus reductus; apis mortua vi. Junii, causa superstes.* The Library notes that the cause outlived its plaintiff, as clause 4 provides, and that no further step was ever taken in it.

## The keeper objected to, and the court's reply

In the margin of the third leaf, in the keeper's own hand, beneath clause 13:

> *The paces are a foolishness. A bee flies a mile, and the court forbids a writ at thirty paces, which is a distance no bee observes and no server can measure. Let the paces be struck, and let the server serve, and let the bees come to court like any other party and answer for themselves.*

The court's reply is entered beneath, in the engrossing hand, and is undated:

> *The keeper is answered by his own hive, which came to court on the 3rd of June and pleaded, and which complained of the paces. The paces stay. If the keeper wishes them struck, let him bring a hive to ask it, and the hive shall be heard, and the keeper shall pay the fee.*

## The procedure of a cause brought by a single bee

1. The complainant bee is caught and put in a box, and the box is closed and not tied.
2. The box is carried to the clerk by the keeper's servant, who carries no other writing.
3. The clerk opens the box in the court and does not shake it out.
4. The complaint is stated by the clerk upon the keeper's information, and entered on the roll the same day.
5. The fee is weighed in wax before the clerk, and the plaintiff is put to proof where the wax is clipped.
6. The cause is heard within three days of the complaint, and shall not be continued for want of a form.
7. The bee is asked by the clerk whether it stands by the complaint, and its motion is entered as its answer.
8. The cause is determined, and the judgment read to the apiary whether the parties are agreed or not.
9. The box is returned to the hive the same day, and its door is left open.

## Apparatus

*Under-Librarian's stamp, in violet:* Consulted at the desk only. The keeper is to be admitted, and is not to bring the box.

*Annotation, in a later hand, at the head of the roll, dated 1441:* Upon the reading of clause 9 it is to be understood that the three days are counted from the complaint and not from the swearing, and that the swearing of a bee's complaint is entered as of the day of the complaint. Every cause brought by a single bee has therefore been determined before it was sworn, and the fines under clause 22 have been levied against hives for bees that were, at the hour of the fine, still alive and in the box. The court is directed to continue this practice, the causes having been well decided.

*Marginalia, in pencil, hand unidentified, against clause 13:* The server is fined and the bee is not; which is the whole of the law of Alderwick in one line.

*Conservator's line:* Honey-staining to leaves 2 and 3, stable; the seal is cracked and re-threaded, which is not our work and is not to be undone.
