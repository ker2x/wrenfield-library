## Abstract (as filed)

This paper sets out the hours, wages and moulting returns claimed by the reanimated poultry of eleven smallholdings on the Tenterfield road, and argues that a reanimated hen is a servant and not a tool. The file lacks a completed Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, as does every thesis in this Library.

## 1. What the Employment of Reanimated Poultry Is

The employment of reanimated poultry is the hiring of a bird brought back under licence to do the work of a living bird, upon such hours and for such grain as the bird and the holding agree. The bird is a bird: it moults, sickens and stops, and what it no longer does is die a second time.

First, the licence covers the bird and not the labour. The schedule of 1499 fixes what may be restored, by whom, and at what fee; it enters the bird as *standing stock* [AN 2.3 / C76 1499], the description it applies to beasts, at 6 bells a year, renewed upon the anniversary of its death. The work, the day, the rest and the wage are nowhere described in it.

Second, the labour is therefore the smallholder's to bargain for. The holding holds the licence; the bird holds the labour; the licence is not a receipt for both. Six bells bought the bird; the labour is a second transaction.

Third, a bird refuses by stopping. A hen asked for hours beyond what she will give does not argue; she sits in the nesting box or walks to the door, and if she sits again the day is lost and no remedy lies against her. A tool cannot refuse. A machine cannot grieve.

What it is not. It is not slavery: a licensed bird may not be sold or moved off the holding without the Inspector's endorsement, and a bird that stops is not punishable. It is not machinery: a machine is oiled where a bird is fed, renewed where a bird is rested, and replaced where a bird grieves. It is not the licence's business: the Faculty has twice refused to print hours in the schedule, for the reasons at §12. The holding that wants labour must buy it, from the bird.

Why it matters is the Comb. In the fortnight after the first wages return of 1501 the birds of eleven smallholdings constituted an association, took the name of the Comb, and sent to every holding a paper of rates and a refusal of the second hour after dark, quoted at §5. It could be refused but not ignored, because the birds had grasped the fact on which their case rests: that a bird who will not work is not a bird who has broken a contract but a bird who has stopped.

Terms used here are glossed as the Faculty's examiner requested at the first filing.

## 2. The First Employment Dispute of 1501

The first employment dispute of the road was heard at Michaelmas 1501 and concerned a common hen named Mattins, reanimated in October 1498 by Cassandra Crowe at the instance of William Ashgrove of Long Meadow, a holding of nine acres and four birds.

On the 3rd of October 1501 Ashgrove asked Mattins for eight hours a day, six days in the week, from the third bell to the eleventh, with the collection taken twice and the gleaning once, and offered one peck of barley weekly, the flock's resting in the same pen as before, and the whole of the moult. The offer was made aloud in the yard.

Mattins refused it. A hen refuses by sitting. On the 4th of October she entered the nesting box at the first bell, did not come out at the third, and sat until the ninth, and would not be lifted without distress. She worked the 5th. She sat again on the 11th and the 19th. Ashgrove's account for October shows 3 days entered as *stopped*.

On the 22nd of October Ashgrove presented a complaint, upon Form AN/9, in the sum of 4 bells, being the egg-days lost. The Faculty's inspector attended Long Meadow on the 27th and returned the finding on which the dispute turned: that a licensed bird has no contract, that a thing which has no contract cannot breach one, and that there was no breach before the Faculty. The inspector added that the hours had not been filed.

The Comb took up the matter on the 29th, its secretary having been informed by a duck of the neighbouring holding. The secretary's letter to Ashgrove, entered by beak-mark in the Comb's book, stated that Mattins's hours were the flock's hours and that the flock would withhold the morning collection until the matter was settled, the withheld collection standing at eleven egg-days and two pecks. It is this letter, not the sitting, that the smallholders remember, and it is the origin of the rule at §11.

The withholding cost the holding eleven egg-days and two pecks of grain, and the hearing before Dr Emeric Tallow on the 19th of November cost Ashgrove 4 bells, the fee for a complaint withdrawn before finding. The award of the 27th was that the bird owed nothing, that the holding was owed nothing, and that until a holding files its hours in advance no hours bind the bird that works them. Ashgrove had filed none, having assumed that the licence was the contract.

The settlement was entered on the 2nd of December 1501: six hours, from the second bell to the eighth; the second hour after dark not worked; the rest payable at half a peck weekly and not withheld in moult. Mattins resumed on the 4th, upon the filing of the hours. The secretary wrote that the flock was satisfied; the flock had not been consulted and was satisfied. The rule that a bird bargains by stopping, and that what it wins is the filing, is called the Mattins rule.

## 4. The Rest, and the Question of Repair

The schedule requires an hour in which the bird does nothing, which the Faculty calls the rest and the author, in the earlier work, calls the permission to stop. The Comb holds that the rest is a right and not a courtesy, falls within the working day, and may not be withheld in the moult. The Faculty holds that the bird does not tire and the rest is a kindness. The author observes that the bird does not tire because it does not repair, and a thing which does not repair is not rested by an hour but replaced by a bird. Neither party has answered this. Insurers of the Faculty's stamp have written the rest into two policies at a premium of 1 bell per bird, which is 1 bell more than the Faculty thinks it worth.

## 5. The Petition of the Comb, in Full

The document below was received at the Faculty's door on the 14th of October 1501, entered, and read. It is the Comb's foundation paper.

> To the Faculty of Applied Necromancy, Provisional, at Alderwick, from the Comb, the association of the reanimated poultry of the Tenterfield road, in the birds' interest.
>
> We are eleven holdings and one hundred and six birds, and we have worked the road since the reanimation of the winter of 1498. We are grateful for the licence, and we do not dispute the fee, though we note that it is paid in bells and our wages in grain, and that the exchange between them is conducted entirely by the person who pays us. We ask three things. First, that the hours of the day be fixed in writing before the week begins, and that no bird be asked for the second hour after dark, in which the yard is not safe and the collection is not counted. Second, that the rest be entered as ours and paid, and not withheld in the moult, in which we work and do not lay. Third, that the renewal of the licence be made upon the anniversary, which is ours, and be kept as a rest day, in which we are not asked for anything. If these three are granted we will work the season without stopping. If they are not granted we will work the season, and we will stop. We do not ask to be believed. We ask to be answered in writing by the Feast of St Cuthbert, and we will come for the answer.
>
> Entered at the Comb by the secretary, upon the 9th of October 1501, by beak-mark. There being no ink at the yard, the mark is made in the dust of the grain store and pressed on the leaf.

The paper was entered in the register under the date of receipt. It bears the Faculty's stamp in red wax and not in violet, the violet pad being at that hour in the Under-Librarian's possession; Mr Oll has noted the deficiency and has not supplied the pad. At the foot of the leaf, in a later hand, a column of the birds' trades has been added: scaring, gleaning, turning, and the walking off of beetles.

## 6. The Objection of the Smallholders, and the Reply

The objection of the smallholdings of the road was put in writing in 1502, at the instance of six holdings, and is given here in their own voice, as their clerk set it down.

> A bird is a tool. We buy the licence as we buy a spade, and we pay for the spade once and own it, and the spade does not ask us for hours. If the bird is a tool, then the hours are ours to set, and the grain is a kindness we are not obliged to give and do give, and the stopping is a breakage, which the holding bears. If the bird is not a tool, then what is it? It is not a servant, for a servant can be dismissed and sold. Show us the contract. There is none, and there can be none, because no plea can be entered for a duck. The Faculty's own inspector found as much in 1501, and if no plea can be entered for a duck, then the duck's grievance does not exist either.

The reply. The objection is a syllogism with a hole at the first step. A tool is not a thing that cannot speak; a tool is a thing the law describes as property, and the schedule of 1499 does not so describe the bird. The schedule enters the bird as standing stock, forbids its sale without endorsement, requires its presence at an annual count, and fines the holding and not the bird. A spade is not summoned to a count. And the premise that no plea can be entered for a duck is the very point: the inspector found in 1501 that a bird cannot breach a contract only after finding there is no contract, and a thing which cannot contract cannot have conveyed its labour away either. The labour is therefore not conveyed: it is bought at the door, week by week. As for courtesy, what is asked is the fourth clause of the Comb's own scale, that a bird who stops state a reason within three days, confirmed by two other birds. That is the language of a return. A machine gives no reason; a slave is not asked for one. The objection is answered by the two instruments the Faculty itself has stamped.

## 7. The Working Day of a Reanimated Bird

The procedure below is the reconciled form of the day; the Faculty's schedule does not contain it in this form, and the fourth step is the only one agreed.

1. Count the bird at the first bell and enter the count in the holding's book.
2. State the hours aloud, in the hearing of the flock, and not fewer than the hours filed.
3. Set the grain in the measure, in the bird's sight, before the work.
4. State the reason for any stoppage within three days, confirmed by two other birds.
5. Take the collection twice, and not a third time, whatever the weather.
6. Grant the rest within the day, and not at its end; it is not owed on a day the bird does not work.
7. Observe the anniversary as a rest day, and file the renewal next morning.
8. Do not take the second hour: no holding that has taken it has kept its flock a season.

The licence is void, and not merely suspended, in the following cases: the sale, pledge or hiring out of the bird without the Inspector's endorsement; its removal from the holding for twenty-eight days or more; the omission of the renewal by the eleventh day after the anniversary; work in the second hour after dark, upon any bird's complaint; an unrecorded stoppage, that is to say one not entered within the three days; the death of the operator named in the schedule, the licence lapsing to the executor and not to the bird; and the one case the Faculty prints in a smaller type than the rest — the feeding of a licensed bird upon the flesh of its own species.

## 8. The Precedent of the Goose

The Capitular Statutes of 1274 exempt geese from reanimation, and the exemption is legal and not magical [FN 1.1 / — 1274]. A goose cannot lawfully be reanimated by the Faculty's operators, by any other operator, or by stealth; the Faculty refused an application upon the point in 1499. The clause was pleaded at the first dispute of the road, Ashgrove's counsel arguing that if the Faculty's own foundation instrument draws a line across the birds by species, then the bird at issue is a chattel within the species so drawn. The argument was refused: the clause exempts geese from reanimation and not fowl from contract, and a statute limiting a remedy cannot be read to extend a status. The geese of the road have no hours, no wages and no standing — and the goose of Long Meadow attended the Comb's meeting of the 1st of November 1501 as an observer, was admitted, claims nothing, and is entered in the Comb's book as *present*.

## 9. The Stoppage of 1502, and the Fee

In the moult of 1502 the Comb's scale was put in writing and the Faculty's schedule amended in its second clause, which requires the rest to be payable and not withheld in the moult. It was won by a stoppage, and the stoppage is this paper's named failure.

At Long Meadow, on the 3rd of August 1502, the holding's four licensed birds, three hens and a cock, went into the empty pen at the second bell and did not come out. The instigator was the cock, whose name is Rowan. The holding had withheld the rest for two days of the moult and had entered the withholding as *economy*. The birds came out on the 6th. In three days the holding lost 27 egg-days and 6 pecks of collection, being 3 marks 5 bells, of which the collection was 4 bells — more, as the margin notes, than the rest would have cost a year. The ruling was given by Dr Emeric Tallow on the 3rd of September 1502, upon the amended schedule. The finding: that nothing was owed to the holding in damages, the damages being the labour of the birds and the labour being the birds'; that the withholding of the rest was a breach of the second clause; and that the bird Rowan was fined 2 bells, the fee entered in the Comb's scale for a stoppage not stated within three days, payable in grain. The flock was not broken and the licence not void. The ruling is cited as the moult case.

## 10. The Closed Shop, and the Grievance of the Comb

No licensed bird may be engaged by a holding at which no member of the Comb is kept. The rule is the Comb's own and not in the Faculty's schedule; the Faculty has been asked to make it so and has twice asked for time. A smallholder who dismisses one bird must dismiss the flock, the notice running at fourteen days, as for a familiar [DE 5.5 / P48 1497]. The fourteen days were proposed in 1502 and observed by nobody; the holdings give three.

The grievance of the Comb, entered by beak-mark and accepted by the Clerk of Appeals only after two refusals, is that wages are paid in grain while the licence fee is paid in bells, and that the exchange is conducted entirely by the employer. The Comb proposes that both be paid in grain. The Faculty's reply is that it has no grain, and the Clerk has entered the reply as *an answer within the meaning of the rule and not otherwise*. The grievance remains open, and the Comb's book records the reply at the head of each quarter's page.

## 11. The Filing of Hours, and What the Faculty Will Do

The Faculty adopted the Mattins rule at its sitting of the 8th of January 1502, and the rule is the whole of what the road has from the Faculty in this matter. It reads, in the register's words: that a holding shall file its hours at the desk before the week in which they are to be worked; that until the hours are filed no hours bind the bird; and that a bird working unfiled hours works them as grace. The Faculty has declined to print the rule in the schedule, the schedule being an instrument of standing and not of hours. A smallholder who wants to know what binds his birds must read the register at the desk, in the hours the desk is open, which are the Under-Librarian's.

## 12. Why the Licence Cannot Simply Be Amended

It has been proposed four times that the difficulties of this paper be settled by amending the licence schedule to include the hours: in 1499 by Crowe, in 1500 by the Faculty's own Forms Committee, in 1502 by the Comb's petition, and in 1503 by Dr Emeric Tallow. The proposal of 1500 is the instructive failure. The Committee reported that the schedule is an instrument of standing, that a form of standing cannot carry a term of hiring, and that a licence which fixed hours would convert the bird from standing stock into a servant by a stroke of the pen. It was referred back to the Committee, which has not sat since the 4th of March 1500. The author proposed it in 1499 and was refused.

Dean Ambrose Quill was consulted upon the wages annex, as he is consulted upon everything. His office replied that the Dean does not approve arrangements of this kind, not that he disapproves of them; the non-approval is noted and is not required, and two copies of the request upon Form EA/2, in triplicate, survive in the file. Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is absent from this file, as from every file in the Library; the author was told at the forms counter that the form in question is a form of another kind.
