# The Diary of Marigold Thistlewick
### The diary of a candidate for the M.M.M. of the Faculty of Agricultural Curses, kept through the catkin weeks of 1487; reissued at the enlarged length

**Call number:** MS 3.1 / T45 1487
**Hand:** Marigold Thistlewick's own; the last week of April is written with the left hand, the right being wrapped
**Class:** MS — Manuscripts & Personal Papers
**Dates of entries:** March – May 1487; no gap, though four leaves at the end are given to a list of remedies
**Provenance:** Deposited with the corrected proof of *Tips & Tricks on the Pollen Curse* in 1488, in the same parcel; the Library takes this for an accident and the Faculty for a claim to priority
**Collation:** 18 leaves, of which 14 written; watermark a sprig of alder
**Condition:** pollen staining through the lower margins

## The Library's Note

This is the day-book of Marigold Thistlewick, a candidate for the M.M.M. of the Faculty of Agricultural Curses, kept by her own hand through the catkin weeks of 1487: the pollen counts at the north wall, the drawing of the plates for *Tips & Tricks on the Pollen Curse*, and her own case of the curse, from which she suffered while she wrote it. It is a diary and not a treatise, and it is the record of an argument conducted through a window. The first issue printed a handful of entries and left out the counts, the fees and the bees; the omitted entries were in the leaves before it.

## The entries, kept from the 21st of March to the 30th of May 1487

**the 21st of March.** Count at the north wall, forty-one grains to the inch. I have the curse myself and my eyes are bad to-day, which is not relevant to the work and is recorded because no one else will.

**the 22nd.** Laid out the plate subjects for the season with the engraver at the Aldermarket, forty in all, of which ten go to him now and ten in April.

**the 23rd.** My window stays shut. There is no controversy in it. Mr. Oll came through at the ninth bell and opened it to the width of his hand, saying the room was close; I said the room is close because the pollen is in and the pollen is in because the window is open, and he said the glass is not a cure and went out.

**the 24th.** Count, sixty-eight. Fees: the Faculty's indemnity of four marks for a curse contracted in the course of instruction was due at the Lady Day and I paid it, though the curse was contracted at the north wall and not in any course. Receipt entered, Faculty's hand.

**the 26th.** Count, one hundred and six. Mr. Oll came through twice and opened the window twice, the second time propping it with a wedge of his own, which I have entered.

**the 2nd of April.** Mr. Oll opened the window at the fourth bell, I shut it at the fifth, he opened it at the sixth. I have written to the Deputy Under-Librarian and had no answer. Copy of the letter, which I kept:

> To Mr. Casper Nunn, Deputy Under-Librarian, from M. Thistlewick, of the Faculty of Agricultural Curses, this second day of April 1487.
> Sir, — I am a candidate of the Faculty and I work in the room with the north window, at the second table, on the plates of a book of which the Faculty has approved the subject. The window of that room is opened by Mr. Oll at his pleasure, sometimes twice in a day, and the pollen of the season enters with it to my certain hurt, I having the curse in my own person. I have asked Mr. Oll civilly to leave the window as he finds it and he has answered that the glass is not a cure. I do not ask for a ruling on the air of the room. I ask only that the window be left as it is found until the Faculty and the desk have settled the matter, and I undertake to shut it myself whenever it is opened. — Your servant, M. Thistlewick.

**the 5th of April.** The engraver's bill, first ten plates, three bells and nine clipped, payable at the quarter and not before, which is fair. He has done the ten cleanly except the fourth, where the catkin is more willow than alder, and I have said so.
**the 9th.** Count, one hundred and forty-four. Sight poor. I have moved the second table half a foot towards the press to get out of the line of the window.

**the 13th.** My plates are at the third of forty and my hands are steady in the morning, which is when I draw. I pinned the window with a wedge of my own and the wedge was taken away in the night, which is a small thing done by a large hand.

**the 19th.** The ten plates with the engraver are to come back together. The diagram of the anther is to be plate XXIX and shall be plate XXIX.

**the 22nd.** Wrote to the keeper of the hives at the north house, that the hives be moved off the river side until the plates are drawn, and had a civil answer that the bees are not to be moved in the catkin weeks, the harvest being the harvest. I have entered his answer as civil.

**the 24th.** Plate XXIX has come back as a hen's head. It is a clean engraving, and it is a hen. The engraver says the number on the plate means the plate, and I say the number means the number I gave him, and there is no use in it either way, the bee's part now hanging on a hen.

**the 25th.** The engraver's letter, which I have pinned in and kept, and which says what he has said to my face:

> Mistress Thistlewick, — The plate you call XXIX came to me with the number XXIX cut on the back of it in my own hand, and I have cut the subject that stood against XXIX in your list, which is the head of a fowl, and I have cut it well and I will not cut it again for nothing. If your list says anther, then your list and your numbering do not agree, which is a matter for you and not for me, and I have had the same trouble with the Herbology people and it is always the numbering. The hen is a good hen. Send me the anther under another number and I will do it at eleven clipped, the price of a second subject, the plate being already cut. — Yours, J. Wallow, engraver, Aldermarket.

**the 28th.** Count, one hundred and sixty-one. Sight bad all day. I had the anther redrawn by the evening and the bee's approach begun, and there is a hole in the chapter now where the anther was to have opened it.

**the 3rd of May.** Mr. Oll opened the window at the fourth bell and left it open at the fifth, and I shut it at the sixth and drew nothing. He came back at the eighth and asked was I unwell, and I said I was unwell in the way that we are all unwell in this room in this season, and he said the room does not make anyone unwell.

**the 9th of May.** Mr. Nunn's answer has come, three weeks late, and it allows the window to be opened once in the morning at the fourth bell for forty-five minutes, and shut after, and it calls the allowance an indult and charges six marks for it to the Faculty, which has refused to pay.

**the 18th of May.** Count, eighty-four. The catkins are down on the river side and Mr. Oll has opened the window twice this week, once in the morning as the indult says and once in the afternoon, which it does not.

**the 23rd of May.** Mr. Oll opened the window at the fourth bell and again at the eighth. My sight was poor all afternoon and the plate of the bee's approach is a day behind. I note the connection. He would not.

**the 30th of May.** The count is down to thirty-one. I have kept the window shut on my own side of the room for eight weeks, and if the Faculty holds its ground the rest will follow. The windows will be shut by Michaelmas. I have entered it, and the Faculty has entered it, and the porter has entered it, and the desk has not.

### The way I draw a plate in the season, which I keep to

1. Draw in the morning, before the ninth bell, the hand being steadiest then and the pollen not yet risen.
2. Work at the second table, with the window shut on my side, and set the board across the line of the light so that the glass does not throw the grains into relief.
3. Stroke the pollen in with the point of the brush, a grain at a time, six grains to the line, and never lay a wash, the wash being a lie about the size.
4. Count at the north wall at the same hour each day and enter the count against the drawing, so that a plate can be dated by its own grains.
5. Give the engraver the number and the subject on one card in the same hand, and keep a copy of the card, which I did not do in April and have done since.
6. Where a plate returns wrong, enter the wrongness and the price in the book before writing to anybody.

### The failure of plate XXIX and what it cost

The anther was to open the bee's chapter at the twenty-ninth page and it does not exist. What it cost in money is eleven clipped to have the subject cut again under another number, and three bells and nine clipped already paid on the first ten plates, of which one is a willow and one is a hen. What it cost in the work is a chapter opened by a plate that has nothing to do with it, and a delay of nine days, during which the count at the north wall rose from one hundred and forty-four to one hundred and sixty-one. The engraver holds that the numbering was mine, and the numbering was mine. What I have never been able to make him see is that the number on the back of a plate is not the number of the subject, and that a list and a set of plates can agree with one another and both be wrong.

*[Here follow four leaves of remedies, in the same hand: elecampane, honey, and a wash of alum.]*

## Apparatus

*Under-Librarian's stamp, in violet:* Windows in the north range are to remain shut. The indult of the ninth of May 1487 is spent and is not renewable. Readers suffering from the pollen are asked to read in the second reading room, where the air is worse.

*Conservator's note, 1504:* Pollen staining is stable and is not to be cleaned, the colour being evidence. The alum wash has eaten a hole through the last two remedy leaves. The engraver's letter is pinned at the twenty-fifth entry and is not to be unpinned.

*Pencil note, in Miss Thistlewick's own hand, later:* I was right about the window and right about the plates. Plate XXIX is the hen that sank the bee's chapter. As to the seventeen years the Faculty counts, I make it eighteen, and my count was of grains and not of years. The indult allowed the window for forty-five minutes in the morning, which is what the desk calls an allowance and what I call a window.
