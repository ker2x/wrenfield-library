## Abstract (as filed)

This thesis establishes a taxonomy of curses which produce no measurable harm, in four classes — decorative, deferred, aspirational and courteous — and defends the position that such curses remain curses notwithstanding their inability to injure. It classifies 214 filed cases, enters twenty-four of them in the taxonomy and two at length, and sets against them a control group of one hundred and six persons merely unfortunate, and one instance of courteous cursing, examined at length and not enjoyed. Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is absent from the submitted envelope, nor in any envelope submitted to this Faculty.

## 1. The Problem of the Harmless Curse

A curse is conventionally defined by its effect. Where the effect is absent, the discipline concludes that the curse is absent also, and the Registry closes the file with a note reading *no harm, no matter*. That is premature: it confuses the measurement of harm with the existence of the instrument, in the way that a bell never rung is not thereby a bucket.

The confusion is not idle. If the effect defines the class, a casting belongs to this Faculty only while it fails, and migrates to Jinxcraft & Minor Maleficence the moment it succeeds; a practitioner could not state her Faculty at the hour of casting, which is the hour at which the licence is required.

The corpus is every complaint of a similar shape held by the Registry between Fourthmonth 1403 and Ninthmonth 1494: 1,310 files, of which 214 described a curse cast correctly, in full, by an enrolled practitioner, upon a named subject, which returned no result. The remaining 1,096 failed at the casting and are not curses but attempts. The 214 are held in eleven bundles, tied in the Faculty's colours; one of the bundles smells of the Tenterfields. Of the 214, 96 are decorative, 64 deferred, 39 aspirational and 15 courteous, the courteous fifteen having been reclassified from decorative on the author's motion in 1494, over the Faculty's objection that an instrument cannot be classified by the temper of its caster.

## 2. The Doorstep in Low Town That Began the Study

The study began at a doorstep in Cooper's Rents, Low Town, in the autumn of 1487.

A complaint had been filed by a neighbour of settled grievance, one Aldis Rope, a cooper of that parish, who had cursed the householder at that door annually since Firstmonth 1487. The complaint was not that the curse had taken effect. It was that the curse had not, and that the householder had begun to take a pleasure in the fact. Rope asked the Faculty to determine what a curse is worth when it does nothing, and whether a man may be cursed annually at his own door by a neighbour against whom he has no complaint, the curse being audible in the street and forming, in the householder's words, "the most reliable thing in the rents".

The author attended on the ninth of Tenthmonth 1487 and took down the formula, which runs to thirty-two syllables and returns upon the speaker in its twenty-ninth; the Faculty has never counted the return a defect, because it is not one. The householder's name is not recorded, at his request, that request being the only fee he has ever paid. Nothing occurred in 1487, nor in 1488, 1489 or 1490, though the curse was cast in each year. In Eleventhmonth 1491 the householder's chimney took; the survey entered the fire as harm, which would have closed the file, and the householder objected, his chimney taking every second winter and having taken twice before the cursing began. The Faculty ruled the fire ordinary and the file remained open. Rope died in Fourthmonth 1492 of an ordinary fever; his son has not renewed the curse. Two findings came out of Cooper's Rents: a curse may be maintained by its subject and not only by its caster, if the subject is content; and the protocol had no means of distinguishing harm that followed a casting from harm that would have followed one, which produced the control group at §5.

### 3.1 The Classes

The **decorative** class is castings made for the form alone; it is the oldest in the practice, its examinations consist of recitation, and its failures are prosodic. The **deferred** class is castings held in suspension, by a stated condition or by the instrument's own latency; the mean of fourteen deferred cases is twenty-three years four months. The **aspirational** class aims at an effect the craft cannot produce, and misses; the miss and the failure being indistinguishable at the time, it is the class the Faculty is least willing to certify, and the only class whose members have sought a fee refund, all refused, the casting having been performed, the syllables correct, and the Faculty not warranting outcomes. The **courteous** class is castings made with the subject's consent, for the subject's ease, rarely recorded and never before 1493.

### 3.2 Latency, and the Register

Latency runs from the last syllable to the first observable event within the harm window; where no event occurs it is entered as *none, ever*, which the Registry has twice refused as an entry, requiring the field to be blank or numbered. The Faculty's answer, sustained on appeal in 1461, is that a roll which cannot record the absence of a result cannot record the absence of a curse. The 214 cases are scheduled by decade in eleven bundles; the decade 1483–1494 holds 71 and is twice the thickness of the others, which the Faculty attributes to the fees. The classes are entered in the register as D, F, A and C, whose headings read *Doubtful*, *Further*, *Attended to Later* and *Clerical*, which are not the classes; the author's Form 9F on the point has been ruled an amendment.

### 3.3 A Named Failure: the Double Classification of Case 173, and Its Fee

Case 173 of 1489 was cast by one Hedley Marle, a certificated practitioner of the Tenterfields, upon a competitor's stall, deferred to the day the competitor should next raise his prices. It is deferred. It is also aspirational, the craft being unable to affect prices. Marle filed it as deferred, which was correct, and was advised at Counter II that the file would close for want of an effect unless the aspirational character were admitted, which was also correct; he admitted it. The schedule charges the full filing fee for the first class and half again for the second, the second fee being for the second classification and not the second class: 2 marks 8 bells and 1 mark 4 bells, 3 marks 4 bells in all. Marle paid in Ninthmonth 1489 and complained in Tenthmonth, on Form 23D (4 bells), that he had been charged twice for one casting; the complaint was entered, refused, and entered again as an entry, giving him a second and a charge. His total outlay was 3 marks 8 bells, of which eleven clipped bells were for the form on which he complained of the fee. His remark at the counter is preserved: "I have paid for two curses and received none." The Faculty answers that he received two classifications, which are things, and one curse, which is a thing, and that the fee is for the things.

## 4. The Four Registers of Harm

Harm is sought in four registers and in no others. A complaint resting on a fifth — luck, reputation before the parish, or the opinion of the subject's mother — is entered under the nearest of the four, and the entry is the record.

| Register | What counts within the window | Standard of proof | Harm found (n = 214) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bodily | Confinement to bed three days or more; a fracture; the loss of a tooth or its use; a cough beyond eleven days | A physician's note, or two neighbours' notes | 9 |
| Financial | A loss of a quarter-mark or more; a debt called early; a beast lost, taken, or made unproductive | The household book, or the parish assessment | 14 |
| Domestic | The failure of a hearth, roof, cistern, marriage-bed, or a familiar's service | The surveyor's entry, made on the day | 22 |
| Reputational | Being named in a lecture, sermon, or the survey as unfortunate; the loss of a surety or a pew | The naming, in writing, with the date | 6 |

Harm was found within the window in fifty-one cases. In forty-eight it was held on review to be ordinary; in two it was the effect of an earlier casting by another hand; in one, Case 137, it was the effect of the curse, which had discharged, and the file was transferred to Jinxcraft & Minor Maleficence, where it remains and where the author has been refused leave to consult it. The registers are not of equal weight, and the Faculty has twice declined to make them so. Bodily harm is the heaviest and the rarest; financial harm the most claimed and least proved, households keeping books as they keep accounts; domestic harm the most reliable, the surveyor being an officer of the Faculty and the entry made on the day; reputational harm the most contentious, and the register in which the control group proved most sensitive.

## 5. The Control Group and Its Grievances

In Firstmonth 1485 the Faculty required of the author a comparison: harm sought where a curse had been cast, against harm not sought at all. One hundred and six persons were enrolled from the ordinary bad luck of Low Town and the Tenterfields, by household, in the order in which their doors were found open. Enrolment conferred nothing; it did not console and did not inform. Notice was considered and found to be a form of consolation, for which no fee was then set. The enrolled discovered their enrolment by being asked to sign for it afterwards. The group was observed for the same ninety days, by the same four registers, without a casting.

The merely unfortunate fared worse than the cursed in three registers and equally badly in the fourth. The Faculty's conclusion, which the author does not dissent from, is that a curse producing less harm than no curse produces no harm.

The group's grievances are sixty-one in number, entered in seventy-four classes, and consolidated in the ledger of 1504, where they are in their nineteenth year. Their words, as entered: "I do not object to the cough. I object to the entry" (Bodily, Form 34E, 3½ bells, 1488). "The goat was assessed as bad luck. Bad luck is not a body to be assessed" (Financial, Form 23D, 4 bells, 1486). "My father was read out as unfortunate at a lecture, and my mother was present" (Reputational, Form 47B, 6 bells, 1487). "The hearth was swept in the ordinary course and entered as curse-adjacent" (Domestic, Form 14E, 3 bells, 1486). The Faculty's reply to each is entered once: ordinary bad luck is a pre-existing condition, which is beyond the Faculty's jurisdiction, whatever else it may be within.

## 6. The Measurement of an Absence

Harm which does not occur cannot be weighed, so the method is comparative, in five steps, set out in the order in which they must be performed, which is not the order in which they are usually performed.

1. **Record the casting verbatim, with the syllables numbered, before any result is sought.** The formula is taken at the door, in the curser's own words, and numbered in the margin by the recording officer. A casting recorded after the window has opened is inadmissible; 31 of the 214 were admitted only on the affiant's oath that the formula was written that evening, which the Faculty accepts and the author does not.
2. **Fix a harm window of ninety days, and have it witnessed.** The window opens at the last syllable and closes at the ninetieth day at the same hour. It is entered in the survey book before it opens, and witnessed by the candidate's advisor, who signs the entry and does not thereafter attend. Where the advisor cannot attend, a Reader of the Faculty may sign, and the window runs to 120 days in compensation, which the Faculty concedes is arbitrary and has declined to correct.
3. **Seek harm in the four registers, and in no others.** Bodily, financial, domestic, reputational, canvassed in that order, the surveyor calling at the household on the eleventh, fortieth and ninetieth days. A register may be canvassed a fourth time if a physician's note is produced, and not otherwise.
4. **Enrol a control group of the not-cursed and merely unfortunate.** Drawn by household and not by person, from the subject's parish, in the order in which doors are found open, to a minimum of one hundred persons. The group is not informed, for the reason at §5, and the omission is entered in the survey book as an omission and not as a courtesy.
5. **Compare; where the cursed fare better than the control, close the file with relief.** A file so closed is entered as *no result*, and the entry is the Faculty's whole finding: not that no curse was cast, which the record contradicts, and not that no harm followed, which the comparison puts beyond argument, but that the curse has returned nothing and the Faculty is relieved.

Two rules attach to the fifth step. A file closed as *no result* remains open on the instrument: the curse is still running, and the Faculty does not claim the power to discharge a curse its maker cast, no maker being present to discharge it. And a curse which has returned nothing has no subject to serve and no onset to await, so its discharge cannot be procured by naming, waiting, or onset; the author has asked Jinxcraft & Minor Maleficence to state the means, and Dr Hasp has replied that the question is answered in the fourth edition, which is withdrawn. Where the cursed fare worse than the control, the file is entered *result, ordinary* and compared again at the next quarter, which costs 6 bells and is charged to the Faculty.

## 7. The Harmless Jurisdiction Question

The Faculty of Jinxcraft & Minor Maleficence has maintained since 1402 that a jinx causing no harm is not a curse at all, and that its remit therefore covers the whole of harmless casting. The claim is a land grab, elegantly drafted. It was put to the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork under cover of the following instrument, filed at Counter II on the ninth of Thirdmonth 1494, and is answered here at length because the author has been asked whether she can answer it at all.

> To the Clerk of the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork, from the Reader and Fellows of Jinxcraft & Minor Maleficence, the ninth of Thirdmonth 1494. The Faculty objects to the jurisdiction assumed by the Faculty of Harmless & Decorative Curses in the matter of castings which return no result, and does so on the ground which that Faculty has itself supplied. HC 1.1 / M89 1496 holds that a curse is constituted by its operation and not by its outcome, and that the same utterance may therefore belong to one Faculty on Monday and another on Tuesday if jurisdiction be made to depend upon consequence. The Faculty agrees, and observes the difficulty into which the argument leads when pursued. If a casting is constituted by its operation, the operation is a jinx, and jinxcraft is this Faculty's remit under the instrument of 1371 and the schedule annexed to it; and if the casting is instead constituted by the absence of an outcome, the Faculty of Harmless Curses claims jurisdiction over an absence, and an absence is not a subject matter. The Faculty further observes that a class of operations defined by the failure of their effect cannot be taught, examined, or insured. It asks that the matter be removed to this Faculty, or that the Faculty of Harmless Curses be required to state in terms what its subject matter consists of. Signed on behalf of the Faculty, C. Vale, D.P.M., Reader in Practical Maleficence; countersigned at the door of the Chamber and not within it.

Three points answer the objection.

**First, the objection's premise disposes of the objection.** JM's teaching holds that latency, not injury, defines minor maleficence, and that a jinx in good order need never discharge. A faculty which claims an instrument for its latency cannot then disclaim it for its latency. If the operation is what constitutes the jinx, the operation is complete at the last syllable, and the absence of an onset is the jinx's ordinary condition and not its boundary; the objection proves that JM's remit extends to every completed casting and then complains that it does. This Faculty claims the four classes at §3, three of which JM has never taught and one of which it has never recorded.

**Second, the roll is longer than the objection.** JM's roll of practised jinxes contains 461 entries it records as *without result*. Were absence of harm sufficient to determine jurisdiction, JM would surrender rather more than it intends, the 461 being filed, stamped and examined as jinxes, and being jinxes. The Faculty's claim is not that harmlessness determines jurisdiction; it is that harmlessness is normal in a jinx, which the 461 establish, and that a class of castings whose whole art is the form has been taught at this Faculty since 1402 under a licence never revoked.

**Third, jurisdiction follows competence.** Three centuries of decorative practice sit with HC. The Faculty holds the ornamental hand, its four traditional forms, the eleven bundles of the roll, its own surveyors, its three instrument-makers, and the only course in the ornamental hand taught in the University. JM has no plates, no instrument-makers, and no course in the ornamental hand, and its fourth edition's chapter on the subject was withdrawn with the edition. Its claim is administrative, not technical, and the instrument of 1371 annexed to JM a schedule of operations and not a schedule of results.

The *Journal of Harmless Maleficence* has been on hiatus pending clarification since 1402, its last published item a correction, and volume xii exists as galley proofs in the Restricted Stack and must not be folded. The clarification is outstanding after ninety-four years, the instrument providing that it be issued by the body that issued the hiatus. The Clerk of Appeals has entered the objection at Counter II and has not answered it. Dean Ambrose Quill has been consulted and has not approved anything.

## 8. The Courteous Curse: Case 201 in Full

In Thirdmonth 1493 Mrs Elinor Halloway of the Tenterfields, a dyer's widow of fifty-eight years, attended this Faculty and requested that her mother-in-law be cursed.

Mrs Halloway's household had contained her mother-in-law, Mrs Sabina Halloway, then eighty-one, for eleven years. The two had not quarrelled. The mother-in-law was, in the petitioner's words at the hearing, "a good woman with a long memory", who recounted the petitioner's failures at table, at the washing, and at her son's funeral, in that order and without malice, on every evening of the eleven years; and the petitioner could not answer her, and could not be angry with her, and could not sleep. She had been told that no remedy lay at law, the speech being true; that no remedy lay at physic, the speaker being well; and that a curse might be cast, and would do nothing, and that nothing was, in her case, the object of it.

Dr Wilhelmina Tarn, examiner of courteous castings, took the petition on the nineteenth of Thirdmonth and satisfied herself that the petitioner understood the curse would not harm her mother-in-law, would not silence her, and would do nothing whatever. The petitioner replied that she understood it, and did not wish her mother-in-law silenced, but wished to have it said. Consent was taken in writing from the petitioner as to her own person, the formula returning upon the speaker at its twenty-ninth syllable; consent could not be taken from the mother-in-law, who was not to be told, and was not told. The formula was recorded verbatim before the casting:

> *Nine locks, nine locks, and the ninth is mine;*
> *Eleven years, eleven years, and the eleventh is mine;*
> *Be it said at the board, be it said at the stair,*
> *Be it said at the washing, be it said at the prayer;*
> *Be the words as they were, be the words as they are,*
> *And the saying be mine, and the weight be mine,*
> *And the evening be mine, and the night be mine;*
> *Nine locks, nine locks, and the ninth is mine.*

The casting was performed at the petitioner's door on the evening of the twenty-second, before the petitioner, the surveyor and the author, and was complete in forty-one seconds. Latency was entered *immediate, harmlessly*, and the ninety-day window was fixed and witnessed by Dr Tarn. Harm was sought in the four registers and none found: the mother-in-law's cough, eleven years old, was canvassed and entered as ordinary; the household accounts were canvassed and found improved, the petitioner having slept; the hearth did not fail; and in the reputational register the petitioner was named in no lecture and lost no pew. The mother-in-law did not alter her speech, which was never the object, and the petitioner reported herself greatly comforted and paid the standard fee twice, the second payment unsolicited and entered as a gift to the Faculty's poor box.

The Faculty, on Dr Tarn's report, allowed the casting to stand. It is the only courteous cursing the Faculty has allowed to stand. Three features of it are new: consent was recorded in writing where the practice had required only the caster's attestation; the subject was the person comforted and the target another; and there was no malice anywhere in the file, which is why the Faculty found it difficult to stamp.

The examiners did not enjoy Case 201. Dr Hasp examined it for one hour and nine minutes and objected that consent converts a curse into a service, and a service is not a curse, so that the Faculty had licensed a courtesy with a fee. Dr Tarn, in the chair, asked whether the mother-in-law had consented; the candidate answered that she had not been told, that her comfort was not the object and her knowledge was not required, and that comfort is not a service rendered to the one comforted. The candidate was asked how she knew nothing had occurred, and answered that the surveyor had called three times and the household accounts were in evidence. The examiners asked leave to consider Case 201 further and did not return to it. Mrs Sabina Halloway died in Secondmonth 1494, of her age; Mrs Elinor Halloway wrote in Thirdmonth to report that her mother-in-law had died, and that she had been sorry, and that the curse had not been mentioned at the funeral or since.

## 9. The Viva and the Examiners

Four hours and twenty minutes were spent in the Regent House on the eleventh of June 1496. Examiners: Dr Wilhelmina Tarn, in the chair, for HC; Dr Corvin Hasp, external, for JM; and Professor Emeritus Q. Ashgrove-Vane, second reader, who attended and asked one question. Dr Hasp examined the definition of harm for three hours and seven minutes, the taxonomy for forty-one minutes, and Case 201 for one hour and nine minutes. The candidate maintained that the question was one of instrument rather than effect, settled for HC in 1404 by the Faculty of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork, which has recused itself ever since.

Five points were examined: whether a curse requires a subject; whether the deferred class is a class of curse or a class of patience; whether the harm window may be closed by the subject's own act, as in Case 211, where consent was withdrawn by letter; whether the control group's outcomes may be read against the cursed without informing the group, Dr Hasp holding that they may not, so that the comparison is unsound and the arithmetic sound; and whether the Faculty's classes are exhaustive, the candidate holding that they are not, four classes being the classes the roll supports and not the classes the craft contains. Professor Ashgrove-Vane asked whether the author had considered that a curse which does nothing may be doing nothing because it is not a curse; the candidate replied that she had considered it and had written her first section against it.

## 10. The Conferral, Without Form 7B

The examiners recommended conferral, on condition that the thesis be deposited as written and not corrected, a condition the candidate has kept, and which is the reason for the present impression's errors.
