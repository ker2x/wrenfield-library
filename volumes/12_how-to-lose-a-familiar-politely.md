## Abstract (as filed)

This manual sets out the etiquette by which a practitioner may release a familiar without offence or the accumulation of obligations. It treats the fourteen-day notice, the release ritual in five steps, the disposal of the collar, the distinction between a familiar that has resigned and one that has been bereaved, and the case of the cat that came back, recorded at §6 from the practitioner's own register. Consent is discussed at §14; the submission is unaccompanied by Form 7B, which is missing, as it is everywhere in this library.

## 1. The Fourteen-Day Notice

Notice of severance is given fourteen days before the intended release, in speech, to the familiar itself, and entered in the practitioner's domestic register the same hour. Fourteen days is not a requirement and never has been; the Faculty of Domestic Enchantment has declined three times to make it one. It is a courtesy, the first courtesy of the business, placed first because everything later depends on the familiar having been told.

The customary objections to the notice are three. The first is that the familiar cannot read the register; it is met by reading the entry aloud, in the room in which the familiar sleeps. The second is that the familiar is a cat, and does not count; this is not an objection but a confession, and confesses that the practitioner has decided in advance which of her household counts. The third is that the practitioner may change her mind during the fourteen days and be embarrassed before the familiar, which is the commonest reason for giving notice and the worst reason for anything.

A shortening to three days is permitted where the familiar is in extremity, the shortening noted in the register with the word *shortened*.

Where the notice is given and the familiar dies within the fourteen days, the notice lapses, the ritual is not performed, and the register is closed with the single word *lapsed*. Two such closures are recorded in this household, in 1490 and 1496.

## 2. Preliminary Enquiries Before Notice

Before notice is given, three enquiries are recommended, and are recommended as enquiries and not as conditions, since a condition would create a form, and there are forms enough.

The first concerns the familiar's age. A familiar under two years is not competent to leave in the sense that matters, and a release of such a familiar is a weaning, whatever the paperwork calls it, and is recorded in the register as a weaning.

The second concerns the household's other animals. Where the familiar is not the only one bound to the practitioner, severance of one is felt by all; in the second instance observed by the author, the remaining familiar ate nothing for nine days.

The third concerns the practitioner's reason, written down before the notice is spoken; a reason written afterwards is a justification, and justifications are for the Faculty. The commonest true reason, in the author's experience of eleven severances since 1487, is that the practitioner has grown and the familiar has not.

## 4. The Release Ritual, Set Down as It Is Spoken

The ritual is performed once. A repetition diminishes it without invalidating it. It is not a formula of power but a formula of parting. The form below is the form in this household's register for 1489, transcribed from the practice of the author's own teacher, and quoted entire because a paraphrase of a parting is a second parting.

> **The Statement.** *"I have kept you, and you have kept this house, and the arrangement between us is ended. I name the day. The day is the day you go, if you go."*
> **The Meal.** *"This is set down for you, on your own dish, and the dish is yours after. Nothing follows from the eating, and nothing from the refusal to eat."*
> **The Opening.** *"The window is open. The door stands open. I am not closing either, today."*
> **The Passage.** *"Go, if you are going. I do not call you back, and I do not say you may return at any hour, because anything said here is heard, remembered, and held, and I will not have said a thing I must then keep."*
> **The Interval.** *"I stay three minutes in this room and speak to no one, and then I am a household of one."*
>
> — *Ritual of Severance, the form of 1489, Domestic Register of Prudence Pell, entry of the 3rd of Fennuary 1489, transcribed at Appendix A. The entry notes that the familiar usually leaves during the third line, at the word open, and that no purpose is served by hurrying the second line to reach it. Of the eleven severances in this range, the familiar left at the third line on seven occasions, at the fourth on two.*

The author adds, as the Faculty's examiner required her to add, that the second line is not a meal within the meaning of the household accounts, is not to be entered in the kitchen ledger, and is not deductible.

## 5. The Rule About the Door

The opening at step three may be a window, an outer door, or both. It may not be a hatch, a cellar stair, a chimney, or an interior door opening onto another room of the same house; the four are recorded as failures of the ritual in the Faculty's returns for 1488, 1492, 1494 and 1499.

The door, where a door is used, stands open for the duration and for the three minutes after, and is closed by the practitioner's own hand only: not by a servant, not by a neighbour. Where the weather makes closing urgent, the ritual is postponed, and the fourteen days are counted afresh from the new date, the only circumstance in which the notice is begun again.

The rule exists because a familiar leaving through a door that a third party closes at the moment of passage has been shown the house does not intend the leaving. Two severances in the Faculty's returns stand as *void for closure*, the fee in each case being 1 mark 4 bells.

The day itself has an order, and the Faculty's returns are kept in it.

1. Nothing is asked of the familiar on the day: no work is given, no door is tested, and no errand is run.
2. The opening is prepared before the hour — a window, an outer door, or both — and nothing that is a hatch, a cellar stair, a chimney, or an interior door onto another room of the same house.
3. The dish is set down with the food the household eats, on the familiar's own dish, where the familiar can reach it.
4. The ritual is spoken once, in the order at §4. A repetition is entered in the register as a repetition, which is a record and not a penalty.
5. The practitioner stays three minutes in the room, speaks to no one, and does not follow.
6. The door, if it is closed at all, is closed by the practitioner's own hand, and after the three minutes.
7. The entry is made that evening: the date, the line at which the familiar left, the dish, the collar, and whether the door was left open.

A practitioner who has kept all seven and has nonetheless lost the familiar has performed the work correctly; the returns show that this is the common case and not a failure.

## 6. The Case of the Cat That Came Back, In Full

The familiar of this study was a grey brindled cat, a female, called Mecklin, seven years in the house, whose name and purchase price are entered at the front of the domestic register, that price being 3 bells 8 clipped bells.

The severance was performed on the 3rd of Fennuary 1493 at the household at Candler's Row, in Low Town, under the ritual at §4, the notice having been given on the 20th of December 1492, fourteen days exactly, the entry made the same hour and read aloud. The reason written before the notice was, in full: *the practitioner's work has gone to the desk and the familiar has not.* Mrs. Tally, the household's own woman, stood at the stair with her back turned, the only witness the schedule permits. The familiar left at the third line, at the word *open*.

She returned on the 7th of Fennuary 1493, on the fourth day, by the scullery window, which had not been opened in the interval and which the author thereafter kept unlatched for a year, a practice she does not recommend and did not repeat. She was thin. She drank, and lay on the south windowsill, which the author records without interpreting, again.

The author did three things and did not do a fourth. She set down a meal, of the familiar's usual preference, on the familiar's own dish, kept unwashed since the severance, an omission she records as an omission and not as a method. She kept the familiar in the house for eleven days, feeding it, and then ceased to keep it and ceased to feed it.

She did not repeat step four. She did not call the familiar back and did not say that it might return at any hour. Her own record of the matter, entered the same evening in the domestic register, is quoted at Appendix A, and its last sentence is this: *I did not say the words of the fourth line. I had said them once and they were held, and a thing said twice is a thing said to be kept twice.*

The collar's fate, under §3, was option ten. It hung on the hook by the scullery door from the day of the severance, unlabelled, and it hangs there at the date of this reissue. Asked twice whether the hook is not a shrine, the author has answered that the hook is a hook, and the drawer is a shrine.

## 8. Re-engagement, and Why It Is Discouraged

Re-engagement with the same familiar is discouraged, on grounds of dignity: the familiar has agreed to return, and the practitioner has agreed to nothing, so the arrangement is unequal in the familiar's favour and unstable in the practitioner's, a household that did not intend the leaving being unable to intend the staying.

Where re-engagement is undertaken, it should be recorded as a new engagement, with a new notice, a new dish, and a new collar, the old collar being retained in accordance with §3, option ten. The practitioner who re-engages without the new notice has performed a severance and a hiring in the same year and has documented neither; the Faculty's returns contain four such practitioners, of whom three were fined and one was promoted.

The author's position is that the cat that came back was not re-engaged and was not a familiar again in the sense of this manual. It was a cat that came back, and the house was glad, and no bargain was made on either side.

## 9. Resignation and Bereavement

A familiar that has **resigned** has left deliberately. The correct response is the ritual, performed briskly, and then a period of no enquiries. Enquiry after a resignation is not care; it is a second severance, and the second is the remembered one.

A familiar that has been **bereaved** has lost its own companion, is not leaving but has been left, and may remain for years, grieving something the practitioner cannot see. The correct response is the meal, the open window, and no ritual at all, a release ritual over a bereaved familiar being an answer to a question never asked. The distinction is not always available at the time, but it is always available afterwards, which is why the register is kept in the practitioner's hand.

Four signs mark the bereaved familiar: it sleeps where the lost companion slept, not where it slept before; it eats irregularly, and does not fast; it does not approach the opening, though it will sit beside it; and it answers to its own name. The third sign is the reliable one.

## 10. The Objection of the Faculty of Beast Husbandry

The Faculty of Beast Husbandry & Veterinary Thaumaturgy objected, in its return of the 8th of April 1496, that a familiar is a beast, that severance is veterinary work, and that this manual therefore practises within BH's jurisdiction without a licence. The objection is set down in that Faculty's voice, and answered below.

> The familiar is a beast. Its binding is a condition of the beast, and its severance is the treatment of that condition. The treatment of the conditions of beasts belongs to this Faculty by the Concord of Nine Bells, clause 41, and has belonged to it since 1147. It is not an etiquette. It is not a courtesy. A cat bound for seven years and unbound in an afternoon by a laywoman is a cat in a condition, and the condition has a name in our registers, and the name is not *release*. We dispute the author's competence, her instruments, her follow-up, and her right to charge. Should she wish to practise the severance of beasts, this Faculty will examine her; the examination is four parts and the fee is 2 marks. [the return of the 8th of April 1496, filed with the DE Faculty and not printed here]

The reply is threefold, and the first part of it is a concession: the author holds no licence and has never claimed one. The second part concerns dignity. A familiar is not a beast in the sense of clause 41: clause 41 concerns the conditions of beasts, while the difficulty of a familiar is a standing, not a condition. A familiar stands in a bargain; a party to a bargain can be consulted; and a party who can be consulted is answered, not treated. Veterinary work is done to a patient; severance is done with a party, which is why the words of the ritual are addressed to the familiar.

The third part concerns consent. A party whose assent is conduct rather than signature cannot have that assent evidenced by a form. Consent is established by conduct, observed over not less than fourteen days and recorded in the practitioner's hand. This Faculty has never explained what it would examine in a cat.

## 11. A Severance Done Without the Notice

The failure is named because named failures are the only failures the Faculty records. In the autumn of 1491, one Hoskin Marrow severed a familiar, a ferret of five years, without notice of any kind, on the morning of the 2nd of October, the ferret being removed by a servant to the Tenterfields the same afternoon. The register entry was made on the 9th of October, dated the 2nd, and written after the severance and not before it.

The Faculty's return for 1491 finds that no notice was given; that a customary practice observed without exception for ninety-one years is a rule in fact if not in form; that the entry was made after the act, which is a justification, not a record; and that the practitioner had charged 4 bells to a third party, which was the offence that mattered.

The penalty was 3 marks 4 bells: 2 marks for the severance without notice, 8 bells for the entry made out of time, and 8 bells for the fee charged without a licence. The ferret was not recovered.

What the Faculty required afterwards is the part of the case that belongs in this manual. It required the practitioner to give the notice retroactively, by reading it aloud in the empty room on fourteen consecutive mornings; to pay for a witnessed re-reading of the whole register for 1491 before the Faculty's clerk; and to enter the words *notice given late* at the head of every page of his register for four years, the clerk checking four times at 1 bell a visit. In 1495 he left the Nine Cloisters, and his register was deposited with the Faculty.

## 12. Fines, Fees and the Faculty's Schedule

Fines for severance without notice run from 2 marks, as at §11, to 5 marks where the familiar is under two years and the severance is a weaning mis-described in the register. Fines for a void ritual under §5 stand at 1 mark 4 bells. Fines for an entry made after the act stand at 8 bells and double on the second such entry in the same register; the returns accordingly contain one man fined 8 bells and one fined 1 mark 16 bells in the same year, both of them Hoskin Marrow, and the Faculty records the second without comment.

Overdue fees at the Counter accrue at 2 bells 4 clipped bells weekly, and the author's own accession carries an overdue charge, which she has asked the Library to review twice, in 1498 and in 1501, and the Library has reviewed it twice.

## 13. The Register, and the Six Entries Every Practitioner Makes

The register is the whole of the practice. Six entries are made in every severance: the reason, before the notice; the notice, spoken and written in the same hour; the collar's option under §3; the day and hour of the passage, or the word *declined*; the closing, on the thirtieth day after; and the note of any return, entered as an observation and never as a decision.

The sixth entry is the one practitioners omit, on the ground that a return is a private matter. The author's own sixth entry, for the 7th of Fennuary 1493, is four words: *returned, scullery window, glad*. The register is the only record of the matter that will exist in forty years, and the author has kept hers.

## 14. Consent, and Parties Who Cannot File Forms

A familiar's consent to severance cannot be evidenced by a signature or a collar. A signature requires a hand, and the collar says only that a neck was measured. Consent is established by conduct: by the familiar's approach to the opening, by its eating or refusal at step two, by its remaining or its absence in the three minutes, and by nothing said at step four. Conduct is observed over not less than fourteen days and recorded in the practitioner's hand.

This is the point at which the manual is weakest, and the author records it as such. A record kept by one party about the consent of another is not evidence; it is a diary. The Faculty's forms are inadequate to the parties in question, and such parties cannot file, cannot appeal, and cannot be heard at Counter III, which requires a form. The remedy is not a form. It is duration: fourteen days, spoken aloud, twice read, with the practitioner's reason written down before the first word of it. A courtesy kept for ninety-one years is not consent either; it is the nearest thing to it that this Faculty has.

Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is required for submissions of this Faculty and is absent from this file, as it is absent from every file in this library. The author has not seen one and knows no one who has, and the Library's note at the forms counter is that the form is obtainable at a counter that does not exist.
