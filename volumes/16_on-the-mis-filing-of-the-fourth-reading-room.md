# On the Mis-Filing of the Fourth Reading Room
### A Thesis upon the Incident of the Sixth of March 1487, Its Discovery by the Return of Lamps, and the Question Whether Anything Was Done Wrong (Nothing Was)

## Abstract (as filed)

This thesis accounts for the disappearance of the fourth reading room of the Wrenfield Library on the
sixth of March 1487, when it was filed under R in the reordering of the Registry, with its counter, its
lamp, one completed Form 7B, and the only copy of *Form 7B for Dummies*. The candidate argues that the
filing was correct, that the room had awaited it since 1388, and that his grandfather performed it.
Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, was to have been filed with this thesis; the
candidate cannot obtain one, and the Registry has accepted the excuse in writing.

## 1. The Room Itself

The fourth reading room occupied the north range at the far end of the north corridor: forty-one feet
by nineteen, one door, one window, one counter. The window was the only river window in the building
to be set above a counter rather than above a desk, an arrangement the plan of 1223 does not show. The
counter was a hatch in the north wall, eleven inches deep, with a drawer beneath it that locked on the
room's side and could not be locked from the corridor.

It was the only room in which Form 7B was issued, and it was issued at the counter, by the Clerk of
that room, and to the applicant in person. The Library's only copy of the practitioner's manual was
shelved at the counter, because it is the kind of book consulted in the room, at the counter, in a
hurry. The room was booked by the hour, entered at the hour, and vacated at the hour; its schedule was
the most consulted document in the Registry after the Registry itself.

The room's own register mark was **R/4**. It had borne that mark since 1388, painted on the door in
white, on the inside, at the height of a man's eye. The door was removed on the sixth of March 1487,
with its mark, and the corridor is therefore unmarked and has been since.

a place at which an appointment may be made, being filed.

## 3. The Classification of 1388, and the Letter R

The Classification in use at Wrenfield was devised in 1388 by Mr Ephraim Sallow, Under-Librarian
1381–1391, who was unwell. It is preserved as **FN 6.1 / S25 1388**, and the Library's note to that
text establishes what this chapter now sets out clause by clause. The scheme assigns to each thing in
the Library's care a series letter, a shelf, and a position; and it assigns to the Registry the series
letter R, without comment, in a line of the fair copy that occupies nine words.

Four clauses of the Classification bear upon the present matter.

**Clause 1 (Scope).** Every item of the Library's care shall bear a series letter, and shall stand in
the sequence of that letter. An item not bearing a letter is not in the classification, and what is not
in the classification is not in the Library.

**Clause 4 (Rooms).** A room of the Library is an item of the series of the office that keeps it.

**Clause 7 (Marks).** A mark once assigned is not reassigned. The mark is the item's name in the
sequence, and an item renamed is an item lost.

**Clause 12 (Registry).** The Registry series is R. Its items comprise the registers, the schedules,
the files, the marks, and such rooms as the Registry keeps.

Clause 12 is the whole of the case. The fourth reading room was kept by the Registry, was entered in
the Registry's schedule of rooms, was booked through the Registry's counter, and was painted with an R
mark of its own. In the Classification's terms it was an item of the Registry, bearing an R mark, and
in 1487 its file came up in sequence with every other R item. The room had been misfiled by its own
designation for ninety-nine years. The clerk of 1487 did not misfile it. He completed a filing that had
been waiting since before he was born. The candidate is aware that the Classification's final note is
three words long, and that the words are not *this will hold* (**FN 6.1**, Sallow's final note).

## 4. The Registry's Practice in 1487

It is necessary to state the Registry's practice as it stood in 1487, because the present generation of
officers has formed its habits under a reordering that never closed. The practice is set out in the
Registry's own book of procedure, which the Registry will not produce and whose contents three retired
clerks have separately described, in terms that agree. It is short: eleven rules. Rule 6 is the rule that
failed at the sixth of March, and it failed in the Registry's favour, which the Registry has never
conceded.

Two of the eleven rules matter here. Rule 3 required an officer to read an item's mark from the item
itself and, where the item was a room, from the door's own face at the height of the eye, in the
presence of a witness. Rule 5 required the officer to verify a counter's contents against the counter's
schedule, item by item, and to enter each on the file in the schedule's own order. Neither rule admits
of exception, and neither is discretionary: the book of procedure gives the officer no power to vary
either, and the book of forms bound with it contains no form by which a variation might be requested.

The practice rests on older ground. The Capitular Statutes of 1274 (**FN 1.1 / — 1274**) fix the status
of a room in two clauses: a room named in the schedule is kept by the office that keeps its schedule,
and an unclaimed deposit lapses to the finder in the eleventh year. The first is why the room stood in
the R series at all. The second is why the Library's only completed Form 7B, deposited in 1483 and
transferred to the counter in 1486, was still at the counter and had not lapsed to anyone; and it is
also the clause under which the crate accessioned in 1494 is now in its eleventh year, a matter for
other volumes. The candidate mentions it because the Registry has twice sought to move the crate out of
the contingency on the ground of lapse, the eleventh year falling in 1505.

The Registry's practice is unremarkable and is offered here only because the reader must understand
that the officer who goes to the shelves with a file is not a person making a decision. He is a person
carrying out steps that were decided when the file was raised.

## 5. The Registry's Reordering Procedure of 1487

The procedure below is the Registry's own, as described by the three retired clerks and as confirmed
in part by the day-book. It is numbered here so that the reader may attempt it, and should not.

1. Take up the file. Note on the file's face the date of taking up, and initial it.
2. Read the item's mark from the item, not from the file. Where the two differ, the item governs.
3. Where the item is a room, read the mark from the door's own face, at the height of the eye, and
   require the porter to witness the reading by counter-signature.
4. Carry the file, and the item's contents, to the item itself. Verification is made at the item, never
   at the desk; a mark verified at the desk is not verified.
5. Where the item is a room with a counter, verify the counter's contents against the counter's
   schedule, item by item, and enter each on the file in the order in which the schedule lists them.
6. Enter the sequence letter. Where the item bears that letter itself, the entry is made without
   exception, without reference, and without a separate minute.
7. Close the file. The interval between the entry at step 6 and the closing of the file is not
   recorded, and is not to be recorded. This is rule 6 of the book of practice.
8. Return the file to the sequence. The reordering proceeds, and the officer takes up the next file.

The officer who follows the procedure cannot reach a different entry at step 6. The item bears its own R
mark, and the mark is not reassignable under clause 7 of the Classification. There is no step at which
the item might be set aside, because the procedure provides no such step, and the book of forms provides
no form on which such a step might be requested.

## 6. The Sixth of March 1487

Mr Anselm Pike, Junior Clerk (Third Class, Unconfirmed), aged twenty-three, was engaged upon the
reordering of the Registry. He had been in the post nine months and eleven days. He was unconfirmed,
and the significance of that fact is set out at §14.

**Ten o'clock.** Pike took up the R files from the press in the Registry's outer room and laid them in
the sequence of the schedule. He initialled each file's face, twenty-nine files in all, and entered the
day's work in the day-book in the words *R — taken up, 29*.

**Eleven o'clock.** He reached the entry for the fourth reading room. The file was thin: the room's
schedule card, the 1486 valuation, and a receipt of 1486 for one completed Form 7B transferred to the
room's counter for verification and not since collected. Pike read the item's mark from the item — that
is, he walked the length of the north corridor and read R/4 on the inside of the door at the height of
his eye — and required the porter to witness the reading. The porter witnessed it and wrote his name,
which was Hollis, and the hour, which was eleven.

**Noon.** The Senior Clerk was called away. The record does not say by whom; the Senior Clerk, asked in
1489, said that he was called away, and that he had told Pike to carry on, and that he had told him so
twice. He was not asked what he had told him to carry on with. Before leaving he gave Pike the one
instruction that Pike repeated to the end of his life: *read the marks off the things, not off the
paper.* Pike was told nothing about the room. He was told nothing about the counter. He was not told
that the counter's contents were to be verified at all, and he verified them because step 5 of the
procedure required it and because he had read the book.

**One o'clock.** Pike carried the file to the room, and the contents of the counter with it. The
contents were those the schedule listed against the counter: one completed Form 7B, in its wrapper,
deposited with the Library in 1483 and transferred in 1486 for verification; one copy of *Form 7B for
Dummies*, octavo, boards rubbed, purchased 1483 for 4 bells; and the register stand. The counter's own
schedule card, which is not to be removed from the drawer, was not removed. He set the file on the oak
table, laid the wrapper beside it, and worked through the counter's contents item by item, entering each
on the file in the order in which the schedule listed them. He took forty minutes over the entry, which
was slow, and it is the only entry of the day on which the Registry has never been able to find fault.

**Two o'clock.** Pike reached step 6. The item bore the sequence letter itself. He entered the letter.
He recorded the entry in the day-book in three words, and closed the file, and the interval between the
entry and the closing of the file is not recorded and is not to be recorded, rule 6 of the book of
practice forbidding it.

**Forty-one minutes past two.** The Registry's clock stopped. It has not been wound since. The
witnesses to the hour are the clock and the day-book, and the clock has not been wrong before or since,
having not run.

**Three o'clock.** Pike returned the file to the sequence and took up the next, R/5, the register of
the loan of lamps, and worked on it until six. He did not file the room. That is to say: he did not
perform any act upon the room other than the entry at step 6. The room was not moved, emptied,
dismantled, or sealed. It was entered, and upon the entry it was no longer a place a person might be,
and the space at the end of the north corridor was a corridor of the same dimensions, and the draught in
it was already there. Pike noticed nothing. He went home at six by the bell.

## 7. The Door, Filed Separately Under D

The door had been removed from its hinges on the morning of the sixth of March by the porter Hollis,
under a work order of the fifth of March requiring the plan chest to be carried out of the room, which
could not be done with the door hung and the chest full. The work order bears the Under-Librarian's
counter-signature. The door stood in the north corridor, leaning against the wall, from eleven in the
morning until the eighth of March, when Pike, working through the D series, found it.

The door was filed under D on the eighth of March, and filed correctly: D is the series of doors,
thresholds and removable members under the Classification, and the door was a removable member, and it
bore no R mark. The candidate has been asked whether the door should have been returned to the room
before the room was entered, and answers that the room was entered at one o'clock and the door had been
off its hinges since eleven, and that no procedure of the Registry directs an officer to hang a door
before filing a room, and no officer of the Registry hangs doors. The door is in the door store. It is
available for inspection on written application. The Under-Librarian has declined to permit the
inspection on the ground that the application would be made in the north corridor.

The Registry's entry for the eleventh day is the last entry on the page. The enquiry was opened on the
twelfth day and has not been closed. The candidate notes that on the fourth day the Registry sought a
schedule card from a counter at which no officer was posted, and that on the sixth day a porter closed
a drawer through a hatch in a wall, and that on the ninth day a lamplighter trimmed a lamp that the
schedule placed in a room, and that none of the four officers is recorded as having thought anything
amiss. The Registry's own view is that the eleven days were ordinary. On the record, they were.

## 9. The Return of Lamps

The annual return of lamps is the oldest routine in the Library. Its form is a single sheet, ruled in
four columns: the place of the lamp, the officer of the lamp, the trimming, and the finding. The return
of 1487 is the return that found the room, because the return is made by place and the room is a place,
and a place that cannot be walked to is a place whose lamp is not found.

The Registry has since 1487 made the return twice yearly, in March and in September, which is the only
procedural change the incident has produced. The September return has four times returned the lamp as
*with the room*, which is accurate and is not a finding. The candidate has inspected the September
returns for 1487 to 1503: they are uniform, the officer who signs them changes in 1494, and the hand
that writes *with the room* does not change at all. The return, unlike the room, always comes back
(**LT 3.7 / F47 1498**, §IX, on coming back as a separate discipline).

## 10. The Surveyors, and the North Wall

The north range has not been surveyed since 1484. The reason is not the wall. The reason is that a
survey requires the surveyor to stand in the range, and the range contains a corridor of the same
dimensions as the fourth reading room, and the officers of the Registry will not enter the corridor,
and the surveyors of the University will not enter it without an escort of the Registry, and the
Registry's escort cannot be provided because the escort's schedule places the escort in the room.

The last measurement, of 1484, found the north wall two feet four inches thick, of which two feet four
inches is wall. The surveyor of 1484 was Mr Giles Amberley, who measured the wall in eleven places and
entered the same figure eleven times, and who added in the margin that the wall was *thicker at the
hatch, the hatch being a hole*. The plans approved in 1223 (**FN 2.1**) were not amended after 1487 and
show the room plainly, with four desks and a river window. The marginal notes on the plan sheets run
from 1401 to 1494; the last of them, in the Under-Librarian's hand, reads *chest here, and here it
stays*. The plan chest is in the room, where the plan says it is, and the plans are in the chest, and
the chest cannot be reached, and the plans of the north range are consequently in the north range, which
the candidate offers as the only complete statement in this thesis.

## 11. The Three Consequences

Three consequences follow, and the candidate sets them out in the order in which the Library has
suffered them.

**The form.** Form 7B is issued only at the counter of the fourth reading room, a point established in
**EA 1.1 / G74 1493**, §6, which this thesis may be said to annotate. Application for the form cannot
be made without the form. The Committee on Standing Matters has considered the matter four times; each
consideration required the form. The four refusals are reproduced at Appendix C, and the fourth adds a
fifth ground, which is that the question has already been considered four times. The one completed Form
7B in the University's history is not in the Registry's hands, having been entered at step 6.

**The manual.** *Form 7B for Dummies* was an Aldermarket piracy, octavo, in boards, purchased by the
Library in 1483 for 4 bells — the only copy held, and the only copy known. It was shelved at the counter
and it was filed with the room. The Library has not replaced it: a replacement cannot be accessioned
without the counter's mark, and there is no counter. The candidate is informed that the Aldermarket
series to which it belonged also included *Pollen for Dummies* (not held), *Jinxes for Dummies*
(withdrawn), and *Draughts for Dummies* (held, and missing). Readers continue to ask for the manual.
They are referred to the room, which is filed, and then to this volume, which is not.

**The smell.** Since March 1487 the smell of a room that nobody has been in has been reported
throughout Alderwick. The Faculty of Chronology attributes it to the interval (**CR 1.1 / — 1483**, §8)
and has published on it. This Faculty makes no finding. It notes only that the reports began in the week
of the incident, that they have never once been of use, and that the smell is strongest in the Tenterfields
and in the Weeping Compass, which are both downwind, and that the draught is not to be stopped.

## 12. The Loop, and Why It Is Not a Defect

The Registry has one further objection, and it is the only one put to the candidate in writing. It is
that the affair is a loop: the form is issued in the room; the form is required to enter the room; the
room has been filed; and the filing cannot be undone for want of a form that cannot be obtained but in
the room. The Registry calls this a defect in the procedure of 1487, and proposes that the procedure be
amended so that a room may be unfiled upon the application of the Registry, by minute, without a form.

The candidate replies that the loop is not a defect in the procedure. It is the procedure, considered
whole: a sequence of steps each of which is correct. A defect is a step that fails. No step failed. What
the Registry calls a loop is the shape a correct procedure takes when it is followed to the end; and the
Registry mistakes the shape for the fault.

The Registry's proposed amendment is the real danger, and the candidate opposes it. An unfiling by
minute would be a step not in the procedure, taken by officers who cannot enter the corridor in which
the record would have to be read. Under clause 7 of the Classification a mark once assigned is not
reassigned; the amendment would reassign the room's mark by dissolving it; and the room would then be
an item not bearing a letter, and what is not in the classification is not in the Library. The
amendment would remove the room. It has been proposed three times, in 1491, in 1497, and in 1503, and
has failed three times, and the candidate records his gratitude to the Committee, which has not reached
the motion, and his fear of the day it does.

## 13. The Exoneration of Mr Anselm Pike

The candidate's grandfather was unconfirmed in his post, so that no charge could be laid against him
under the statute; the Registry's remedy, where no charge lies, is promotion; he was promoted in 1491,
confirmed in 1494, and retired in 1500 on a pension of 3 marks, paid from the Registry's contingency.
The candidate's case is that the pension is the Registry's finding. The Registry had no charge and had a
remedy, and it applied the remedy, and it has paid the marks for four years. The pension has never been
removed from the contingency; the Registry, which strikes every other entry, has left this one standing,
which is as near as the Registry has come to a finding in the matter.

The Registry's own case, in the Registry's voice, is set out at §12 of the Registry's memorandum of
1493, which the candidate has been permitted to read but not to copy, and which he therefore sets out
from memory, and fairly:

> The Registry's position is that the officer was negligent, and the Registry does not withdraw the
> word. An officer of nine months' standing was left alone with the R series by a Senior Clerk who
> ought not to have been called away and was. He read the marks off the things, which is what he was
> told; but he did not ask himself what the things were, which is what he was for. His betters had
> asked themselves, and had left the room alone for ninety-nine years, and had left it alone for a
> reason, namely that a room is not a file. That the Classification calls a room an item, and gives
> the Registry an R, is a misfortune of the scheme of 1388 and not an instruction to the Registry. A
> clerk of sense would have stopped at step 5 and sent for the Senior Clerk; a clerk of sense did stop,
> twice, in 1401 and in 1444, and the Registry notes with regret that both officers were confirmed in
> their posts and that neither was promoted, the Registry's remedy not being available where a charge
> lies. The Registry does not say that the officer acted unlawfully. No charge lies. The Registry says
> that he acted negligently, and that it has paid for it, and that it is paying still.

The candidate replies as follows. The room bore the Registry's own mark, painted by the Registry's own
officers and entered in the Registry's own schedule of rooms, and it had borne it since 1388. The
officer did not choose the mark; he read it, in the presence of a witness, at the height of the eye, as
step 3 of the procedure directed. He then had the two courses, and no third: to file it, or to leave
it out. The Registry says a clerk of sense would have sent for the Senior Clerk, and the candidate accepts that a clerk of
sense would have sent for him, and observes that the Senior Clerk had said carry on, twice, before he
went. But sending for the Senior Clerk is not one of the two courses. It is a delay, and at the end of
the delay there are still the two courses, and the Registry has never named a third. It has instead
named two officers, of 1401 and of 1444, who each stopped; the record does not show that either stopped
because of the room, and the R series was reordered twice between 1388 and 1487 without the room being
reached, which is not the same as its being set aside.

No procedure permitted leaving it out. That is the answer to the whole of the Registry's case, and it
is an answer the Registry cannot make, because it wrote the procedure.

The Committee's standing motion to discuss the incident has been item 4 on every agenda since 1489 and
has never been reached, which the candidate submits is an admission. The candidate does not ask the
Committee to find that his grandfather was right. He asks the Committee to reach item 4. The crate
accessioned in 1494 is not discussed in this thesis. Its manifest has been described to the candidate
as one volume, octavo, self-help. The draught is not to be stopped.

## 14. The Missing Form

Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is required for every thesis. The candidate's is
required, and is not filed, and the Registry has accepted the excuse in writing. The candidate states
the ground plainly: the form is obtainable only at the counter of the fourth reading room, and the
counter was filed with the room on the sixth of March 1487 at forty-one minutes past two, and the
Registry's own reordering procedure of 1487, followed correctly, produced the filing. The candidate is
therefore unable to annex to this thesis the very declaration whose absence the thesis explains. He is
told that this is the joke, and he does not find it funny, and he has set out the procedure at §5 so
that the reader may see that there is no step at which it might have been done otherwise.

The candidate requests that this chapter be read as the form. It is longer than the form, which has
four clauses, and it is drawn from the same materials, and it makes the same declaration, which is that
the candidate has not interfered with time. He has not. He has read a day-book.

## Appendix C: The Four Refusals of the Committee on Standing Matters

Taken from the Committee's minute book, items 4 of 1489, 1493, 1497 and 1503, each reached no further
than the reading of the item.

**1493.** The Committee has considered the Registry's memorandum. The Registry's remedy has been
applied and the officer's pension is in payment. The item is held over.

Committee declines to make a finding in another Faculty's matter. The item is held over.
