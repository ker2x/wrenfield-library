# The Chronicle of the Nine Bells

### Annals of the founding of the University of Alderwick in the Year of the Weeping Compass, with the bequest of Margrave Wrenfield, and the first hundred years of the Library

**Call number:** FN 5.1 / — 1147
**Class:** FN — The Foundation Collection
**Text:** English, annals hand; copied 1211 by a scribe signing *B. of the Compass*, from a roll said to be the founding roll
**Date:** The events of 1147 and following; the copy of 1211; the Library doubts four entries of 1163, which are in a later ink and too well informed
**Custody:** Upper Crypt, first press, in the chest of the Concord; not to be produced on a bell-day, nor to any person who has rung the fourth bell out of order
**Collation:** 22 leaves, one gathering of eight in a different hand, 2 folding leaves at the end in the hand of 1211
**Condition:** The first leaf is a replacement of 1211 and whiter than the rest; the bells are named in red, and the red is faded in the order of the ring, so that the ninth bell is the faintest

## The Library's Note

This is the chronicle of the University of Alderwick, kept as annals by years from the founding in the Year of the Weeping Compass to the making of the present copy in 1211, and it does one thing: it fixes the order of events upon which the Concord, the motto, the Library and the bequest have ever since been argued. It is not a history and not a register. It records no dispute without recording the year of the dispute, and it settles nothing; where the Concord could not decide, the chronicle says so and passes on. The copy of 1211 is the only copy, and the Library has never seen the roll from which it was taken, and does not expect to, the roll having been carried in procession until 1371 and not since. It can verify the copyist and the ink; it cannot verify the four entries of 1163, and declines to investigate, the parties to that matter being the bells, and the bells not having been asked. The enlarged reissue is set from the same leaves; nothing has been added to the annals, and the marginal objection of the Chapter (1302) is printed here for the first time. The shelf-mate entries below were added by the Library in later hands. They are not the chronicler's citations, and the chronicler is not answerable for them.

## The Annals, by Years

**1147.** In the Year of the Weeping Compass the masters of the Nine Cloisters came together in the house at the sign of the compass, which stands in Bell Court and is now a public house, and there made the Concord of Nine Bells, one bell for each cloister; and it was agreed that the bells should be rung in the order of the ring and that the ringing should be the whole of the covenant between the cloisters. Those who swore it were Master Aldwyn of the First Cloister, who kept the roll; Master Berewald of the Second; Master Cuillin of the Third; Master Dunstan of the Fourth, who was called the Slow; Master Egbert of the Fifth; Master Fulk of the Sixth; Master Guthlac of the Seventh; Master Hereric of the Eighth; and Master Ingulf of the Ninth, who rang first and signed last. Their eight successors in each cloister are not named, the chronicle having been begun by Aldwyn and continued by no one.

**1148.** The Concord took the motto *Festina Lente, Erra Celeriter*, rendered in the schools as *make haste slowly; err quickly*, and by the Chancellor's clerk as *be slow, and be wrong at once*, for which he was fined a bell.

**1150.** The first buildings: the hall of the Nine Cloisters was begun and the cloister roofs were carried on the old walls; the school had no roof of its own for the whole of that year, and the first lecture was read in the yard, and the rain was entered as an authority.

**1151.** The first Masters: the Concord admitted three, of whom one taught grammar, one the reckoning of days, and one the properties of herbs, and it was provided that no Master should be admitted who had not been examined by the master of another cloister, and that the examiner should be paid two bells, and should be named in the roll.

**1151.** The first quarrel: whether the fourth bell should be rung for the dead of the town, who are not of the University, and cannot hear it, and do not know it is rung. The Chancellor said it should be rung for the living, who hear it. The sub-Chancellor said a bell rung for those who cannot hear is a bell that does nothing. The question was put to the Nine, and the Nine would not decide it, and it stood.

**1152.** The courses were set at three, and the year of study at seven, and a scholar was required to keep his own window open, and to answer for the lantern, and to pay the bell-ringer at Candlemas.

**1156.** The library of the cloisters was found short of four hundred books, and the entry is the first appearance of the number.

**1159.** The first plague: the schools were shut from the feast of St Mark to Michaelmas, and the Yarrow was not to be drawn upon above the ford. Of the nine Masters, three died; the Fourth Cloister came through whole, and says so.

**1160.** The second plague, in a wet autumn. The Low Town buried its dead in the Tenterfields, and the Concord allowed the fourth bell to be rung for them without deciding the quarrel of 1151, and entered the allowance as a matter of weather.

**1161.** Margrave Wrenfield died at his house at the Tenterfields, in the odour of a settled opinion, and by his will left the Concord **four hundred books and one unresolved argument**, the books to be chained, the argument kept. He named the argument in the will, and the chronicle names it here: *whether a thing done that harms no one is a harm.*

**1162.** *The bequest received.* The books were brought from the Tenterfields in nine carts on the eleventh of March, and were chained in the long press above the hall in the order in which they came out of the carts, which is not an order; and the argument was received and shelved with them, and is the reason the Library is a library.

**1163.** *[later ink]* The argument was moved; read; answered; and left where it was.

**1174.** The first reordering of the presses, in which the books were found to be more than four hundred by eleven, and the eleven were chained also, and no one asked the eleventh question.

**1189.** The copying of the great roll was begun, and it is entered that the scribes were lodged at the University's charge, and that the charge was two bells the week, and that the fourth scribe left at Candlemas without returning his pen.

**1202.** The heirs of the Margrave asked the Concord for the argument back, offering to pay the value of the four hundred books, the books having been chained and the argument not having been read. The Concord answered that the books were the library and the argument was the burden, and that a bequest of both could not be returned by halves; and it entered the answer and the offer, and no more.

**1211.** This copy made, the roll being worn where the Argument is named, and worn by reading. The copyist adds that he copied the roll as it stood, including the entry of 1163, which he did not understand, and that he has left the leaf for it.

## The Condition of the Bequest, as it stands in the will of 1161

Copied from the roll of the Tenterfields, which the Concord had of the heirs in 1162, and entered here that the terms be read with the gift and not after it:

> I, Roger, Margrave Wrenfield, being of sound mind and of a settled opinion, give to the Concord of the Nine Bells of Alderwick four hundred books, to be chained and kept, and one argument, to be kept unresolved. The argument I name: whether a thing done that harms no one is a harm. And I lay this condition upon the gift and upon the keepers of it: that the argument be not decided, nor closed, nor put by, nor exchanged for another, nor answered in a sermon; and that it be read, and read again, and left standing; and I charge the books with it, so that a reader who takes down the one takes up the other. And if the Concord shall decide it, the books pass to the Chapter, and the Chapter shall keep them as I have said, and the argument shall stand as it stood.

**The Chapter's objection, entered in the margin of the fifth leaf in 1302, in the hand of the Chapter clerk:** The will forbids deciding and commands reading, and no man can read four hundred books and one argument without forming an opinion of it, and an opinion held is the argument answered. The condition is therefore either impossible or idle, and the Concord has kept it by doing neither. — **The Concord's reply, entered beneath it in the same year:** The condition forbids a *decision*, which is a thing done by a body and entered in a roll. It does not forbid a reader to lean. The Chapter's reading is the leaning of one clerk; the Concord has decided nothing, and points to the roll, in which nothing is decided.

## The Procedure of the Ring, and of the Reading

1. The bell-ringer is appointed at Michaelmas, is paid three bells for the year, and finds his own rope.
2. He rings the nine in the order of the ring, and the fourth bell only at the hour set, and never between the seventh and the eighth.
3. One who rings out of order is fined one bell, and pays it to the ringer, and not to the Concord, the ringer's time being the thing lost.
4. One who rings out of order twice pays nothing for the second, the second being the offence, and is entered in the roll of faults.
5. The books are chained in the order of the carts of 1162 and are not to be re-chained to another order, the order being evidence.
6. A reader who takes down a book takes up the argument, and signs for the book, and signs again when the argument is returned with it.
7. The chronicle is read aloud at Michaelmas in the hall, and any entry disputed is disputed then, and not later.

## Named Failures and Their Penalties

**The clerk of the Chancellor, 1148.** For rendering the motto as *be slow, and be wrong at once* in the presence of the Nine, fined one bell, and the bell was hung in the Fourth Cloister with his name upon it, and it is rung there still.

**Master Dunstan of the Fourth, called the Slow, 1151.** For ringing the fourth bell for a townsman dead of the plague without a decision of the Nine, fined three bells and required to attend the funeral of the next townsman at his own charge. He attended, and entered in the cloister book that the bell had been right and the fine just, and both entries stand.

**The fourth scribe, 1189.** For leaving the great roll at Candlemas, and for taking his pen, discommoned from the University for one year, and the pen charged to the Concord at four bells and never recovered.

**The eleventh book, 1174.** For being one of eleven chained above the four hundred without a donor's name, and for having no entry in the register of 1162: no penalty could be found for a book, and the Concord ordered that it be chained and the matter entered, and the matter is entered.

## Apparatus

*Under-Librarian's stamp, in violet:* The first press is opened on Tuesdays, ten to eleven, and on no bell-day.

*Marginalia, in pencil, hand unidentified, against 1161:* The one condition of the gift is that the argument stand unresolved. A library that settled it would be in breach. Every Chancellor since has known this, and that is why the question is open, and open by design.

*Annotation, in a later hand, at the foot of the leaf of 1147, dated 1504:* Read the nine names again in the order in which the Concord swore them, and read the bequest's condition with them. The first swore the roll, the ninth swore last and rang first, and Master Ingulf of the Ninth was Margrave Wrenfield's uncle by his mother. The gift of 1161 was therefore not a gift to a school that happened to want books. It was a condition laid upon the family's own foundation, and the argument was placed in the chest of the Concord by a man who had chosen the chest, and who wrote *nor exchanged for another* knowing that the Nine had already failed to decide a smaller question in 1151. Sallow, who saw this leaf for one afternoon in 1391 and was not well, wrote three words about the ring order and left them where he found them.

*Shelf note, in the Under-Librarian's hand:* Not to be shelved with the histories. It is the foundation; they are about the foundation; they disagree with it, and the disagreement is to be seen from a distance.

*Conservator's line:* First leaf a replacement, to be left so; the red is faded in the ring-order; the gathering of eight is foxed and its sewing is later — do not resew.
