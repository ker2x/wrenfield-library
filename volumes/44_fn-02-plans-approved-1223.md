# The Plans Approved 1223

### Nine plan sheets of the University's first building campaign, with the surveyors' marginal notes of 1401–1494 and the measurements taken at the north wall

- **Call number:** FN 2.1 / — 1223
- **Class:** FN — The Foundation Collection
- **Text:** Ink on nine sheets of prepared calf, in three draughtsmen's hands; dimensions in Latin, surveyors' notes in English from 1401
- **Date:** Approved by the Chapter in the summer of 1223; the Library doubts sheet 6, whose water does not run downhill
- **Custody:** Flat in the plan chest, Lower Gallery, shelf 2, in a solander case; not to be produced to readers drawn upon the sheets
- **Collation:** 9 plan sheets, 1 sheet of survey notes pasted to the case lid, 1 loose slip of measurements in the hand of W. Coote
- **Condition:** Sheet 4 is cockled and was pressed flat in 1490, which flattened also two notes; the lid paste fails at one corner, and the corner is the interesting one

## The Library's Note

The text is the University's approved building plans: nine sheets of 1223, drawn for the Chapter by three draughtsmen, showing the precinct wall, the Cloisters, the hall and brewhouse, the Library's first and second ranges, the fourth reading room, the stables, the conduit and the whole precinct in one view. It is not a survey; it is a design, and it is the design against which every later survey of the north range has been taken. What it does is fix the fourth reading room where the building does not have one. The Library has never established which sheets were approved at the Chapter of 1223 and which were drawn after the meeting to show what had been approved. It can verify the sheets and the survey notes; it cannot verify the approvals, and declines to investigate, the minute-book for that summer being a list of candle purchases, which is to say intact. The chest holding these sheets stands in the room they describe. No Form 7B is filed with the plans; the form governs theses.

## What each sheet shows

Sheet 1 lays the precinct wall in one straight run from the Yarrow gate to the Tenterfield gate, with a third gate, the Cowgate, drawn shut and so annotated by the draughtsman, who has written *claudatur* beneath it. The wall is drawn at one uniform thickness which no surveyor has ever been able to measure and no mason has ever been able to build.

Sheet 2 sets the Nine Cloisters about the well, and the well off its mark by a foot; the draughtsman has ruled the cloister arcade twice and left both rulings standing, which later hands have read as an argument between two masons.

Sheet 3 gives the hall, the kitchen and the brewhouse, and gives the brewhouse the larger floor, with a note in drawer B's hand that the brewing of the Chapter's ale required it. Sheet 4 is the Library's first range: three reading rooms on one floor, each with its presses, its desks, its window and its name written in the room, and the names are the names in use in 1504, which is either a great continuity or a great carelessness.

Sheet 5 draws the second range and its presses, lettered A to R by a hand later than the drawing, and the letter R is on the smallest press in the darkest corner.

Sheet 6 is the fourth reading room, entered from the north range: four desks set in a square, a catalogue press against the western wall, and a river window in the north wall, four feet one inch above the floor by the survey of 1401. The sheet is drawn with the window open, which no other sheet does. The water in the conduit on sheet 8 runs downhill to the kitchen; the water on sheet 6 runs uphill from the river to the window.

Sheet 7 shows the stables and the yard before them, and the yard has been annotated as flooding in 1401, 1433 and 1461, in three hands. Sheet 8 draws the conduit from the Yarrow to the kitchen, with its four inspection chambers, and a surveyor's line of 1477 finds it dry. Sheet 9 shows the whole precinct in one view from the south-west, and does not show sheet 6, or the room on it.

Beneath the table stands the note of 1494, in Coote's hand and entered after her last measurement: that the wall was sounded at the reveals with a mallet, from the third desk to the presses, and returned *as before*; and that no further measurement was taken, it being impossible to measure the thickness of a wall from one side of it when the room on the inner face is not the room in the plan. The mallet is entered in the Lower Gallery's account at one bell and six clipped bells, refaced.

## The plan of 1489

The plan of 1489 is not among these nine sheets. It is kept in a chest of its own in the Lower Gallery, which is the chest that the plan draws; the sheet shows the Lower Gallery chest standing inside the fourth reading room, under the river window, with the catalogue press moved a foot to the east to receive it. A surveyor of 1494 records that the chest is where the plan of 1489 has it, and that the room is not. The Library has kept the plan of 1489 with the chest since 1490, and the arrangement has the advantage that the plan is never far from the thing it is wrong about.

## The survey of 1462, and why it was not entered

The survey of 1462 is the only one that failed, and the Library has the surveyor's own account of the failure. R. Sallow, mason, was directed by the Chapter to measure the fourth reading room and to report its dimensions, its desks and its window, so that the presses of the second range might be rehung without damage to the room. On the 3rd of May he went to the north range and found no door. On the 4th he measured the window from the outside and found it giving upon the presses. On the 6th he measured the window from the inside and found it giving upon the outer air, which he entered as a contradiction. On the 9th he measured the north wall at the reveals, at the presses and at the third desk, and the three measurements differed, and he entered all three, and reported that the wall was thickening from west to east at about a quarter-inch the year. The Chapter declined to receive the survey, on the ground that a wall cannot thicken, and fined Sallow two bells for a false return. He paid the fine and appended a note: *I have measured it three times and it is thicker each time.* The survey stands in the roll with the note and the fine, and the Library has taken no wall measurement since 1484 without Coote.

## The surveyor objected to, and Coote's reply

In the margin of the 1462 return, in the hand of T. Beddoes, who had measured the wall in 1433:

> *The man is measuring his own error. A wall that grows a quarter-inch in the year has grown two inches since I had it, and in two hundred years it will have closed the range. The plain reading is that the range is settling and the wall is being patched from the inside, and a patched wall measures thick because the patch is wall.*

Coote's reply is entered under it, in a different ink, and undated:

> *Beddoes is right about the patching and wrong about the reading. The patch is wall; the wall is two feet four inches; and what the room is entered from is not the question, because no one has ever entered it. I measure what is there. What is there is two feet four inches of wall.*

## The procedure of a survey, as the Lower Gallery practises it

1. The Chapter directs the survey in writing, and names the wall, the room and the use to which the measurement is to be put.
2. The surveyor attends with the plan chest key and with no assistant.
3. The surveyor measures at the reveals, at the presses and at the third desk, and enters all three, whatever they agree.
4. The surveyor measures twice on different days, and where the measurements differ he enters both and does not average them.
5. The surveyor signs the return and the fine-slip, the fine-slip being required before the return is read.
6. The Chapter receives the return at the next sitting, and may decline it; a declined return stands in the roll with its fine.
7. Where a return is declined, the surveyor may append a note, and the note is entered with the return.
8. No surveyor is to survey a room he has not entered, and the rule has never been waived.

## Apparatus

*Under-Librarian's stamp, in violet:* Consulted in the Lower Gallery only. The Gallery window is to be shut before the sheets are unfolded. — F.O., twice.

*Conservator's line, 1502:* Calf cockled; the 1490 press-flattening cost us two notes at sheet 4; the case lid is not to be pasted down again.

*Marginalia, in pencil, hand unidentified, against sheet 6:* The river window is on the north range, and the river is south. A window is a thing you see through. What sheet 6 calls a window is therefore not on the outside of the building, and the fourth reading room — the room the plan of 1489 puts a chest in — is not on the outside of the building either.

*Annotation, in a later hand, at the foot of the lid-sheet, dated 1496:* The nine sheets are not nine plans but one campaign, and the fourth reading room on sheet 6 is not a room but the reservation of a room; when the second range was built the reservation was carried into the fabric, and the room that was reserved is now the space between the north wall and the presses, which is a corridor of the same dimensions, and the draught in it is not to be stopped.
