## Abstract (as filed)

This treatise states first what mirror walking is, and then the threshold gait, the three-quarter pivot, and the discipline of return,
together with a schedule of surfaces that may not lawfully or safely be walked. It prints the Register
of Lost Walkers in full, reports the Bell Court wrong-frame arrival of 1493, answers the Faculty of
Thaumaturgical Engineering, and states the loss of the fourth reading room, on which the licence of
1455 now depends. An annex records the incident of 1461. A Declaration of Non-Interference with Time
(Form 7B) was to have been filed with this submission; it is absent, and the omission is at least
philosophically consistent.

## I. What Mirror Walking Is

Mirror walking is the licensed passage of a person through a framed, silvered surface, and his standing, for a stated time, inside the room the glass composes. It is a craft of thresholds, taught in this University since the Guild's instrument of 1310 and licensed by this Faculty since 1455. It is the only discipline in the Faculty's remit in which the practitioner's body, and not merely his attention, crosses the boundary; everything else the Faculty teaches is done by a man standing still.

The whole of the craft rests on one distinction. A mirror composes an *arrangement* of the room before it: it reverses the room, sets it behind the glass, and holds it there so long as the glass is framed and unbroken. A walker who crosses does not travel to another country. He enters the arrangement — that is, he stands inside the composed room, at an arm's length from where he stood, seeing the same furniture from the other side of the frame. What he finds there is the room, composed; what he does not find there is anything the room does not hold.

Everything else in this treatise follows from that. Because the arrangement is composed and not true, the walker may reach for what the room does not contain and miss what it does; because it is held by the frame, he cannot leave by it twice; and because it is a room, he is in a place, with a floor, and may fall upon it.

What a crossing is like, in the walker's own words, is the subject on which the Faculty has published most and established least. The substance of the evidence is three things: nothing is loud; the light is the light of the room and comes from the wrong side; and for the first three or four steps the walker hears his own footfalls arrive slightly after he does.

**What it is not.** It is not divination: the arrangement shows the room as arranged, never as it was, and it says nothing whatever about the future — that is the business of the Faculty of Divination (**DP 44.9**). It is not conjuration: nothing is made, and the arrangement's contents are the room's contents, composed. It is not time travel, though a walker who returns by the wrong frame may arrive on the wrong day and at a frame standing elsewhere — another room, another building, four miles off in the Tenterfields (§IX) — which is a fault in the frame and not an itinerary: four miles is not another country, and a day is not another year, though the Faculty has charged for both. And it is not, whatever the first-year students believe, a way into a locked room: the locked door in the room is a locked door in the arrangement, and this Faculty has buried two practitioners who thought otherwise.

**What a walker may lawfully do inside** is set out in the syllabus at §XIII and in the rulings at §XV. The substance is four things — retrieve a named object, read a page, deliver a document, and inspect a threshold — and the prohibitions are three: nothing may be taken out, no person seen in the arrangement may be addressed, and nothing may be sat upon.

The craft turns on seven terms. Each is fixed below by what it means, and by the error it is most often taken for:

| term | what it means | the error it is taken for |
|---|---|---|
| the arrangement | the room as the glass composes it, reversed and held behind the frame | a copy, or a second room |
| the frame | the made edge that holds an arrangement in place | a doorway; a doorway is not a threshold |
| the crossing | the passage of the body through the frame by the threshold gait (§VI) | travel; nothing is traversed |
| the far side | the interior of the arrangement, at an arm's length from the near side | another country, or another year |
| the return | the separate discipline of leaving, treated at §IX | the crossing, performed backwards |
| transit | the condition of a walker who has crossed and has not returned | death; see the Register at §XI |
| the register | the Faculty's list of those in transit, kept since 1381 | a list of the dead |

A reader who has understood this chapter has understood the discipline, and will find the remainder of the treatise to be a set of rules for surviving it.

## II. The Room That Was Filed, and Why This Is the Second Edition

A crossing is made in a room, and the room must hold still while it is made. For thirty-two years this
Faculty taught the craft in the fourth reading room of this Library, at the far end of the north
corridor: forty-one feet by nineteen, one door, one window, one counter, the counter a hatch in the north
wall with a drawer beneath it that locked on the room's side and could not be locked from the corridor.
That drawer is why the room was ours. A walker must return to the frame as he left it, and others may
enter a room; the Faculty's rule was therefore that the glass stand in a room the walker could bar
himself, and the fourth reading room held the only such glass in the University. It was booked by the
hour, entered at the hour and vacated at the hour, and its hours were kept in a schedule more consulted
than any document in the Registry but the Registry's own.

On the sixth of March 1487 the Registry reordered its series and filed the room under R, with its
counter, its lamp, its register stand, its two rush-seated chairs, its window bar and ring, one
completed Form 7B, and this Library's only copy of *Form 7B for Dummies*. The door had been taken off
its hinges at eleven that morning, to carry out the plan chest, so it stood in the corridor and was
filed separately, three days later, under D, that being the series of removable members, and correctly,
the door bearing no mark on its corridor face. The space is a corridor of the same dimensions. The
draught in it is not to be stopped. The clock stopped at forty-one minutes past two and has not been
wound.

**On the form, and the Faculty's position.** Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is
required with every work in this Library, and this treatise has none. The Faculty has twice declined to
offer the absence as a defect, because it is not one: the form is issued at the counter of the fourth
reading room, by the Clerk of that room, to the applicant in person, and no applicant in the
University's history has completed one except the applicant whose completed form was transferred to that
counter in 1486 for verification and filed with the room the following March. The Faculty is required to
declare that its walkers do not interfere with time; the instrument of the declaration is kept in the
place the declaration is about; and the body that filed the place declines to search for it, on the
ground that the search may already have taken place. This Faculty will not be told that time has not
been interfered with by a Registry that will not say whether a room has been lost or merely moved. A
room that stood in the north range until 1487, that is in the plan of 1223, and that is not in the
building now, has crossed a threshold; this Faculty knows what it is to cross one, and will not certify
an event it cannot name. The one comfort is that the form, like the room, is filed.

Three dates are given for the demonstration of 1461, and the Faculty keeps all three. The walker's own
diary, this Library's **MS 1.1**, gives the sixth of February. The three statements taken on the seventh
and eighth of March give the sixth of March, and the Register, which is kept from the statements, prints
the sixth of March. The porter's second return, taken in 1470, gives the sixth of May, and the porter,
asked why, said that it was May. The Faculty has entered all three and chosen none, agreement not being a
property of witnesses; and it records without comment that the room went into the reordering on the
sixth of March 1487, which is one of the three days on which, by one of the three accounts, the walker
went. The Register prints its dates and does not choose.

## III. Preliminary: On Silvered Glass

Still water shows the truth. Silvered glass shows an arrangement. A walker cannot cross a truth, but
an arrangement is a room with a frame around it, and a frame is a threshold, and thresholds are the
Faculty's business.

Hence the first rule of the discipline: **never walk a surface that has not been framed.** A shop
window qualifies, provided the shop is shut. A polished floor does not qualify, and anyone who says
otherwise is selling something, or has never done it, or has done it and cannot describe it.

The palm test is set out in §V rather than in an appendix, because the appendix is where a Faculty
puts a thing it has decided not to defend. The distinction governing this work is that silvered glass
presents an *arrangement* — one room, composed by a maker — whereas water presents a *truth*, which
has no frame and no way back.

This Faculty numbers its plates by the order of printing and its surfaces by the order of licensing,
which are not the same order.

## IV. The History of the Discipline, and the Prohibition of Still Water

Mirror walking entered the University by the back door, as a maintenance practice of the glaziers of
Bell Court. The Glaziers' Guild of Alderwick laid silver on glass from 1310 and kept a warder at the
Yarrow ford, whose duty was to test new pieces at the water's edge, a true surface and a silvered one
being distinguishable only by a lantern at dusk.

The Faculty of Locomotion, Doors & Threshold Studies was founded in 1398, and acquired mirror walking
from the Guild by a deed of transfer witnessed in the vestry of the Nine Cloisters on 2 September of
that year. The deed's operative clause reads that the Guild "delivers up the practice, together with
its casualties, to the Faculty, that the same may be taught by rule and insured by premium."

Still water was prohibited in 1401, and the grounds were not magical. The Lake Question turned on the
fact that a still surface is never quite still: the Yarrow's millpond is drawn down twice daily, and
a walker who enters a moving surface arrives at a frame that is moving and therefore not there. Six
of the nine early casualties are attributed to the millpond. The prohibition was extended in 1455 to
all open water, including ice.

## V. The Palm Test, and Its Nine Failures

Lay the palm flat on the glass. Warm glass is occupied. An occupied glass is not necessarily hostile,
but the occupant has not been asked, and it is not the walker's place to overrule him. Cold glass is
glass.

The test fails, in the author's experience, about one time in nine, and he has been criticised for
saying so.

| mode | description | first recorded |
|---|---|---|
| occupied glass reading cold | occupant asleep, absent, or between occupancies | 1399 |
| cold glass reading warm | an arrangement made in a cold room by a cold maker | 1404 |
| the second reading | the glass answers differently at the second test | 1412 |
| the borrowed hand | the test performed by anyone other than the walker | 1419 |
| the gloved palm | four layers or more of wool, and one of leather | 1426 |
| the south aspect | glass that has held sun for three hours or longer | 1433 |
| the wet palm | rain, or licking the hand before testing | 1447 |

| the courteous glass | occupant steps aside, walker declines to notice | 1484 |

The ninth mode is not a failure of the test but of the tester, and is included because it has cost the
Faculty more in appeals than the other eight together.

## VI. The Threshold Gait, Taught as Instructions

The gait is six steps. A student who has learned the six steps has not thereby learned the gait, but
a student who skips one has certainly not learned it.

1. **The approach, at a quarter-angle.** Some twenty degrees off square. A squared approach commits
   the walker before he has finished deciding.
2. **The pause, at the frame.** One full breath. This is not ceremony. It is the last moment at
   which the walker may still be said to be choosing.
3. **The palm test.** Flat palm, bare, on the glass, for the length of that same breath. Do not
   repeat it: a second test is a second opinion, and this Faculty takes no second opinions at the
   frame.
4. **The entrance step.** Leading foot only, weight held back until the sole is lost. The sensation
   is described by the author's first instructor as *the floor declining to comment*.
5. **The crossing.** Look at the room. The first-year student's commonest failure is to look at his
   own face. The second commonest is to look at the face of the person he came to see: the same
   error with a better motive.
6. **The arrival: turn, do not step, out.** The pivot is taken on the ball of the rear foot,
   three-quarters of a turn, in the time it takes to close a door behind a guest. Practise it until
   the count is boring.

A walker who crosses without pivoting arrives facing the glass, from the inside. From that position
the way out is also the way in, and the walker will be found eventually, by someone looking for
something else entirely.

## VII. The Pivot, and the Cost of Losing It

The pivot is examined separately from the gait, the gait being taught by the Faculty and the pivot by
the Faculty's casualties. The count is: *ball, heel, hip, look*. Four beats, on the rear foot's ball,
the leading heel descending on the third. The look is on the fourth, at the room.

Four faults are examinable. The **early look** — the walker turns his head before his hip, arriving
facing the room with his feet still facing the glass. The **flat pivot** — the full turn taken on the
whole foot, slower by a beat and a half, and audible. The **double pivot** — the pivot taken twice, in
the belief that the first was insufficient, which returns the walker to the glass and is the
commonest cause of the wrong-frame arrival. The **pivot on the entering foot** — a fault of the
second-year student who has read ahead, and the only fault to have produced a fatality, that of
Aveline Sarr in 1471.

The fee for a fourth examination in the pivot is 4 bells; for a fifth, a licensed demonstrator's
demonstration at the demonstrator's own fee.

## IX. Return, and the Wrong-Frame Arrival

Return has its own faults, and they are not the faults of going, which is why the Faculty examines
them separately and fails students separately. The **wrong-frame arrival** is the commonest: the
walker steps back into a mirror the same size, the same age and in the same house, and not the one he
left by. The way out is usually between six and nine feet to the left of the way in, and *usually* is
not a plan.

The most instructive case in the Faculty's records is **Nell Ravenscroft**, second-year M.L.S.
candidate, of the parish of St. Cuthbert in the Tenterfields. On the eleventh of April 1493, at about
the tenth hour, she entered the second of the two mirrors hanging in the workshop of Aldous Peake,
glazier, in Bell Court — an unlicensed surface, the shop being shut and the licence lapsed at Lady
Day — and came out at once in the Tenterfields, at the dyeworks of the Widow Halloway: four miles
from Bell Court and one day wrong, the twelfth of April.

The mechanism was established by the Faculty's examiner, Dr. Ottoline Marchetti-Bell, and the error
was Peake's. The two mirrors were leaves of one commission of 1463, sold as a pair to the dyeworks in
1464, whence one leaf had returned to Peake in 1491 for re-silvering and had never been collected.
Ravenscroft entered the re-silvered leaf and returned through its original, the frames being
identical in dimension: the condition under which the wrong-frame arrival is not merely possible but
likely. Peake had hung them three feet apart and not thought to say so, not knowing that there was
anything to say.

The recovery took four days and is the origin of the procedure now taught. Ravenscroft was retrieved
on the twelfth by a party of two, the frame at the dyeworks being secured by draping and by the
posting of the porter, paid 6 clipped bells and a certificate. The frames were then brought into
agreement by turning the Bell Court leaf to the wall, at which the Tenterfields leaf ceased to open:
the Faculty's standard test of agreement, called matching (step 3 below). She was walked out through
the frame by which she had arrived on the fifteenth of April, having spent three nights in the
dyeworks' counting-house, where the Widow Halloway charged the Faculty 1 mark 4 bells.

She was fined on two counts. The first was the unlicensed surface: 1 mark, the standard penalty,
reduced to 12 bells on the ground that the licence had lapsed through the glazier and not through
her. The second was the wrong-frame arrival itself, a return fault and not an entry fault: 9 bells,
less 3 bells for the promptness of her identification of the frame when asked. A further 2 bells was
charged for a glass she broke while demonstrating to the porter that she could not simply walk home,
refunded in 1495, the porter having asked her to.

Dr. Marchetti-Bell's remark to her at the hearing is entered in the Faculty's minutes, and she has
since published a document correcting the minutes. As recorded, she said: *"You have committed the
only error in this discipline that the Faculty can forgive and cannot prevent, and you have committed
it in a shop I have been telling the Guild about since before you were born. You will pass. Sit
down."* The correction runs to nine pages and does not alter the sense.

## X. The Four Recovery Steps

These are to be attempted in order and not otherwise. Each step has a form; the forms are held at
the Faculty's counter, and are obtainable there, with the exception of Form 7B, which is not.

1. **Secure the frame of arrival.** Drape the glass. Post a witness, who must be paid and named. Do
   not permit the walker to return through the frame she arrived by until step 3 is complete, though
   she will ask, and will ask first.
2. **Establish the walker's identity on the three canonical facts.** Name, parish, and the name of
   the person met at the last Michaelmas. One who cannot give all three is not to be moved, because
   one who cannot give all three is not necessarily the walker.
3. **Match the frames.** The keeper of the frame of entry turns that glass to the wall. If the frame
   of arrival then ceases to open, the frames are matched. If it does not, the walker is not in the
   house she thinks she is in, and the Faculty is to be sent for.
4. **File the Wrong-Frame Return (Form 7C) within seven days**, with the fine, the name of the
   witness, the parish of arrival, and a certificate of discharge signed by the Faculty's examiner.
   The late fee is doubled: Ravenscroft filed on the nineteenth and paid 2 bells.

## XII. Annex: The Incident of 1461, with the Witnesses

In the spring of 1461 a visiting adept gave a demonstration of the three-quarter pivot in the
third-floor reading room of this library. An undergraduate of the second year, Percival Nott, aged
nineteen, was asked to hold the frame steady. He did not hold it steady. He entered.

He has not arrived. The Faculty lists him as **in transit**, a distinction the family, after some
years, agreed to accept. A chair stands at the frame and is kept dusted. The roll is read annually,
on the seventh of the month, in a room otherwise silent. His library card remains active, his fines
accrue, and the Under-Librarian waives them once a year, and has done so forty-three times.

The witness statements taken on the seventh and eighth of March 1461 are held at the Faculty's
counter as File LT/1461/3. They do not agree, and the Faculty has declined for thirty-seven years to
prefer one to another, agreement not being a property of witnesses.

> **Statement of Godwin Ash, visiting adept, taken the 7th of March 1461.** I had asked for a
> steady hand, and I had asked the small one at the second desk, and the librarian said no, the
> small one was a reader and not a servant. Then the boy at the third desk stood up of his own
> accord and put both hands on the frame, and I said, keep it square, and he said, I have it. I
> turned to the class. When I turned back the hands were on the frame still and the boy was not
> between them. I did not ask him, and it has been said that I did.
>
> **Statement of Prudence Ash, of the second desk, taken the 7th of March 1461.** The adept asked
> for a steady hand, twice, in a loud voice, and looked at Percival while he asked, and Percival
> said, shall I, and the adept said, hold it square, and Percival took the frame. He was pleased to
> be asked. He had been at the desk since the bell and had said nothing all morning. I saw him take
> the frame with both hands and I saw him lean, and I did not see him go, and I have not been able
> to say since when I stopped seeing him.
>
> **Statement of Osric Fell, porter of the Wrenfield Library, taken the 8th of March 1461.** I was
> at the door with the lamps. I heard no one ask the lad for anything. The adept was a shouter and I
> would have heard a asking. The lad had a hand on the frame when I came in and the other hand at
> his own coat, and he was looking at his own face in the glass, which he had been told not to do.
> He was not holding it for anybody. He was holding it to go.

The author, having read the three statements many times, records only that the second and the porter
agree that Nott put both hands to the frame and disagree about everything that made the hands worth
putting there. The annex is longer than the treatise; the examiner answers in the note below.

The family's letters number eleven, of which the second is the best-known, being the one that asks
the Faculty to stop reading the roll, and which the Faculty reads aloud every year at the point in
the roll where the family asks the Faculty to stop.

## XIV. Objection, on Behalf of the Faculty of Thaumaturgical Engineering, and Reply

*Entered at the Faculty's direction. The objection is printed as filed, and is not the author's.*

**The objection.** The Faculty of Thaumaturgical Engineering & Load-Bearing Runes has read this
treatise in proof and objects to its premises, its method, and its licence. A threshold is a
structural member. It carries load. A frame carries the wall above it and the glass within it, and
the moment the walker leans on that glass with intent the member is loaded in a manner it was never
cut for. The Faculty's table of load ratings in stone-weight, which this treatise does not cite,
governs every frame in this University, and a walker who passes an unrated frame is performing
unlicensed building work. The Faculty has twice asked the Registry to require a rating plate at every
licensed surface, and twice has been told that the matter is one of locomotion, which is not an
answer. Until the plates are fitted, the Faculty advises its own students to walk nothing.

**Reply.** The Faculty of Locomotion, Doors & Threshold Studies is grateful for the objection and
concedes its premise. A threshold is a structural member, and the Faculty has never said otherwise;
it says only that the member is loaded by the crossing and not by the frame, and the crossing is over
in less time than the member takes to notice. The Engineering table rates members for *standing*
load, and a walker is not a standing load, and the Engineering Faculty's own hall has a threshold of
Bramblegate oak which has carried four hundred students a day since 1398 and has not been rated. If
the plate is what is wanted, this Faculty will fit it and pay for it, provided the Engineering
Faculty supplies the rating in the same year as the request. Three requests for ratings, of 1462,
1479 and 1494, stand on the Registry's file unanswered. As to the assertion that no Engineering
student has been lost, the Faculty accepts it, and notes that the Engineering Faculty teaches no one
to cross anything: its casualty column is a column of zero because it is a column of nothing.

## XV. Appendix: Rulings of the Faculty, 1455 and 1477

**Ruling of the 4th of June 1455, on shop windows and ice.** It is resolved that a shop window is
licensed during such hours as the shop is shut; that the shopkeeper's licence shall run from Lady Day
for one year; that the fee shall be 3 bells; and that the shopkeeper shall be a witness to any
crossing made through his glass and shall be paid 1 bell for it. It is further resolved that all ice
is water with a schedule, and that no ice shall be walked.

**Ruling of the 19th of October 1477, on planes of many parts.** It is resolved that a surface
composed of many parts in one plane is many frames, and that a walker who enters such a plane is
entered by them, and that a burnished blade and a church window, whether leaded or plain, are each
such a plane, and are prohibited without exception, and that a dispensation may be sought from the
Faculty in writing and will not be granted. It is further resolved that the prohibition does not
extend to a hand-mirror, being one part, provided it exceeds eight inches and a witness attends, and
provided the fee of 4 bells is paid in advance; and that the two losses of 1496 and 1497, being both
hand-mirrors, are entered in the Register without comment and without prejudice to the ruling.
