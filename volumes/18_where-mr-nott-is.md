## Abstract (as filed)

This thesis argues that Mr. Percival Nott, who entered the third-floor mirror on 6 May 1461 and did not return, is not lost: he is in the fourth reading room. The evidence is at §2, the wrong-frame statistics at §3, the Register at §4, and the arrival of 1502 at §6. The retrieval procedure at §8 cannot be initiated, because Form 7B is issued at that room's counter, and the counter was filed with the room on 6 March 1487. Form 7B is missing, as from every thesis here.

## 1. The Question as It Is Usually Put

The question is put as *where is Mr. Nott*, by persons expecting an answer of the form *he is at such a place*. Since 1461 the Faculty has held this to be the wrong question. The right question is *under what classification does Mr. Nott fall*, answered since 1462: a class, a shelf, and a bookable hour. It is not a place.

## 2. Why He Is Not Lost

A person is lost when he is nowhere, and no one is looking in the right place. Mr. Nott is somewhere. The Faculty offers four findings.

1. The demonstration of 6 May 1461 was held on a stand bolted into the floor of the third-floor landing, against a silvered glass of the pattern of 1470. The frame of 1461 was warped, and had been trued twice. A warped frame does not answer; it corresponds. Mr. Ferrer's rule of 1498 binds this Faculty: the frame that answers is not always the frame that was addressed.
2. Every threshold measured twice here has measured differently the second time. The fourth reading room was measured in 1401 and in 1484, and is shorter by eleven feet. The surveyors of 1484 recorded the north wall as two feet four inches thick, of which two feet four inches is wall.
3. On the eleventh day after the filing of 6 March 1487, the annual return of lamps found five lamps unaccounted for, with their counter, in the wrong series. The Registry offers no explanation and the Faculty accepts none.
4. Mr. Nott's library card remains active. It was presented at the Reserve Collection desk on 14 September 1502, by a hand not his own, and returned. The day-book records the card and not the person, which is now its standing evidence.

A person entered on a roll is not lost. He is merely late.

The ninth entry proper and the thirteenth are one person, entered twice because he was recorded twice. The fifth is not the younger Halvard Kemp of *Load-Bearing Runes*: his father built an arch in 1397 that answered twice and has since declined to answer.

Two findings follow. Three entries are recoveries and three are non-persons, leaving seven walkers genuinely absent, and every one of the seven entered a surface that stands in the plans of 1223. Further, not one of the thirteen entered and left by the same surface. A person is lost only where there is no record; every name above has one, and the record is the room.

## 5. On "In Transit" as the Stronger Classification

The Faculty prefers "in transit" to "lost" for four reasons, and the Register supplies the last.

First, "lost" is a finding, therefore appealable, and an appeal requires Form 7B, which is missing, having been filed with the room on 6 March 1487. A finding that cannot be appealed is not a finding but a mistake.

Second, "in transit" has a known cause and terminus — the frame — while a lost person has no cause, and a classification without a cause cannot be insured. The Laboratory's indemnity against threshold loss, renewed annually since 1478 at eleven bells, covers persons in transit and excludes persons lost.

Third, the Library does not strike out an entry until it is returned, and has not struck out Mr. Nott, whose fines of 4 marks 6 bells 2 clipped bells are waived every year, forty-three times, by the same motion.

Fourth, seven of the thirteen came back. A class in which more than half the members have returned is a class not of the lost but of the delayed.

Against the Registry the Faculty is content: Mr. Nott is in a corridor of the same dimensions as a room, filed under R, and its draught is not to be stopped. A corridor of the same dimensions as a room is a room, and the Registry's rule is that a thing is where it is filed. The corridor is filed with the room, and is therefore the room.

## 6. The Arrival of 1502

The arrival of 1502 requires this reissue: it is one of the fifty-eight recorded wrongs, and the only one in which the walker was interviewed at length.

Mr. Alderic Thorne, licensed walker, of the Nine Cloisters, entered the glass at the west end of the north corridor on 14 September 1502 at the third bell of the afternoon, intending the Reading Room 2, west window. He emerged into the north corridor, and was found there at forty minutes past the fourth bell, with his hat in his hand. He had been absent forty-four days, which he did not believe. Asked on which surface he had entered, he answered, on the glass, and correctly. He was the only one of the pair to come out in the wrong frame.

The Faculty interviewed him for eleven hours over four days. The recorder was the Deputy Under-Librarian, and the Dean of Ethics, Appeals & Paperwork granted leave for the interview and not for the room. The deposition is a certified copy, entered here as it stands.

> **The deposition of Alderic Thorne, walker, taken 18, 19, 20 and 22 September 1502.**
>
> Being asked to describe the place, he said: it is a room, of the size of the second reading room and the shape of it. It has four desks, set two and two, with the windows at the left hand as you stand in the door, and boards under the desks for the feet. There is a catalogue press against the north wall, of four-and-twenty drawers, and the fourth drawer from the top on the left does not open, and he tried it twice because he is a careful man. There is a river window, one, not shuttered, and the river is the Yarrow, with a boat upon it that does not move, or moves, and he did not watch it long. There is a table by the window with a lamp on it, and the lamp was lit. There is a clock that does not go; it stands at one-and-forty minutes past two, and he looked at it more than once. There is a draught. He could not say from where, and he was not cold. He heard a bell at the hour and at the half-hour, and one at the quarter, and could not see the bell, and the sound came from the room and not from outside, and it was not the Bell Court bell, which he knows. There is no door in the west wall where he came in. He looked for it, and there is none, and there is a mark on the plaster where a door might have been and he did not like it.
>
> Being asked whom he saw, he said: there was a reader at the second desk with an octavo manual, the boards rubbed, and he sat with his back to the room, and he did not look up. He wrote in it. He heard the man turn two leaves. He did not see his face, and did not ask him for the hour. He was asked why, and he said that the man was reading, and that he himself is a walker and knows what it is to be read to, which the recorder has left in the copy.
>
> Being asked how he came out, he said that he did not come out; that the room's hour came round, and that he was then in the north corridor with his hat in his hand, and the draught behind him, and the lamp he had carried gone.

Mr. Thorne was returned to the corridor at the fourth bell of 22 September and charged the fee for arriving without a booking, 4 marks 6 bells, which stands unpaid. He has since declined two further interviews.

## 7. What the Faculty Made of the Deposition

The Faculty entered four findings on the deposition in the minutes of 30 September 1502.

First, the room is described. Four desks, one press, one window, one lamp, one clock, one bell, one draught, agreeing with the plans of 1223, with the survey of 1484, and with Miss Finch of 1471, who saw three desks and no reader. A description agreeing with a plan drawn two hundred and seventy-nine years before the witness was born is a measurement.

Second, the room is furnished. The press and the lamp are fittings, and fittings are moveable; the inventory of 1487 lists, among the fittings filed with the room, one counter, one lamp, one inkstand, one stool and a bell-pull. The lamp is the Library's lamp no. 4, found in the wrong series and unaccounted for still.

Third, the room is occupied. Mr. Thorne saw one person, at the second desk, reading an octavo with rubbed boards, who did not look up. The Library's only octavo with rubbed boards in that class is the Aldermarket piracy of *Form 7B for Dummies*, purchased in 1483 for four bells, catalogued and not held, and filed with the room. A person reading it in the room is a person in the room.

Fourth, the clock. Mr. Thorne looked twice at a clock standing at forty-one minutes past two, at which the Registry's clock stopped on 6 March 1487 and has not been wound since. The two clocks, or the one clock twice, are the Faculty's best evidence that the room and the Registry are one threshold at different stations; and the reader who will not look up may be a clerk, and the clerk may be reading. The second reader, on this chapter, wrote: "Broadly."

## 8. A Proposed Retrieval Procedure

It cannot be initiated, and the Faculty states it at length in order that the point at which it stops shall be beyond argument.

1. The applicant attends the room's counter and requests Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, which is issued there and nowhere else, and which is the only form under which a walker may be removed from a threshold without the modification of an outcome.
2. The applicant completes the form in duplicate before the room's clock and files one copy with the room, the second being, in the Registry's phrase, a comfort.
3. The applicant books the room. It is bookable. The book of hours is with the counter, and the hour is the third bell, and the counter confirms the booking.
4. The applicant enters at the hour booked, retrieves Mr. Nott, and leaves by the door by which he entered, which is not the same door and will not be found in the west wall.
5. The applicant returns the form, the room and Mr. Nott, and reports to the Faculty within three days on the first form of report, and pays nothing, the room being unable to charge.

The procedure stops at the head of the first step. The counter was filed with the room on 6 March 1487, under R, with its lamp, its clock, the only completed Form 7B known, and the Library's only copy of *Form 7B for Dummies*. A form issued at a counter, where the counter is with the room and the room is not in the building, is issued nowhere. That is the circularity which has kept this Faculty patient since 1461. The Faculty therefore recommends patience, as it did in 1462, in the words of the minute of 14 October: *the room is in order; the form is not.*

## 9. The Objection of the Committee on Standing Matters

The Committee on Standing Matters, to which this thesis was referred on 2 November 1504, entered the following objection in its minutes of 19 November, in the Committee's own voice:

> "The Committee has read the thesis with attention and finds it orderly, well indexed and, in the matter of the filing, correct. The Committee does not find the room. The Faculty asserts a room of four desks and a river window on the evidence of a plan of 1223, a survey of 1484, one lamp, and a walker who had been absent forty-four days. In this Committee's experience a walker who has been absent forty-four days will say a great deal, and will say it in good faith, and will be believed; and the Committee notes that the walker in question was detained not an hour, or a day, but eleven hours over four days, by the Faculty that wished to hear him. The Committee further notes that the Faculty's chief exhibit is a form which by its own account does not exist, a counter filed with a room that is not in the building, and a manual catalogued and not held. The Committee cannot visit an exhibit it cannot show. That is the standing-matter. As to the lamp: a lamp is a moveable. As to the plan: a plan is a wish. As to the reader at the second desk: the Committee will not examine a person who will not look up."

The Committee's objection is the strongest put to this thesis, and the Faculty acknowledges its force and its courtesy, and its author, whose habit of granting an opponent his best case has cost the Committee more appeals than any incivility.

## 10. The Faculty's Reply

The Faculty answers from the lamps, from the depositions, and from the Committee's own practice.

From the lamps. The annual return of lamps is a count, taken on the eleventh day of March since 1411 by two clerks of the Registry. On 17 March 1487 it marked five lamps absent, four found later in the wrong series, in the wrong rooms, and the fifth never found. The count is the Committee's own instrument: the Committee ordered the return of lamps in 1411, and that order discovered the filing of 1487. The Faculty asks the Committee to believe its own clerks.

From the depositions. Three walkers have described that room — Miss Finch in 1471, a second walker in 1493 whose name Dean Quill granted be withheld, and Mr. Thorne in 1502 — and no two descriptions agree, which is the Faculty's whole case: they agree on the room and differ on the furniture, as three witnesses will agree on the walls and differ on the chairs. Miss Finch saw three desks and no reader. The walker of 1493 saw a broken drawer and said the room smelt of lamp oil. Mr. Thorne saw a lit lamp and a reader, and said it smelt of nothing.

From the Committee's own practice. In 1477 the Committee held, in the matter of the fourth arch of the Nine Cloisters, that a threshold described by three witnesses and entered by one is a threshold, and that it will not require a fourth. The Faculty asks nothing further, and will hold the Committee to its rule in the third reading room at the third bell, where the walker of 1502 will be examined a fifth time, and where the counter is not.

## 11. The Unauthorised Attempt of 1499

The Faculty records the only attempt on the procedure to date, that the recommendation of §8 be understood as a finding and not a preference.

In the early hours of 3 August 1499, Mr. Hob Brindle, licensed walker of Low Town, whose licence had lapsed fourteen days earlier, entered the north corridor with the key to Reading Room 3, three lamps, a thirty-fathom rope, a hand-bell, a bag of sand, and a plan traced from the plans of 1223. His purpose was to fetch Mr. Nott out by the corridor, which he held to be a room with the door already open.

He was recovered thirty-one hours later at the foot of the north corridor stair, with his coat inside out, his left boot off, and his rope paid out to the full thirty fathoms and cut clean at the end. Two of the three lamps were gone, and his bell was silent, not broken. His bag of sand was returned to him in his hat, dry. He had marked his route with sand, and the sand was returned to him in his hat, and on this the whole matter turns, since a person who loses his way does not gather up the sand.

The Faculty fined Mr. Brindle nine marks four bells for entering without a booking, and suspended his licence for five years. He paid seven marks in the first year and the remainder in the third, and has twice asked whether he may try again, and been told that he may not.

The Faculty's statement of 11 August 1499 is entered here in full, and stands:

> "The draught in the north corridor is not to be stopped, and the corridor is not a room for entering. The walker of the third of August went in by the door, which is the wrong way, and came out by the door he went in at, which is the way that follows. The Faculty notes for the guidance of walkers that the corridor receives; it does not admit. The loss of two lamps is entered to the room."

## 12. Patience as an Instrument

The Faculty is asked, once or twice a decade, whether patience is a policy or an excuse. The Faculty's answer is that patience is an instrument, that it is the only instrument on the shelf, and that an instrument which cannot be taken down is still an instrument.

There is a reason for it beyond procedure. Of the seven walkers genuinely absent, three were recovered — in 1471, 1447 and 1438 — and all three without a form, without an hour booked, and without anything done. Miss Finch came back of herself, in the wrong frame, at the second bell, with her lamp unlit. Mr. Sallow came back through the door he had entered, three days later, complaining of the smell and of his coat. Miss Rill came back in the Tenterfields, eight days out of her way, by a route the surveyors of 1488 declined to walk. Not one of the three was retrieved. Each arrived.

The Faculty returns to the question with which it began. Mr. Nott is not lost. He is in the fourth reading room, approved in 1223, measured twice, filed under R on 6 March 1487, and bookable at the third bell. The room has a lamp, a bell, and a draught that is not to be stopped. The reader at the second desk has an octavo manual with rubbed boards and does not look up.
