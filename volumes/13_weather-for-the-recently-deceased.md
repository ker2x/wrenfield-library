## Abstract (as filed)

This thesis sets out the nine readings of the afterlife, the comfort each may lawfully give and the comfort each may not; it reports the misprint of 1466, its complaints, its refunds and the clerk answerable for it; it distinguishes the diviner's office from the priest's, and concedes that the forecast may be wrong. Form 7B, *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time*, is not bound with this volume; it was never obtained.

## 1. The Nine Standard Readings

The Faculty certifies nine readings and no others, taken at the hour of death where the diviner is sent for in time, and at notification where he is not. A tenth reading, *Clear, With Kin Near*, was proposed by the chapter of St. Ives in 1441 and refused: the instrument cannot see persons, only conditions, and a reading mentioning kin would be a prophecy about a place rather than a report of it.

1. **Fair Interval.** Clear, with good light. No duration is stated, and none may be supplied.
2. **Long Fair.** Clear and continuing. The only reading carrying a duration, entered as "continuing".
3. **Overcast That Does Not Fall.** Grey, dry overhead.
4. **Gentle Rain.** Warm rain, without wind; the ground takes it.
5. **Rain, Lifting by Morning.** Rain with a stated end.
6. **Low Cloud on the Hill.** Distance obscured; the deceased not visible from here.
7. **Wind Without Rain.** Movement without change; uneasy, and licensed as uneasy.
8. **Hard Frost, Bright.** Cold, clear, still.
9. **No Reading Obtainable.** The instrument returns nothing. This is a reading, not a failure, and is entered as a reading.

I have taken some three thousand of them. The ninth is the one most often taken for a refusal. It is not: the diviner has still attended, at the hour.

## 2. What Each Reading Is Licensed to Say

The licence is the *Warrant to Forecast in Cases of Bereavement*, renewed every third year at 2 bells, 5 clipped bells for the duplicate. Every diviner buys the duplicate, the first having been mislaid at the worst hour. The warrant recites four abstentions, the fourth governing this table: **no fee for comfort, the fee being for the reading only.** A diviner sells the hour, not the consolation.

**Table I — The nine readings against lawful comfort, with the fee.**

| Reading | Lawfully given to | Withheld from | What it may not promise | Fee |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fair Interval | Any household that asks | No one; given freely | That it holds | 1 bell 6 clipped |
| Long Fair | Households with children under twelve | The dying who have asked to be told plainly | That it is good | 1 bell 6 clipped |
| Overcast That Does Not Fall | Households in dispute over the body | The household of a suicide, without the priest's note | That the deceased is at rest | 2 bells |
| Gentle Rain | Any household that asks | No one | Warmth of any kind | 2 bells |
| Rain, Lifting by Morning | Households in the first three days | Households past the ninth day, without a second reading | That the morning is theirs | 2 bells |
| Low Cloud on the Hill | Households that have asked to be told the truth | The household that has asked to be told only what is kind | That absence and presence differ | 2 bells 4 clipped |
| Wind Without Rain | Households that ask twice | Households in which a child is dying | That it will settle | 3 bells |
| Hard Frost, Bright | The old, who generally ask for it | Households in the first night | That it will not deepen | 3 bells |
| No Reading Obtainable | Any household that asks | No one | Anything at all | 6 clipped |

The word *happy* appears neither in the table nor in the warrant. Proposed in 1371, 1402, 1441 and 1488, it has been refused four times; the refusals are printed in the Faculty's *Register of Refused Wording*, a volume I recommend and have never seen another man open.

## 3. On the Prevailing Fairness of the Forecast

Fair is the reading in thirty-one returns in every hundred. The others follow at a distance: Gentle Rain in nineteen, Overcast in eleven, Low Cloud in nine, Wind Without Rain in eight, Hard Frost in seven, Rain Lifting in six, Long Fair in five, No Reading Obtainable in four. Fair is therefore both the commonest reading and the least satisfying.

The bereaved do not ask what the weather is. They ask whether it is well, and the warrant permits the diviner to say only that nothing is falling. *Fair* withholds the reassurance of settled good; it also withholds the dignity of grief. *Gentle Rain* at least permits tears in the house. The Alderwick chapter has three times petitioned, in 1402, 1441 and 1488, for a reading called **Fair and Likely to Remain So**, and three times been refused: mercy that cannot be withdrawn is not mercy but merchandise. I have issued Fair six hundred times and never watched it comfort anybody.

## 4. The Widow of Bell Court, and the Year of Rain

The case is entered in the Faculty's letter-book for Michaelmas term 1467, and is the one on which I was examined.

The widow of Bell Court was a laundress named Aelith Marle. Her husband, Corin, a lighterman, died in November 1466 of a fever taken off the water, in a house at the Bell Court end of the rope-walk that took the river damp. The diviner sent for was Master Orrin Vell of the Nine Cloisters, warrant number 1,208, who had bought his *Diviner's Pocket Table* from the Press in the spring. The reading returned a clear interval with good light. He opened his table, found at Fair Interval the word **Rain**, and told her, in the words the table licensed, that it was raining where her husband was, and that rain in that place was not cold.

She did not question it. She was thirty-four years old and had two children living. She bore the rain, as she put it, for eleven months. She had a new roof put on the back room that had no need of it, at four marks and five bells she could not afford, because if it rained where he was she did not want him to think the house had gone to ruin. She stopped laying his coat out on the dry nights, a wet man having no use for a coat. By February she spoke of him as being out in it, and for a year the family spoke of the dead man as one who was out in the rain and would come in when it suited him.

In the October of 1467 the corrected sheet was pasted into the parish table at St. Werburgh's, and Master Vell, who had seen the Press's notice, walked to Bell Court and told her that the reading had been clear, that the table had been misprinted, and that her husband was not in rain and never had been. He refunded the two bells, unasked, and did it in front of no one, which I regard as the act of a man who knew what he had taken from her.

Her complaint is dated the fourth of November 1467 and is entered at folio 61, and its burden is not cruelty but accuracy. She complained not of having been comforted but of having been comforted by what was not true, so that she held a year of peace belonging to a different year than the one she had lived, and asked the Faculty what she was to do with it. The clerk wrote in the margin the single word **Compliment**, and the Prefect endorsed it *a credit to the office*. It is the Faculty's position, and mine, that a complaint about accuracy is the only complaint a diviner should fear. The eleven months are not recoverable; the entry is not nothing.

## 5. The Misprint of 1466, and What Was Asked

In March 1466 the Wrenfield Press set the *Diviner's Pocket Table* from standing type, and at **Fair Interval** the word **Fair** was set as **Rain**. Four hundred copies ran before the error was found, and ninety-one diviners used them for a year, in Alderwick and the four parishes across the water. For that year the bereaved of this city were told, in a form they were entitled to take as authoritative, that it rained where their dead were. My father was a diviner, and I remember the corrected sheet pasted into our parish table, and his saying that a man should check his tools before going among the grieving. Of the complaints entered in the Faculty's letter-book for those fourteen months there were two hundred and eleven.

**Table II — The complaints of 1466–1467, by class, and what each was given.**

| Class of complainant | Number | What each asked for | What each was given |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bereaved, on accuracy | 153 | A corrected reading, and a statement of what was true | Corrected reading, free; a written statement, free |
| Bereaved, on the fee | 49 | Refund of 1 bell 6 clipped | Refund of 2 bells; the excess by Faculty rule |
| Bereaved, on the manner of delivery | 9 | An apology from the diviner who had spoken | Apology, spoken in the house; travelling money 4 clipped |
| Diviners, on their registers | 34 | The registers received as filed, not as reprinted | Received as filed; a certified copy at 5 clipped |
| Diviners, on the Press | 11 | The cost of the recalled sheets | Three-quarters of the cost, the rest being their own care |
| Parish clerks, on the pasted sheet | 6 | A printed instruction for the pasting-in | The instruction, printed, in the *Register of Refused Wording* |

The 153 on accuracy are, on the Faculty's reading, not complaints at all: a complaint that the forecast was false is the highest mark the office receives, since the complainant has held the diviner to a statement about the world. The nine on the manner of delivery are the ones the Faculty feared, and was right to fear: a man told that his wife is in rain, in a doorway, with the neighbours listening, is injured beyond the reach of any refund.

## 6. The Rule of the Third Reading

The nine readings are taken as follows, and the order may not be varied, the instrument being read against itself: a reading taken out of order answers the previous reading rather than the case.

1. **Attend first.** Send word to the priest and wait at the gate until word comes back; the diviner does not enter a house the priest has not released.
2. **Fix the warrant.** Produce the *Warrant to Forecast* and read the four abstentions aloud.
3. **Take the death-hour reading** if the body is still in the house, and the notification reading if it is not.
4. **Enter the reading** in the register in ink, before speaking it.
5. **Speak the licensed comfort**, in the words of Table I and no others.
6. **Withhold the licence of the next reading**; do not volunteer what the following morning would have shown.
7. **Take the second reading** on the third day, at the hour of the death, without fee.
8. **Take the third reading** on the ninth day if it is asked for.
9. **Close the case** with the entry of the ninth day, and send the register to the Faculty within the month, on pain of four bells.

**The rule of the third reading** is the whole of the ninth step's difficulty. If the same reading returns three times in one household within one year, and that household is not one of a single person, the diviner shall read no fourth time, shall not say that the weather has settled, and shall send for the priest and stand down. The instrument is not answered in such a case; it is repeating. I have stood down twice in nineteen years; both times the household was angry with me, and both times I have thought it the right anger.

## 7. The Diviner and the Priest

A diviner forecasts conditions. A priest binds and looses. The two offices are not permitted to appear to overlap, which is why in this city they walk to a house together and part at the gate. The diviner may say what the weather is doing; he may not say what it is for, may not absolve, bury, or hold another man's book.

Father Ambrose Wexley, priest of St. Werburgh's, put the opposing case to me in writing in the March of 1486, when this volume was a paper read to the Faculty, and has given me leave to print it.

> *To Master Rook. You say you forecast conditions. I say that in this parish the hour of a death is my office entire, and that a man who tells a widow what the weather is where her husband is has entered it, whatever papers he holds. It is not forecast that she needs at that hour; it is the Church's word, that he is in God's hands, which speaks to his good and not his comfort. Your table licenses you to tell her that nothing is falling. Standing where I stand, that is no comfort at all, but the withholding of the only comfort there is, done with a form in the hand. You do not absolve, you say. No. You do something harder to answer: you stand in the room where absolution belongs and offer a weather report instead, and the widow, not knowing the difference, takes it. If the forecast comforts, it is doing my work; if it does not comfort, it is not worth the fee; and you cannot have it both ways for 1 bell 6 clipped.* — A. Wexley, 4.iii.1486

My reply, which the Faculty adopted with two amendments, is this. The priest is right that the house has only one room to be comforted in. He is wrong that the two offices therefore compete. The priest speaks of the good of the dead; the diviner reports the conditions of the dead: two ledgers, and no entry in one can be carried into the other. What the priest cannot do is what the hour most often requires: say something specific, in a form the bereaved can hold and keep. A widow can hold a weather report, put it in a drawer, and take it out on a dry night and read it again. The Church's word will not go in a drawer, being of an order that resists drawers; it must be believed, and at the hour of death belief is precisely what has failed.

The warrant therefore recites four abstentions, which I read aloud whether the household wishes it or not: **no sacrament; no promise; no name for the dead beyond the birth-name; no fee for comfort.** A priest may divine if he holds the warrant; a diviner may not priest, with or without orders. The division was agreed by Faculty and Diocese in 1371 and re-agreed four times since, on each occasion in a document of which no copy survives.

## 8. The Letter of Margery Ash, and What I Answered

The Faculty requires that a volume of this class print one request entire. The following is from the letter-book for 1483, folio 44. She was a smith's wife of Low Town; the clerk called her hand *slow and without abbreviation*.

> *Master Rook. I am told that you are the man who tells what it is like where they are, and I ask you to come. My mother died on the second of this month and I was not with her, being at my sister's, and I cannot be easy for not knowing whether she is cold. I do not ask you to tell me she is happy, for I know that is not in your table. I ask what the reading is, and I ask what it may not say, because I would rather be told the edge of a thing than find it later on my own. Tell me also plainly whether you will come for the fee or for nothing, as I have four children and the smith has had no work since the frost, and I will not be behindhand with a man who has done me a kindness. If the reading is bad, say so at the door. I will take it at the door. I need it said once. — Margery Ash, the 9th of November, 1483.*

I went. The reading was Low Cloud on the Hill, and I said at the door that it was bad, and that its comfort was that she was not visible, which is not the same as not there. She asked whether I had seen a low cloud lift, and I said I had, on Candlemas Hill, in an hour. That was not in the table and not in the licence, and I have never decided whether that fell within the abstentions. She paid me the six clipped bells herself, and said the reading was the better for not being a kindness.

## 9. The Clerk, the Refunds, and the Entry in the Book of Errors

The misprint was made by a compositor named Orrin Tace, at the Press from 1455, working from standing type at the end of a Friday. He set **Rain** for **Fair**, did not read the forme back, and was not dismissed. The Prefect of the Press, A. Heggarty, entered it as *an error of the eye and not of the hand*, fined Tace two bells and the price of the sheets, and set the corrected forme. I name Tace because the Faculty's book of errors names him, and a man who sets an error into four hundred copies ought to be in a register rather than out of one.

The Faculty's entry, in the *Book of Errors Kept by the Prefect of Divination*, is the shortest in that volume:

> 1466. Item. The word *Fair* printed as *Rain* at Fair Interval, in the *Diviner's Pocket Table*, by the Press. Four hundred sheets. Ninety-one warrants affected. Complaints, 211. Refunded, 909 readings at 1 bell 6 clipped, by Faculty rule at 2 bells, being 60 marks 12 bells 6 clipped, paid from the Press's bond and not from the contingency. The Press's bond stands at 40 marks, and stands short. Response adopted: the register is the record and the table is the tool. Censure: none. Remedy: a proof-sheet to be returned by the diviner before use, at his own cost of 2 clipped bells.

The refund figure requires a word. Nine hundred and nine readings were refunded, but not because that many were taken in error: the Faculty refunds the case and not the reading, and a household in which four readings had been taken was entered at four. The difference between the bond and the payments, twenty marks twelve bells six clipped, was made good by the Diocese on the written understanding that the Press would not again set a table from standing type.

## 10. Whether the Forecast Can Be Wrong

It can, and that sentence has cost the Faculty more correspondence than any other in its literature. The instrument is an instrument, read by a man at an hour when the house is full, the light poor, and someone waits to be told something. I have been wrong toward Fair when the reading was Wind Without Rain, and knew it twice. In one house I said nothing, and have not forgiven myself; in the other I corrected myself a week later, at no charge, and was not forgiven.

My argument, which I make without expecting to be believed, is that this does not matter, or not as it is said to. The reading is not a report of a place no one has been to and returned from; it is an act of attention when attention is all that is left, in a form the bereaved can hold. The widow of Bell Court was wrong for eleven months, and unaccompanied for none of them. The Faculty holds that a forecast is a statement about conditions, and a false one a falsehood to be answered with a refund. Mine is that it is a statement from a person to a person at the worst hour, and that the Faculty has confused the accuracy of the instrument with the faithfulness of the reading. The instrument may be wrong. The attendance need not be.

## 11. Conclusion

The office is to attend, to speak within the licence, to enter what he has read before he speaks it, and to come back when he is wrong. What the bereaved are given is not the weather but the hour; and the hour cannot be misprinted.
