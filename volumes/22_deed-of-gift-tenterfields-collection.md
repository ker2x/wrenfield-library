## Abstract (as filed)

By deed enrolled in 1501 the donor gave the Library the Tenterfields Collection, one hundred and forty-one volumes, upon three covenants. The Library has since become unable to satisfy all three and has not ceased to be in breach. The compiler tabulates their standing, records the Bursar's replies, and argues that a covenant which cannot now be performed is not thereby void. Form 7B is absent; a Form 7B/absence is filed in its place.

## 1. The covenants, and their present standing

The deed is brief, as deeds are when the donor intends them enforced. It recites the gift, its receipt, the accession of the volumes under the Classification of 1388, and three covenants, each introduced by *the Library shall*. The Bursar's office accepted all three without qualification, without enquiry, and, the compiler records, without reading them, the instrument being engrossed on both sides of a skin. An unreleased note observes that an office which accepts an unread covenant has performed an act of faith, and that faith is not a defence known to the Faculty.

## 2. The three covenants, each with the Library's annotation

**Clause 1, upon the window.** The covenant cannot be performed, the Faculty requiring the north range windows to stand open for the pollen counts, a requirement the Under-Librarian has refused in writing twice, in 1502 and 1503; the dispute is treated in AC 118.7. The Library's annotation reads: *satisfiable only by prevailing against a Faculty; the Library has not prevailed.* A covenant to keep a window shut is a covenant against a Faculty, and the Library is not the larger of the two.

**Clause 2, upon the bees.** The covenant cannot be performed, the apiary having held since 1389 the right to bring claims through its keeper, and a single bee on its own account; it has done so twice, and the schedule of that year sets out the fees and the rule that a settlement be read aloud to the apiary. The Library's annotation reads: *satisfiable only by the removal of a party; the Library removes nuisances.* A nuisance is removed, and a party is served. Service was attempted in 1498, and returned unopened, the hive not being named in the style of the suit, and again in 1502, when it was served and answered within the year.

**Clause 3, upon the room.** The covenant cannot be performed, the room having been filed under R in the reordering of the Registry in March 1487, with its counter, its lamp, and the only completed Form 7B, and being now a corridor of those dimensions. The Library's annotation reads: *satisfiable in the plans; the plans are in good order.* The room is procedurally extant and bookable, and cannot be kept in good order, order in it having been determined once. The draught in it is not to be stopped, and the lamp, by the annual return, is still in it. The plans of 1223 show it; the surveyor's note of 1484 finds its north wall two feet four inches thick.

## 3. The Bursar's three replies

On clause 1: *The Library does not open windows. The Faculty opens them. The covenant binds the Library, and the Library is not the hand.*

On clause 2: *The bees are not upon the premises. They are within the jurisdiction, which is worse; once a thing is within the jurisdiction the office has no further view.*

On clause 3: *The room is in the plans, and the plans are in the Library, and the Library keeps its plans in good order. The covenant does not say where the room must be.*

The compiler prints the three replies without comment, in obedience to an instruction to print them without comment, which he prints. He adds that the first distinguishes an act from a hand, the second a place from a jurisdiction, the third a covenant from a building, and that together they establish that the Library promised nothing it could do.

## 4. The bee of 1497, told in full

The first breach in earnest was not a window and not a room. It was a bee, or eleven thousand of them. On the ninth of April, 1497, a swarm settled in the outside recess of the second window of the north range. The Under-Librarian noticed it on the eleventh and recorded it in the day-book as weather. It was noticed properly on the fourteenth by Marigold Thistlewick, M.M.M., of the Faculty of Agricultural Curses, taking the April pollen count, who filed on Form AC/3 that afternoon, in three lines, the third of which matters: *there is pollen on the sill that is not ours, and it is going into my counts, and I am not counting for a hive.*

The complaint went to the Apiary's office at Bramblegate, as the schedule of 1389 requires, which appointed Mr. Obadiah Finch, keeper to the Hive of the Nine Meadows, as inspector; he attended on the second of May. His report, dated the fourth, found a settled colony; a single queen, marked, though not by the Library; comb four inches deep; and the entrance opening upon the north range at eleven feet four inches. On the fifth he served notice on the Library; on the ninth he entered the finding in the register: *standing, 1497.* Its effect is that the hive had standing through its keeper, and that the keeper declined to withdraw the claim. Under the schedule the hive decides for itself. The Library had not suffered a nuisance but acquired a party in its wall, with a cause of action accruing daily.

The Bursar's office wrote to the donor on the sixteenth of May, 1497:

> *Sir. The office is instructed to inform you that a hive of bees is settled in the recess of the second window of the north range, that the apiary hath found the same to be a bee upon the premises, and that the hive hath standing to claim. The office is advised that it cannot be removed as a nuisance, being no nuisance, and cannot be proceeded against as a trespass, being a party. The office has done nothing, which is the course the office prefers, and the cost of any settlement will be met from the ordinary. Signed, for the Bursar, A. Greaves, Clerk.*

The donor did not answer, being in his last illness; the letter was found among his papers after his death in 1502. The matter was settled that autumn, on terms read aloud to the apiary before two witnesses, both stung. The penalty was fourteen marks four bells: the apiary's standing fee of four marks, and three bells a day for the twenty-four days the hive stood upon the premises, a count the office did not contest, having been taken by the hive. The compensation to the donor was three marks six bells, the value of the pollen the Faculty's counts had carried off that season, made over to the estate in the year following as a payment and not a fine. The cost of the apiary's claim and of removing a hive that had standing to sue was four marks three bells eight clipped bells, paid twice: to the apiary's remover, who attended in October with a skep and declined to proceed, a party not being removable without its consent; and again in March to Mr. Finch, the inspector and not the remover, who removed nothing, the hive having departed of itself. The window has three times been opened since, and the recess twice reoccupied, in 1502 and 1504, the apiary notified on each occasion: the only covenant kept.

## 6. The clause itself

The covenant is quoted from the enrolled copy, by permission of the Registry, which granted permission on Form R/9 and has not recorded the grant.

> *And the said Library shall, in consideration of the said gift, keep and observe the following: that no window be opened in the north range of the said Library, whether for air, for light, for the counting of pollen, or for any cause whatsoever; and that no bees be upon the premises of the said Library, whether hive, skep, swarm or single bee, and whether the same be brought thither or come of itself; and that the fourth reading room, late the room of the maps, be kept in good order, swept, lamped and warmed as the other reading rooms of the said Library are kept; provided always that if the said Library be prevented in any of the said covenants by any Faculty, jurisdiction, court, party or standing of any kind, the same shall not be a breach but a prevention, and the Library shall certify the prevention to the donor within the year, and the covenant shall stand.*
>
> *And to this the said Library hath set its hand and seal. Sealed with the seal of the Wrenfield Library, being the Compass and the Bell, in green wax, on the eleventh day of March, 1501, in the presence of the Clerk of Appeals and of the Under-Librarian, whose hand trembles and is nonetheless admitted.*

The proviso has never been certified. The compiler has searched the Register and finds no certificate of prevention, and finds in the margin a note in the Under-Librarian's hand observing that a certificate of prevention would be a notice to the donor that the Library intends to do nothing, and that the office has preferred silence.

## 7. The donor's heir, in her own voice

The donor died in 1502. The estate is represented by his daughter, whose letter is printed at her request and against the office's advice.

> *To the Bursar of the University of Alderwick. My father gave you one hundred and forty-one volumes and a house's papers, and asked three things of you, and you have failed in all three. You have opened the window he asked you to shut, and when we complained you told us the Faculty opened it. You have harboured a bee he asked you to keep off, and when we complained you told us the bee is a party. You have lost a room he asked you to keep in order, and when we complained you told us the room is in the plans. We do not want your plans. We want the room, or the gift back. A gift made on conditions you cannot keep is not a gift; it is a loan you have spent. Return the collection and we will say nothing further. Keep it, and we will say a great deal, on such forms as you are pleased to invent.*

The letter is dated the fourteenth of November, 1503, and received on the nineteenth, the delay being attributable to the forms counter, closed for the counting.

## 8. The Bursar's reply, and a footnote on the word *return*

The Bursar replied within the year, which the office records as promptness:

> *Madam. The gift is accepted, accessioned and stamped. What is given to the Library is given to the Library. The covenants stand, and the Library stands in breach of them, and the breach is recorded in this instrument, which is more than most donors receive. As to return: the collection cannot be returned, the collection being in the collection. The office cannot undo an accession, the Classification admitting no such operation, and the Register admitting no erasure.*

*The compiler's footnote on the word return:* the estate uses the word in three senses, only one of which the office considered. The first is delivery of the volumes to the house, which the office answers by the Register. The second is restoration of the donor's position, never in the office's hands, the position having lapsed at the donor's death and the covenants not being heritable. The third, and the one the lady intends, is the return of a promise unperformed: the Library holds the collection, the deed holds the promise, and the promise is the part that went out of the building in 1487. The compiler has drafted a form for the third sense, in six parts, and has not filed it, the fee being 2 marks.

## 9. Procedure for the inspection of a room that is not in the building

The office has adopted this procedure:

1. Present yourself at the north range at eight of the clock, with a rule, a lamp, and Form EA/19 in triplicate.
2. Measure the north wall at the second bay and the fourth; record both, which will agree.
3. Confirm that no hinge has been fitted, renewed or oiled since the last inspection; record its absence as a satisfaction of clause 1.2, not a breach.
4. Count the sill-plates through the glass, and do not open the window to count them; plates not visible are counted absent.
5. Proceed to the Registry and read the entry under R, unaltered since 1487 and not to be altered.
6. Ask at the forms counter for the room, and record the answer: that it is bookable, and booked at the room's own counter.
7. Book the room, record the hour, and note that the hour will pass.
8. Leave a sealed wrapper under the counter, addressed to the room, stating the purpose of the inspection; it will be returned by the draught, or not, which is recorded.
9. On returning, complete Form EA/19 in triplicate: one copy to the Registry, one to the Bursar's office, one to the room, the last retained in your keeping, the room having no counter at which to leave it.

The inspector must not open the window to establish that it is shut; the covenant is against opening and the inspection is no exception to it. The inspector must not enter the corridor to look for the room, the entry being indistinguishable from a possession the office does not claim. The inspector must not address the hive, name it in any document, or accept anything from it. He must not wind the clock.

## 10. Whether a covenant that cannot be performed is void

It is not. A covenant is void at the outset if what it undertakes was impossible then. Each of the three was possible in 1501 — the windows were then shut, the bees over the river, the room thought to be in the building — and each became impossible afterwards, by a Faculty, by the standing of a party, and by the filing of a room. Impossibility at the deed defeats the deed; impossibility after it defeats only performance, and leaves the deed standing, and the Library in breach.

The compiler must record one qualification. The bee was upon the premises in 1497 and the deed was executed in 1501, and the office accepted clause 2.1 while the recess was occupied. Either the office did not know, which is its own account, or it knew and accepted a covenant already incapable, which would make clause 2.1 void at the deed and leave the estate the remedy it seeks: the covenant gone, the gift standing. The Bursar observed that a void covenant is not a returned gift, and that the office prefers the breach.

## 11. On breach without remedy, and what the file lacks

The office has considered whether the covenants might be satisfied in kind at all. A window may be glazed and sealed, which is not opening it; the Faculty has removed the glazing twice. A bee may be invited to withdraw, which is not removal; the apiary has not answered. The fourth reading room may be entered, which is not keeping it in good order, and is barred without a Form 7B. Each covenant can be satisfied only by a party the Library does not control, which is a promise to be lucky. The office never claimed to be lucky. What it offers instead is the record: the instrument, the schedule, the breach, the intact seal. The compiler has been instructed to call this an accommodation, and notes that a documented breach is still a breach.

The file lacks one thing. Form 7B, the Declaration of Non-Interference with Time, is required for every thesis and every instrument touching the fourth reading room, and is absent. The compiler has filed a Form 7B/absence in its place, which is not a Form 7B, and has stamped it twice. The only completed Form 7B known to the Library was filed with the room in March 1487 and went with it. For any later hand finding this volume bound with the deeds of 1499 and 1500: the absence of Form 7B is not the cause of the breaches and would not have prevented them, and a form blamed for everything is a form never examined.
