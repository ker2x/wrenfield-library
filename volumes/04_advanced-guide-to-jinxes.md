## Abstract (as filed)

The jinx is the only malefic operation in common use which its practitioner performs before deciding to perform it. This edition adds chapters on latency and the eight classes, the withdrawal of the third edition in 1488, a schedule of onsets with the fee for reversal, the accreditation requirements of 1494, and a reply to the Faculty of Harmless & Decorative Curses. Form 7B is not present in the copy deposited, and the author has left the matter to the Committee on Standing Matters, which has not met since 1489.

## 1. The Advanced Student, Defined

The case that produced this edition is set out in full at §4. On the ninth of Fourthmonth 1488 Perrin Ashcombe, D.P.M., of Low Town, followed a printed interval at p. 44 of the third edition, named his jinx aloud on the seventeenth in the belief that he was preserving a right of reversal, and completed the first mark of the irrevocable in the act of preserving it. The person jinxed, Margery Tull, laundress of the Tenterfields, was never told, discharged the maleficence for nine years, and was assessed at 8 marks 4 bells. Ashcombe had done nothing the Faculty's book did not tell him to do; the fourth edition exists because a handbook that instructs wrongly is worse than no handbook.

The advanced student is not defined by competence but by a prior incident, and this volume is written for the student who has already jinxed someone by accident. Legibility requires three statements in the candidate's own hand:

1. The date, hour, and place of the jinx, and the place of the person jinxed.
2. The class of the jinx, on the reckoning at §2, and its present state of onset.
3. Whether the jinx is still reversible, and if not, on which of the three marks at §3 it ceased to be so.

The Faculty's charter of 1474 defines the advanced student as one who holds all three statements, and prints the roll of the eleven who have held them. The roll is closed, and printed in full, because candidates believe it longer.

The Green Register of Accidental Practitioners holds 611 names; of its 461 entries recorded *without result*, 118 were filed by practitioners who believed they had produced a curse and had produced a discourtesy. A jinx declared after its onset is recorded and not reversed; a jinx not declared is fined at 11 bells.

## 2. Latency, and the Eight Classes

A jinx runs from utterance to onset, and the interval is not a delay in the operation but the operation working; a jinx that has not discharged has not failed but is at work, which is why the Faculty refuses to describe a jinx by its result. Four classes were recognised in 1461 and eight since 1474: the Heptadic was separated from the Deferred when it was observed that a seven-year jinx and a hundred-day jinx have nothing in common but their patience, and the Irrevocable from the Terminal when it was observed that some jinxes discharge and some can never be recalled.

The eight classes give the Faculty 156 assigned forms, of which the candidate is examined on forty. The fourteen forms most often met, with their classes, onsets, reversibility and certified fee for reversal, are tabulated below; the fee is set by the practitioner and may not fall below the fee charged for casting. 1 mark is 20 bells and 1 bell is 12 clipped bells.

## 3. Reversibility, and the Three Marks of the Irrevocable

A jinx is reversible until its first onset. The reversal restores the subject and not the practitioner, which is a separate proceeding under §7. A jinx becomes irrevocable on the earliest of three marks:

1. **The mark of naming.** The jinx is named aloud in the presence of the person jinxed, whether by the practitioner, a third party, or the person jinxed. The naming completes the operation. No apology after that hour has been found to alter the record, and the Faculty has twice tested the proposition, in 1479 and 1491.
2. **The mark of repetition.** The person jinxed uses the word "again" of the event. This mark is the most frequently missed, the practitioner being usually present and usually talking.
3. **The mark of the Register.** The jinx is entered in the Green Register, which the Faculty requires, and which therefore makes every properly filed jinx irrevocable on filing.

The third mark is the Faculty's most persistent administrative complaint: members who file promptly lose the option of reversal, and members who delay are fined for delay. The Committee on Standing Matters has twice declined to resolve this, in 1471 and 1483. Of the 96 reversals certified between 1474 and 1493, 71 were performed within nine days of utterance and 21 on misreckoned onsets.

## 4. The Withdrawal of 1488

The third edition advised, at p. 44, a settling interval of three days before the naming of a jinx, the fee for reversal falling by two bells where the class declared itself. The advice was printed thus: *let the jinx stand three days from utterance, then name it and reckon the class.* The manuscript had read *from onset*. The printer's reader, Elias Marle, amended the phrase to *from utterance*, thinking the manuscript unclear, and entered the amendment in the margin of the copy sent to the Faculty, where it was filed on the second of Thirdmonth and not read.

Perrin Ashcombe, D.P.M., of Low Town, licensed 1484, followed that rule on the ninth of Fourthmonth 1488, having the day before quarrelled with a carter over the standing of a handcart outside The Weeping Compass. He uttered the Homely Stumble, a Seasonal form, and entered in his day-book that he would name it on the twelfth. He named it on the seventeenth, in Bell Court, before two witnesses and the person jinxed, believing himself within the interval. The jinx had discharged on the fourteenth; on the seventeenth he named it aloud, and completed the first mark himself in the act of preserving it.

Margery Tull, laundress, of the Tenterfields, discharged the maleficence for nine years. She broke a wrist in 1489, a collarbone in 1491, and a toe in 1493, and was dismissed from the Tenterfields laundry in 1492 because she could not be trusted with a full basket on a wet step. Her earnings for the four quarters before dismissal were 6 marks 11 bells; the Faculty assessed her loss at 8 marks 4 bells. She was never told it was a jinx until Thirdmonth 1494, when the Faculty's officer called with notice of the findings, and consented to nothing at any point, which the Faculty recorded as an aggravating circumstance.

The inquiry sat from the twentieth of Fifthmonth to the ninth of Ninthmonth 1488, took evidence from nineteen persons, and found the printed rule defective; the defect introduced at the press and filed with the Faculty; the filing no excuse to the Faculty; and the edition to be withdrawn without errata. Of 400 copies printed, 386 were returned.

The fourth edition omits the page and prints no errata. The setting of 1494 was taken from the standing type of 1488, and the page was struck at the press rather than recomposed, so that the signatures fall four pages short of the third where the interval stood. The index was not reset and still lists *Interval, the settling (3)*, with the page number of a page that is not there. The Faculty has declined three requests to print a note; its reason, minuted once, is that an errata slip is a publication, a correction tells four hundred readers what the rule was, and the Faculty holds that the rule was never the rule. The omission is the correction, and the candidate who annotates it in the margin has understood the edition better than the edition intends.

## 5. The Ethics Appendix, and Why It Is Not Read

Appendix iv, *On the Obligation to Disclose*, runs to thirty-one pages and is bound at the rear, after the index, in paper the printer had left over; the stitching is unbroken in the Faculty's copy. The consultation record for the last thirteen years has two entries: one reader reached p. 4, and one reached p. 3 on two occasions, which the clerk has counted as two consultations. Pages 3 and 4 are reproduced in full, by permission of the Faculty, which has not been asked.

> **Appendix iv, *On the Obligation to Disclose*, pp. 3–4.**
>
> A jinx is disclosed to the person jinxed before its onset, or at the earliest hour at which disclosure is possible, and the practitioner who delays because the disclosure is awkward has not found an exception; he has found the awkwardness the rule was written to require him to bear. The disclosure need not be made in the Faculty's form, but it must be made in words the person jinxed can understand, and in a place where that person may leave. Where the person jinxed is a body corporate, disclosure is made to its clerk; where an animal, to its keeper and to the animal as the keeper directs; where the person jinxed is in transit, the obligation is suspended and not extinguished, and the jinx may not be performed on the assurance that notice will be given later, since the practitioner cannot know when later is. Consent to a jinx is consent to that jinx and not to its consequences, and any consequence the practitioner can foresee must be disclosed, including consequences the subject has said he does not mind. Where disclosure would itself produce the harm the jinx intends, the obligation stands; the exception protects the person jinxed from the harm, and not the practitioner from the conversation.
>
> *Four exceptions are permitted, and none of them is convenience, ignorance, or the subject's temperament.*

The appendix is sound and ought to be read. It is unread because a document which is right about a thing the reader intends to do is a reproach.

## 6. The Harmless Jurisdiction Question

The Faculty of Harmless & Decorative Curses maintains that a jinx causing no harm is not a curse, and so not a matter for this Faculty at all. The dispute is in its ninety-second year; the *Journal of Harmless Maleficence* has been on hiatus pending clarification since 1402. The case is put at length in HC 1.1 / M89 1496, and put fairly:

> "The Jinxcraft Faculty maintains that latency, and not injury, defines minor maleficence, and that a jinx in good order need never discharge. A faculty which claims an instrument for its latency cannot then disclaim it for its latency. Its own roll of practised jinxes contains 461 entries it records *without result*; were absence of harm sufficient to determine jurisdiction, JM would surrender rather more than it intends, and would surrender it to us, which we should be sorry to receive. Jurisdiction follows competence, and three centuries of decorative practice sit with this Faculty: we hold the plates, the instrument-makers, the ornamental hand, and the cases in which nothing was intended to happen and nothing did. JM has no plates and no course in the ornamental hand. Its claim is administrative, not technical. We do not say that a jinx causing no harm is a good thing. We say that it is ours."

The Faculty's answer is that a definition depending on an outcome cannot be applied at the moment of the act. The HC test requires the Faculty to wait for the result before knowing which Faculty governs the utterance, which would mean that neither Faculty could certify a practitioner in advance. A rule admitting harmlessness as a ground of jurisdiction must also admit a jinx as evidence of itself: the practitioner would have to prove his own absence of effect, and the only proof is the passage of time, during which he practises without a licence in one Faculty or the other. That is not a jurisdiction but a wager. The Faculty notes that HC puts its case with great courtesy, and that courtesy is not jurisdiction.

The author concedes the following, and concedes it in full. The Faculty cannot produce a jinx which caused no harm to anybody, ever. The 118 entries in the Green Register that describe a discourtesy are the Faculty's own record that it cannot, and HC has asked for the jinx without harm nineteen times since 1402 and been given a discourtesy eighteen times and, on the nineteenth, the minutes of the Committee on Standing Matters. This is not a concession. The Faculty's position has never been that harmless jinxes exist in number; it is that the question is one of definition, and that a Faculty which defines an instrument by its failure has defined the instrument away.

## 7. The Fee, and the Schedule of Indemnity

The degree of D.P.M. requires two years of supervised practice, a docket of forty recorded jinxes of which no more than nine may be of the Immediate or Diurnal class, the examination at §8, and a bond of 4 marks. Indemnity is written by the University's three underwriters at Bell Court, at a rate set by grade and permitted class.

| Grade | Permitted classes | Indemnity, per annum | Annual fee, in bells |
|---|---|---|---|
| Candidate (unlicensed) | Immediate, under supervision only | 2 marks, 7 marks in the Tenterfields | 2 |
| Grade V | Immediate, Diurnal | 5 marks | 6 |
| Grade IV | Immediate, Diurnal, Seasonal | 7 marks indoors, 11 at fairs, markets, or in the Tenterfields | 9 |
| Grade III | IV, and Annual | 12 marks | 14 |
| Grade II | III, and one Deferred form named on the licence | 19 marks | 21 |
| Grade I | II, and any Deferred or Heptadic form | 28 marks | 34 |
| Faculty Grade | all classes except Irrevocable | 41 marks | 60, and one plate a year |
| Warden Grade | all classes, Irrevocable excepted by standing order | 60 marks, and a surety of 20 | 60, and one plate a year |

Practitioners who cannot state the class of their jinx are indemnified at the Warden rate for the whole of the year in which the inability occurs. Practitioners whose jinx was cast without a fee are indemnified at Grade V whatever their standing. The fee for reversal is certified by the Faculty, tabulated with the onsets at §2, and may not fall below the fee charged for casting.

## 8. The Examination, and the Licence

The examination is in nine parts, sat over two days in the Regent House, in a fixed order; the candidate is not told which of the forty forms he will be asked until the table at part iv is laid.

| Part | Subject | Weight | Fee |
|---|---|---|---|
| i | Definitions, and the answer to *what is a jinx not yet discharged* | 1 | — |
| ii | The eight classes, by band and by form | 2 | — |
| iii | Reckoning of onset: nine forms, no tables | 3 | 3 bells |
| iv | Identification at the table: forty forms laid, ten to be named | 2 | 2 bells |
| v | The three marks, applied to three prepared cases | 2 | — |
| vi | Reversal, performed on an artificial onset | 3 | 4 bells, 6 if indoors |
| vii | Disclosure, spoken to an officer of the Faculty | 1 | — |
| viii | The 1488 interval, and the fourth edition's silence | 1 | — |
| ix | The candidate's own register entry, read aloud | 2 | — |

Parts i to v are sat on the first day; a candidate who fails part iii is not admitted to the second, and the fee for parts iv to vi is not returned.

The licence is issued in the Faculty's name, in two classes, and stamped twice. The second stamp is not the Faculty's requirement; it is the Under-Librarian's, applied to every instrument leaving the building, and the Faculty has twice asked him to stop.

No licence is issued until the candidate's Form 7B — *Declaration of Non-Interference with Time* — is lodged with the Registry. The form is required for every thesis and every licence, and is missing from every thesis in this library. It exists at the forms counter, at the counter of a room that is not in the building, and that counter has been closed since 1487. The Faculty's standing practice since that year is to record the absence in the candidate's file and proceed; it has recorded 34 such absences. The Faculty has never waived the form; it has recorded it, which the Faculty distinguishes from waiver.

## 9. Worked Examples

**A reversal performed: Pellow and the Borrowed Umbrella, 1466.** John Pellow, Grade II, cast the Borrowed Umbrella at an alderman's wife on the fourteenth of Sixthmonth 1466 and charged 4 bells, the market rate and not the schedule rate. The onset is nine months, so Pellow had four months in which to work. He reckoned the onset in writing with the hour of utterance entered by his apprentice, confirmed the subject's consent on the second of Tenthmonth, lodged his notice on the third, and performed the reversal on the fourth, in the rain, in the Tenterfields, and did not repeat it. The reversal was certified and cost 7 bells, the fee being fixed above the fee for casting; the register entry was amended at the fifth hour, and Pellow's licence carried no indorsement. The case is the Faculty's standing proof that the reversible class is worth the trouble, and the origin of the rule that a practitioner may not himself bring the subject within reach.

**A reversal refused: the case of Hedley Orris, 1491.** Orris, Grade III, named a Seasonal jinx upon a corn-factor of Bell Court in Thirdmonth 1491, and the corn-factor, on being told, forbade the reversal in the plainest words the Faculty's file contains. Orris entered the refusal with the subject's own words, referred the matter to the Faculty, and accepted the hearing and the refusal. The file records no fine. The Faculty prints the case because a candidate who reads only of fines will suppose the refusal a formality, and the refusal is the whole of the law on the point.

**An irreversibility discovered late: Spink and the Nodding Acquaintance, 1479.** Elinor Spink cast the Nodding Acquaintance upon a fellow lodger over a matter of spoons on the second of Eighthmonth 1479, and charged nothing. The onset is one afternoon. She named it aloud at breakfast the following morning, before the onset had discharged, believing that a jinx named before onset is a jinx reversed. It is not: the naming before onset is the first mark and the more dangerous for being early. She discovered the irreversibility that afternoon, when the lodger said the words "it will happen again", which is the second mark, and entered it in the register the same day. The Faculty's note, used in part v of the examination, is that Spink preserved nothing, lost the reversal without using it, and reported the whole of it correctly, and is enrolled in the roll at §1 on that account.

The procedure below is the Faculty's, and is to be performed only upon a jinx reversible within §3. It is set out in the body of the work because a procedure bound at the rear is a procedure read after the practitioner has already acted.

1. **Reckon the onset.** State the class from the table at §2 and put the reckoning in writing, the hour of utterance being set down by a witness and not by the practitioner; all 21 misreckonings at §3 were made alone.
2. **Confirm the subject's presence and consent.** The reversal requires the presence of the person jinxed, or of the keeper, clerk, or officer at §5, and that person's consent, spoken, at the hour. Silence is not consent.
3. **Lodge the notice.** Give notice at the forms counter not less than one day before the reversal. Where Form 7B is demanded, record it in the practitioner's own file, with the hour and the officer.
4. **Perform the reversal.** The reversal is spoken once, in the form set at Appendix v of the *Standard Cantrip Grammar*, and is not to be repeated.
5. **Record the class as it stood at the fifth hour after reversal.** The class of a reversed jinx is not struck from the register; the entry is amended, and the amendment is the record.
6. **Return the fee.** Where the reversal fails, the practitioner returns the fee for the reversal and may retain the fee for the casting.
7. **Where the reversal is refused, do nothing.** The refusal is final at the hour it is spoken, and is not reversible by the practitioner's argument, by his distress, or by his offer of a larger sum. Enter it in the register with the subject's own words, and refer the matter to the Faculty, which will not reverse the jinx and will hear him. A practitioner who performs the reversal anyway is to expect a fine of 9 bells and the restoration of the jinx on himself.

## Appendix i. The Findings of the Inquiry of 1488

> **Faculty of Jinxcraft & Minor Maleficence. Inquiry into the third edition of this volume, 1488. Findings, engrossed, sealed, and filed.**
>
> First, that the interval printed at p. 44 of the third edition, requiring the jinx to stand three days from utterance, was followed exactly by the practitioner Perrin Ashcombe, D.P.M., on the ninth, fourteenth, and seventeenth days of Fourthmonth 1488, as his day-book shows.
>
> Second, that the printed interval is defective, that the manuscript sent to the press read *from onset*, and that the difference in the commonest Seasonal forms is not less than nine days.
>
> Third, that the amendment was introduced at the press by the reader Elias Marle, who believed the manuscript ambiguous, and who entered it in the margin of the copy returned to this Faculty, where it was received by the Clerk of Appeals on the second of Thirdmonth 1488 and filed.
>
> Fourth, that the filing of the amendment by this Faculty does not excuse this Faculty, the amendment having stood in the Faculty's own file for two months of a press run.
>
> Fifth, that no finding is entered against Ashcombe, and that the loss to Margery Tull, laundress, of the Tenterfields, be assessed at 8 marks 4 bells and paid from the Faculty's contingency.
>
> Sixth, that the third edition be withdrawn, that no errata be printed, and that the page be omitted from the next edition without a note, the Faculty being of the view that a note would republish the rule.
>
> Seventh, that the reader Elias Marle be fined 3 bells for an amendment beyond his instruction, the fine being the smallest in the Faculty's book and the only one the Faculty has ever regretted not making larger.

*Entered the ninth of Ninthmonth 1488, over the dissent of two of the four members sitting.*
