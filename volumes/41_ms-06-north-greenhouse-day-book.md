# The North Greenhouse Day-Book
### Kept by the staff of the north houses through the insult trials upon *Tenterfield Quiet*

- **Call number:** MS 6.1 / H35 1500
- **Hand:** several; the keeper writes the weather and the water, the second gardener the temperatures, a student's hand the counts
- **Class:** MS — Manuscripts & Personal Papers
- **Dates of entries:** 3 March – 29 September 1500; no gap, though the whole of August is one line a day
- **Provenance:** Sent with the greenhouse accounts in 1501; the covering note lists four houses and this day-book is of the two north ones
- **Collation:** 64 leaves, 61 written; watermark a tulip and a crown
- **Condition:** damp at the spine, which has cockled the middle gathering; the struck line at the twentieth of June is legible

## The Library's Note

This is a day-book: the register of the two north greenhouses of the Faculty of Agricultural Curses, in which the staff entered what was watered, what the houses stood at, what was sown, what came up, and what was done to the rows of *Tenterfield Quiet*, a bean of the Faculty's stock which will not germinate within earshot of laughter. The hands take over without ceremony, so that a day may be written by three persons and a temperature by none. The line struck out at the twentieth of June was struck by the Deputy Under-Librarian, who did not sign the strike; the Library has not removed it. Row F stands at nought throughout.

## The hands, and what each kept

**the 3rd of March.** Fires in both houses. North one at sixty-one degrees, north two at fifty-eight. Watered the two benches of *Tenterfield Quiet*, which are up and leggy and want the light. I am Hesper Mabb, keeper of the north houses these nineteen years, and I keep this book because the Faculty will not pay for a clerk and because a trial that is not written down is an argument. The second gardener, Mr. Coll Tunnock, keeps the temperatures, having bought the glass himself in the year of the frost; the counts are kept by the student, Miss Ada Tarry, who is not to be spoken to while she counts. Anything here in a fourth hand is a person who should not have had the pen.

**the 5th.** Read the protocol to the house, as clause 4 of the Faculty's licence requires: that mockery shall be administered at the eighth bell, by a licensed party, from the schedule, and not departed from. The schedule is one page, and I have copied it into the leaf, and no one has taken it out.

**the 7th.** Frost in the night. North two fell to forty-four. Lost eleven of the Quiet in the second row. Tunnock sat with the fire until the third bell and would not be relieved, and I have entered that too, being what happened.

**the 12th.** Counted. Row A, silence, ninety-eight up of one hundred and four sown. Row B, ordinary speech, one hundred and one. Row C, mockery, nine. Row F was laughed at by seventeen persons for an hour, and stands at nought. Miss Tarry counted twice, and I counted after her and made nought.

**the 19th.** Row C now four. The insult administered at the eighth bell by the licensed party, Mr. Obadiah Rusk, of the Tenterfields, four bells the season and his dinner, who read from the schedule and has done it forty-one days without a departure.

**the 24th.** Sowed the second sowing: rows A to F again, one hundred and four to the row, the seed from the same parcel, weighed at the counter, four ounces to the row. Row F is the visitors' row and is laughed at by whoever comes, which is the whole of the treatment and the whole of the difficulty.

**the 2nd of April.** Thistlewick came from the south houses with a count of forty-one grains to the inch at her wall and stood in my doorway with her eyes streaming, and said the pollen is worse in a closed house; I said the pollen is worse in the north house because the north house is where the trial is. She is not wrong and I am not wrong, and we stood in the door and did not settle it.

**the 4th of April.** Watered all. Row D, laughter, one of one hundred and four, and that one is pale. Row E, contempt by gesture, eleven. The gestures are not in the schedule and were administered by the student, and the question is with the Faculty.

**the 8th.** Dr. Wren came with the sheets of her study and asked to see Row F, and stood at it a long while and then made her objection, which I write down as it was made, since it will be raised again. She said: *A licence is a permission to speak and not an obligation to laugh. If the row is laughed at because the protocol requires it, then the protocol has made you a party to the insult and not a witness to it, and no count taken in this house can be used, because the treatment will have been administered by persons who did not mean it, and a bean is entitled to hear a joke that is a joke.* I said that the protocol is the Faculty's and that the laughing was in the licence at clause 6; she said she had read the licence, and that clause 6 was a mistake. I have no answer to her but to note that the trial is hers and the book is mine.

**the 17th.** Dr. Wren's objection came again in writing, and I answered it in writing. I copy both under one date, as they belong together.

> **To the keeper of the north houses, from R. Wren, D.Th., at the Faculty, the 15th of April 1500.**
>
> Keeper. You have entered my objection and my name against it, which I do not object to. I object to clause 6. A row kept for visitors is not a control and not a treatment; it is a demonstration, and a demonstration is the one thing the licence does not permit, the licence being for mockery administered from the schedule and by a licensed party. If the visitors are to laugh at Row F, then Row F is a treatment without a licence and eleven weeks of it will be struck from the record. Put it to the Deputy Under-Librarian if you will not put it to me. I would rather be told I am wrong by a person who has stood in the house than be agreed with by a Faculty that has not. R. Wren.

**the 21st of April.** My answer, as sent: that the visitors' row is in the licence at clause 6; that the laughing at Row F is not administered from the schedule; that I have kept the row because a row laughed at by the same licensed man at the same bell is a different row from a row laughed at by strangers at no bell, and both are needed if the bean's part is to be had at all; that I will enter every count as taken and will not sum them; and that if the Faculty strikes Row F I will write the strike in the book and not rub out the row. She has accepted the last two and nothing else, which is a fair settlement for April.

**the 2nd of May.** The visitors' row began to be kept daily, the day-book noting the number present and the hour. Twenty-two present on the 2nd; thirty-one on the 9th, the largest hour of the season, and, I am told, after a lecture. Row F was laughed at for one hour on each of these days, and the counts for it are nought, which Miss Tarry has entered in her own hand each time without remark.

**the 20th of May.** Temperature seventy. Watered morning and evening. Row C now two. Row E up to fourteen and standing. Row D has not moved since the 4th of April and the pale one is pale. Dr. Wren came again and did not make her objection again, which I record in her favour.

**the 2nd of June.** Canvas on the north light, against the glare. The Deputy Under-Librarian came in the afternoon about the canvas, the north light being visible from the corridor, and asked what was being done in the house and was told. He did not stay for the eighth bell.

**the 20th of June.** Row F, nothing, and the row has been laughed at every day since the second of May ~~and the keeper is of opinion that the keeper must be the one to begin the laughing~~

**the 21st.** The Deputy Under-Librarian came again at the ninth bell, took the book, read back three weeks of it at the desk, and struck out the line of the twentieth. He did not sign the strike nor say by what authority, only that a day-book is a record and an opinion is not a record. He returned the book at the tenth bell with the strike in it. I have entered the strike, as I said I would, and no opinion about it.

**the 24th.** My own account of the twentieth, so that the record has one. On the 20th of June I was in the house from the fifth bell. North one stood at seventy-six degrees, north two at seventy-one. The seed merchant's man was at the door from the eighth bell and I did not go to him. At the eighth bell there were nine present, the smallest hour since the row began, the warm weather having taken the visitors to the water-meadow. Row F was laughed at for an hour, and I could not hear it, which has not happened to me in the house in nineteen years; I stood at the second bench and could not hear nine people laughing four yards from me. At the ninth bell I counted Row F without Miss Tarry and made nought, as she had. At the tenth bell I took the pen to enter it, and what I wrote stands struck and I will not read it out again. At the eleventh bell I went home. I did not begin the laughing, and the entry is mine all the same.

**the 27th of June.** Temperature seventy-four. Watered morning and evening. The reader will excuse a gap in the weather from the twentieth, the keeper having been unwell. Tunnock kept the fires and Miss Tarry the counts, and the counts are continuous, which is the one thing that is.

**the 4th of July.** Visitors, sixteen. Mr. Rusk read the schedule at the eighth bell and did not depart from it. Miss Tarry asked me whether a count may be taken by a person who is laughing, and I said it may not, and she said she has not laughed since the 2nd of May, and I have no reason to doubt her.

**the 29th of September.** The season is closed. Row C stands at one and Row F at nothing, and the keeper has entered the figures as they are and has not summed them. The seedling in Row C is potted and under the north bench, watered and not spoken to. Miss Tarry goes back to her lectures on Monday and has asked for the book to be kept for her.

## Apparatus

*Under-Librarian's stamp, in violet:* Greenhouses are not reading rooms. Water is not to be carried through the north corridor, the floor there being unsound at the eleventh flag, and a day-book is not to be read at the counter between the eighth bell and the ninth.

*Conservator's note, 1504:* The strike at the twentieth of June was made with a blunter instrument than the text, and has bruised the paper without cutting it. The line is legible; the word *laughing* is the least legible of the fourteen words struck, the pen having halted in it.

*Pencil note, in the keeper's own hand, later:* The season was a fair one and the house did well by it. On Row F I have nothing to add, and I did not write that line about the laughing, or if I did I was not myself at the time. The trial will want another season, and I have said so. Miss Tarry married the Conservator's brother and is now Mrs. Cray of the Library; she counts twice and makes nought, and I have never asked her what she saw on the twentieth.
